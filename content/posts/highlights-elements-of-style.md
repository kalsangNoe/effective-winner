## TL;DR
A 1935 grammar/rhetoric pamphlet by Cornell professor William Strunk Jr., revived and expanded by his former student E. B. White in 1957 (and updated in later editions), organized into 11 elementary usage rules, 11 composition principles, a chapter of manuscript-form conventions, an alphabetical glossary of ~100 commonly misused words, and White's own closing chapter of 21 "reminders" on style as an expression of self. Its central, repeated thesis: "Omit needless words" — write with active voice, positive statements, concrete language, and parallel structure, then trust your ear where rules run out.

## Key takeaways
1. **Every sentence should earn its words.** Rule 17 ("Omit needless words") is the book's spine — a sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, "for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."
2. **Prefer the active, the positive, and the concrete** over the passive, the negative, and the abstract (Rules 14–16) — these three moves consistently make prose shorter, clearer, and more vigorous.
3. **Punctuation has load-bearing logic, not just decoration** — comma splices (Rule 5), dangling participles (Rule 11), and misplaced modifiers (Rule 20) aren't stylistic nitpicks; they create real ambiguity, illustrated with memorably bad real-world examples (the "eighteen men frozen in a stainless steel tank" sentence).
4. **Style cannot be reduced to rules.** White's Chapter V argues style is inseparable from the writer's identity (Faulkner vs. Hemingway, Frost vs. Whitman) and offers "reminders," not commands — chief among them: place yourself in the background, avoid qualifiers ("very," "rather," "little," "pretty"), and when in doubt, trust your ear.
5. **The misused-words glossary (Ch. IV) is the book's most practically reusable section** — a ~100-entry lookup table (hopefully, unique, that/which, comprise, disinterested, finalize, etc.) worth returning to as a live style-check reference rather than reading once.

## Chapter notes

### Foreword & Introduction
Roger Angell's foreword and E. B. White's 1979 introduction tell the book's origin: Strunk's self-published 1918 Cornell coursebook ("the little book"), White's 1919 encounter with it as a student, and his 1957 Macmillan-commissioned revision that made it a classic. Strunk taught by repeating rules three times aloud ("Omit needless words! Omit needless words! Omit needless words!").

Key quote: "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts."

### Chapter I: Elementary Rules of Usage (Rules 1–11)
Punctuation and grammar mechanics: possessive 's (Rule 1), serial comma (Rule 2), parenthetic/restrictive vs. nonrestrictive commas (Rule 3), comma before a conjunction joining independent clauses (Rule 4), no comma splices — use a semicolon (Rule 5), no sentence fragments (Rule 6), colon usage (Rule 7), dash usage (Rule 8), subject-verb number agreement (Rule 9), pronoun case (Rule 10), and no dangling participial phrases (Rule 11, with the classic bad example: *"Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap"*).

### Chapter II: Elementary Principles of Composition (Rules 12–22)
Structural principles: design first (12), one topic per paragraph (13), active voice (14), positive statements (15), concrete/specific language (16, featuring Orwell's deliberately deadened rewrite of Ecclesiastes 9:11 as a teaching example), omit needless words (17), avoid strings of loose sentences (18), parallel construction for coordinate ideas (19, exemplified by the Beatitudes), keep related words together (20, featuring the "eighteen men frozen in a stainless steel tank" ambiguous-modifier example), one tense in summaries (21), and place emphatic words at the sentence's end (22).

### Chapter III: A Few Matters of Form
Manuscript/typographic conventions in alphabetical order: colloquialisms (don't quote-mark them), exclamation points (reserve for true exclamations), headings, hyphens (the *Chattanooga News-Free Press* cautionary anecdote), margins, numerals, parentheses, quotation punctuation (comma inside closing quotes), citation format (*Othello* II.iii.264–267 instead of spelling out act/scene/line), syllabication, and title formatting.

### Chapter IV: Words and Expressions Commonly Misused
An alphabetical glossary from *Aggravate* to *Would* (~100 entries) — see the dedicated chunk notes file for the full compressed reference list. Standout entries: *hopefully* ("not merely wrong, it is silly"), *unique* (absolute, no degrees), *that vs. which* (restrictive vs. nonrestrictive), *finalize*/*-ize* coinages, *comprise*, *disinterested* vs. *uninterested*, *imply* vs. *infer*, singular *they* with distributive antecedents (offers four alternative strategies).

### Chapter V: An Approach to Style (With a List of Reminders)
White's own chapter, 21 numbered reminders on style as inseparable from identity: place yourself in the background (1), write naturally but know all writing starts as imitation (2), work from a design (3), write with nouns and verbs not adjectives/adverbs (4), revise and rewrite (5), don't overwrite (6) or overstate (7), avoid qualifiers — "leeches that infest the pond of prose" (8), don't affect breeziness (9), use orthodox spelling (10), don't over-explain dialogue (11), don't force awkward -ly adverbs (12), make speakers identifiable (13), avoid fancy words but trust your ear on exceptions like Lincoln's "Four score and seven years ago" (14), use dialect only with a good ear (15), be clear — "Clarity, clarity, clarity" (16), don't inject unsolicited opinion (17), use figures of speech sparingly (18), don't sacrifice clarity for brevity — spell out acronyms on first use (19), avoid foreign languages (20), and prefer the standard to the offbeat, wary of business jargon and advertising-speak (21). Closes: "Writing is an act of faith, not a trick of grammar."

### Afterword & Glossary
Charles Osgood's afterword frames Strunk and White as collaborators across a 38-year gap. Robert DiYanni's appended glossary defines ~50 grammar terms (gerund, periodic sentence, restrictive/nonrestrictive, appositive, etc.) used without definition earlier in the book — useful as a standing reference.
