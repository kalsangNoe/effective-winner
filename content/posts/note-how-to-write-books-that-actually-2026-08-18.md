## TL;DR
David Baldacci — 53 novels, seven children's books, ~200 million copies sold — walks David Perell through his craft and business philosophy. On craft: he writes without outlines, engineers a "big pop" early misdirection to seize control from the reader, gives every character unrelated "baggage," treats dialogue as something to be cut down rather than expanded, and slows down big moments instead of rushing them. On business: as a former lawyer, he insists writers must wear both a creative and a business hat, and he personally renegotiated his publishing deals from a royalty model to a profit-sharing "partnership" model because he believed the standard split was unfair. Throughout, he keeps returning to one idea: write only what genuinely excites you, because readers can tell when you don't.

## Key ideas

**Craft**
- **The "big pop":** Open a scene with real ambiguity, let the reader form a confident (wrong) assumption, then invalidate it fast — e.g., a book that seems to link two murders in one house, until it's revealed they're unrelated killers with different motives. This "puts me, the writer, back in control," because once fooled, the reader stops trying to predict the plot and just goes along for the ride.
- **No outlines, ever:** He tried outlining and always abandoned it — "why did I waste three weeks writing this crap?" He compares outlining to Tom Brady in practice versus a real game: "he's not at his sharpest because he doesn't have to be." Writing "in the trenches," where decisions actually matter to the characters in the moment, sharpens his instincts. He writes strictly linearly, page one to the end, then does the real structural work — moving and cutting scenes — in editing.
- **Give every character baggage unrelated to the plot at hand:** Sean King and Michelle Maxwell are disgraced ex-Secret Service agents; Amos Decker has brain trauma and hypermnesia after dying on the football field; Atlee Pine's twin sister was kidnapped when she was six. In a series, the plot of each book is secondary to the slow unpeeling of that emotional backstory across the whole series.
- **Twists aren't the whole job:** Misdirection matters, but "you can't make it all about the twist." He wants readers to leave thinking as much about how a character changed — broken, healed, or somewhere between — as about the reveal. Any extreme action a character takes needs motivation to match its magnitude, or readers reject it as implausible.
- **Cut dialogue relentlessly:** "Long dialogue... is lazy writing" — it's outsourcing to speech what should be done through action or interior monologue. His model is the Gettysburg Address (277 words, remembered) versus the two-hour speech before it (forgotten, by someone nobody recalls). On a film shoot after Superstorm Sandy wrecked his schedule, he cut a planned scene down to one line and let an Academy Award-winning actress's face do the rest: "if you've built up that character precisely... one line of dialogue can do the work of 10." He says he now aims to write in 10 words what he used to write in 100.
- **Every scene needs (at least) two purposes:** Borrowed from screenwriting, where producers demanded it because scenes cost money to shoot. In novels he applies it as: if a scene or even a line doesn't advance plot, flesh out character, or deliver needed information, cut it — including hard-won research. "Research is important... you have to leave 99% of it out. Otherwise you're writing a textbook."
- **The "pen ending":** When nearing a book's end without a clear finale, he writes a placeholder ("pen ending") and tells his publisher it may change, then deliberately steps away — a walk, chopping wood, working on another book — to let his subconscious solve it. "I solved more plot problems in pen endings in the shower than any other place."
- **Slow down the big moments:** A novel has only a handful of true "go for it" setups; don't rush them. His touchstone is the death scene in *Unforgiven* — Eastwood watches a man die slowly over several minutes instead of a quick kill — and Hitchcock's line, "There was no terror in the bang, only in the anticipation of it." Speeding up action removes consequence; slowing it down is how it becomes memorable, the way people recall car crashes in slow motion.
- **Character over plot:** He spends his time with characters because he, as the writer (not just the reader), needs to stay interested. He wants characters "away from perfection" — vulnerable, fragile, uncertain — and pushes them "through the wall" rather than just against it, because real character shows up in how someone responds to being knocked flat, not in good times.
- **Write only what excites you:** "If I am fascinated by what I'm writing about, everyone else will be fascinated too... if I'm not interested in it, I don't care how well I write it, nobody else is going to be interested either." He now lets ideas percolate rather than rushing to write, having learned that premature starts lead to dead ends.
- **Research to saturation, then cut almost all of it:** For a 1958 Los Angeles-set Archer novel he bought a period gas-station map on Etsy; for 1953 Las Vegas he found a 25-minute amateur video of a convertible drive down the Strip and went through it frame by frame. The goal is to "suspend disbelief" with a few well-chosen details (like a mechanical cowboy sign that gave people heart attacks) — but more than three or four sentences of scene-setting description gets the page flipped.
- **On children's books:** "The only difference between adult fiction and writing for younger audiences is word choice." Kids are sophisticated and can tell when they're being written down to.

**Business**
- **"No one should make more off of the book than the person who wrote it."** After ten years of building his own fan base and doing his own publicity, he renegotiated away from standard royalties (roughly 15% of retail, which nets an author ~30% return while the publisher keeps ~70% after wholesale/print/distribution costs) toward a profit-sharing "partnership" — publisher gets a fixed ~30% return on their investment, he gets the rest.
- **Writers must wear two hats:** "You have to be the content creator and you have to be the business person." Writers who ignore the business side get blindsided later — declining sales, shrinking marketing spend, disappearing from stores — because they never learned to ask why.
- **Read contracts and ask hard questions:** As a lawyer he stresses knowing deal structure (single-book vs. multi-book, royalty vs. profit-share, seasonal release timing) as well as or better than your own agent.
- **Read the masters, not writing-craft books:** He deliberately avoids how-to-write books, preferring to read great novels for pleasure, then reverse-engineer afterward why they worked. Rereading *To Kill a Mockingbird* as a kid versus in college revealed entirely different layers — "the best books grow with you." Growing up under segregation in Richmond, Virginia, books were his substitute for Mark Twain's "travel is fatal to prejudice": "books are like body armor against bigotry."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The way you do that is to open up a scene that has a lot of ambiguity and multiple directions it could go in and then just blow them out of the water by picking probably the least likely one." — Baldacci, on the "big pop"
- "Doing an outline is like Tom Brady in practice. He's going through the motions. Nobody's going to hurt him on the field." — Baldacci
- "I hate lengthy dialogue... it's lazy writing because what you're doing is trying to put into words what you should be able to do in other elements of storytelling." — Baldacci
- "If the word is imprinted on that page in one of my books, it's there for a reason. And if you choose to skip over it, then you do so at your own peril." — Baldacci
- "No one should make more off of the book than the person who wrote it. No one." — Baldacci
- "When you write action, the best action is when you slow it down. Not when you speed it up. When you speed it up, people miss the impact." — Baldacci
- "Bombs can eliminate mountains, but books can move them. And I'd rather move mountains than eliminate them." — Baldacci
- "The best books grow with you because the maturity in the books is always there, but it's not in you yet." — Baldacci

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I over-explaining through dialogue or narration what a single well-chosen detail or action could carry instead?
- Do I have a "big pop" moment early on — something that seizes control of the reader's expectations — or am I letting the reader stay ahead of me?
- Am I giving secondary characters even a little unrelated "baggage," or are they functioning as pure plot tools?
- Which of my projects am I actually excited about right now, versus grinding through because I already started it?
- Is there a place in my work where speeding through a big moment is costing it impact — where slowing down would make it land harder?
