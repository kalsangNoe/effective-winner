## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Sahil Bloom about the mechanics behind his writing and his newsletter/Twitter growth. Sahil's core argument: forced constraints (character limits, deadlines, brevity) are what generate creativity, not free-flowing time — and the best writing sits on a spectrum between "growth-hacky" and "soulful," where his own goal is maximizing reach without sacrificing authenticity. He also walks through the operational side — how he 10x'd newsletter growth by hiring a "chief of staff" (Shane) to own the business/ops layer so he could stay in the creative layer — and his personal creative process: an analog notebook, writing-from-conversation to test ideas before publishing, and deliberately not checking engagement metrics for 24 hours to protect the soul of the work.

## Key ideas

**Origins**
- **Selfish writing compounds.** Sahil ran a "what I've been reading" newsletter by hand-typing 10 friends' emails into MailChimp starting ~2014, growing it to ~2,000 people over 5+ years purely as a labor of love, long before he knew "newsletter" or "Creator economy" were terms.
- **The OG content creators didn't know they were building an audience.** Warren Buffett's shareholder letters (since 1960), Howard Marks's memos, and Jim Grant's Interest Rate Observer all started as private clarifying-thinking exercises that only later became massive distribution engines.

**Forced constraints breed creativity**
- **Constraint, not inspiration, is the real creative engine.** Sahil wrote investment memos to himself in private equity because PowerPoint didn't force clarity; Twitter's character limit did the same thing later — "if you weren't clear on your thinking around it, you couldn't do it."
- **The "creative cliff illusion":** people expect their best ideas at the start of a session and a decline after, but real data shows creativity actually ramps up after an initial struggle — "trudging through the mud" produces the unlock, not fresh energy.
- **Dr. Seuss as proof of constraint-as-creativity:** he bet his publisher he could write a bestseller using only 50 distinct words, and wrote *Green Eggs and Ham* (49 words, 10M+ copies sold).

**Where to sit on the growth-vs-soul spectrum**
- **Every piece of writing is "a call for the kind of person you want to bring into your life."** Growth-hacky content ("10 TED Talks that'll change your life") reaches many but builds no real connection; soulful writing (Maria Popova, Tim Urban) builds deep trust but caps your publishing cadence. Sahil's ambition — impact a billion people over 50 years — forces him to find a middle position rather than pick a pole.
- **Certainty kills good writing.** "The problem with certainty is that insistence on certainty leads to consensus thinking, and so much of writing is [about] breaking away from the consensus." He cites Thales of Miletus, who proved his worth by cornering the olive-press market rather than through philosophy alone — inputs (thinking) aren't rewarded; outputs are.
- **95% of great non-fiction isn't new — it's an old idea repackaged 10% better.** James Clear didn't discover habits; he packaged known ideas into "a slightly more beautiful package." True novelty (Tim Ferriss's *4-Hour Work Week*) is rare.
- **His stated mission in one sentence:** to help people "ask better questions about their own life" — because he believes it's impossible to hand anyone answers, only better questions.

**Process**
- **He tracks ideas in a paper Moleskine dot-grid notebook, not Notion/Rome/ChatGPT** — deliberately analog, because thinking about *how* to write (like the centipede asked which leg moves first) freezes intuitive flow.
- **"Writing from conversation":** he only considers a piece ready to publish once he's tested the idea on ~5 people in a row and watched their eyes light up — a real anecdote is the *what the hell is going on* essay, tested at dinner with a friend until it landed five times running.
- **24-hour rule for new ideas:** any idea he writes down must be talked through with someone within 24 hours, or he loses it — this is also how he "remembers everything" despite not considering himself naturally good at memory.
- **He doesn't check post/email engagement for 24 hours (posts) or a week (newsletter opens)**, because metric-chasing is "inversely correlated to the soul" in the work.

**Scaling the newsletter (the ops story)**
- **Two inflection points:** meeting David Perell (mid-2020, prompted him to take email seriously) and hiring a "chief of staff," Shane, in late 2022 to own all backend ops — lead magnets, swaps/collaborations, paid ads, SparkLoop — freeing Sahil from an ~80/20 split where he was spending most time on things he hated.
- **Concrete result:** growth went from ~5–10K/month to ~50K/month (a "true 10x") by January 2023, largely from lead magnets and systematized collaborations/swaps.
- **The newsletter runs at break-even by design** — all sponsor revenue is reinvested into growth rather than taken as profit, since Sahil doesn't monetize via brand deals or courses; other businesses fund his life.
- **This system got productized** into a company called Paperboy (with ConvertKit's Nathan Barry), turning Shane's playbook into "newsletter ops and growth as a service" for other creators.

**Twitter/distribution timeline**
- Sahil's first post was May 12, 2020 with ~500 followers; he was early to writing multi-tweet threads (a format that barely existed yet) and, inspired by Ben Thompson's and David Senra's habit of cross-referencing past work, built a "Twitter-native blog" by linking new threads back to old ones.
- He argues Elon-era Twitter, despite being "harder to grow fast" now, is healthier because it discourages vanity-metric chasing — citing Kevin Kelly's "1,000 true fans" as the real bar, not follower counts.

**Book-writing (a different animal)**
- **The dopamine-cycle problem:** every other format he'd written gave feedback within ~2 days; a book gives none for ~2 years, which he calls the single biggest unadvertised challenge of writing long-form.
- He missed his original book deadline (signed Sept 30, 2022, due Sept 30, 2023) after writing only ~5,000 of 75,000 words in nine months, then forced two non-negotiable hours every morning from July 1 to the deadline — deliberately re-imposing Parkinson's-law constraint on himself.
- **The "shiny dime":** his term (via Write of Passage) for the single distilled essence line a piece orbits around — sometimes found before writing, more often only after "crawling through the mud," as with his line "never let the quest for more distract you from the beauty of enough."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where am I on the growth-hacky vs. soulful spectrum right now, and is that actually a deliberate choice or just where inertia left me?
- What's my equivalent of Sahil's "shiny dime" test — do I know the one-sentence essence of a piece before I start writing it, or only after?
- Could I run my own version of "writing from conversation" — testing an idea out loud on a few people before committing it to a page — instead of editing alone?
- What's the ops/creative split in my own work, and is there a "Shane" role I'm avoiding delegating that's eating into the 20% I actually enjoy?
- Am I checking feedback (views, replies, opens) too early in a way that's quietly reshaping what I choose to write next?
