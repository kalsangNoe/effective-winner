# The role of deliberate practice in expert performance—revisiting Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) by Ericsson, Macnamara, Maitra, et al.

**Source:** Royal Society Open Science, Volume 6, Issue 8, Article 190327, 2019

## TL;DR
This 2019 paper defends and elaborates the original 1993 deliberate practice theory against mounting criticism, particularly from Macnamara and Hambrick's 2014 meta-analysis. The authors argue that critics have misapplied or misunderstood the concept of deliberate practice, and provide additional theoretical and empirical support for the core claim that deliberate practice is the primary mechanism driving expert performance acquisition across domains.

## Key takeaways

1. **Defense Against Critics**: The paper directly responds to the Macnamara & Hambrick (2014) meta-analysis criticism, arguing that critics often confuse "practice" more broadly with "deliberate practice" specifically. The authors contend that deliberate practice remains a powerful predictor when properly measured according to the original definition.

2. **Clarification of Deliberate Practice Definition**: The authors reaffirm that deliberate practice must meet specific criteria: structure designed to improve performance, immediate feedback, high concentration/effort, and typically occurs under expert guidance. Much practice data in prior studies conflated this with general practice time.

3. **Innate Differences Acknowledged But Secondary**: While acknowledging individual differences in physical capacity (height, hand size, etc.) exist and vary by domain, the paper maintains that within the range of normal human variation, deliberate practice remains the dominant predictor of who achieves expertise versus who does not.

4. **Domain Specificity**: The authors note that the role of deliberate practice varies by domain. In domains with clearer performance metrics and feedback (chess, music performance, sports), deliberate practice explains higher variance; in domains with more ambiguous criteria (professions, education), other factors play larger roles.

5. **Longitudinal Evidence Over Cross-sectional**: The paper emphasizes that longitudinal studies tracking practice and performance over time provide stronger evidence than cross-sectional snapshots. Many criticisms rely on the latter type of data.

6. **Methodological Rigor**: The replication studies and recent research using more rigorous measurement of deliberate practice tend to support the original theory. The authors argue that measurement validity is critical—poor operationalization of deliberate practice confounds interpretations.

7. **Champion Development Requires Years**: Evidence across domains shows that reaching elite status requires typically 8-10 years of sustained deliberate practice, consistent with the original framework. The pathway is longer and more structured than chance or talent-only accounts would predict.

8. **Coaching and Structures Amplify Practice Effects**: Elite performance emerges from structured training environments with expert coaching. Deliberate practice doesn't occur in a vacuum; it's embedded in "domains" with established techniques and feedback mechanisms that guide improvement.

9. **Practical Implications**: The paper argues the theory has practical value: it suggests expertise is achievable through structured effort, making it relevant to education, training, and professional development—optimistic compared to talent-only theories.
