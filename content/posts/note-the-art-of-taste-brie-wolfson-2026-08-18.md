## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Brie Wolfson (former Stripe writer/editor, Stripe Press co-creator) about writing culture, editing, and taste. Her central argument: writing is a forcing function for clear thinking, and internal writing culture (at Stripe especially) compounds because it creates an artifact that others can fork, argue with, and build cohesion around. The back half is about "taste" — her viral essay on the topic — and how taste is trained the same way editing is trained: by building a personal "like/dislike meter," staying honest about what you actually love (versus what you're supposed to love), narrowing scope, and doing the reps of articulating *why* something works. She also traces craft lessons from magazine editing (The New Yorker's Harold Ross, Vanity Fair's Tina Brown) and argues good writing means writing to one specific, real person rather than an abstract audience.

## Key ideas

**Internal writing at Stripe**
- **Writing forces clear thinking.** Bad writing is "not clear thinking" — you get clear in your thinking by getting clear on the page; this scales at org level because it produces an artifact people can react to instead of a meeting that evaporates.
- **Two kinds of internal writing existed at Stripe:** work-advancing writing (docs about the actual project) and a "curation layer on top" — friction logs, opinion pieces, thinking-out-loud posts that surfaced what the org was actually grappling with.
- **Stripe's open email policy** ("every email is sort of [open]") built cohesion because people got "unfettered access" to leadership's thinking, more than core teams alone could.
- **Doing vs. thinking is a real trade-off.** You can debate ideas endlessly or ship and let "code settle arguments" — sometimes better to just get moving and see where you land.

**Stripe Press**
- Founded almost by accident: John (Collison) was so taken with an early draft of *The High Growth Handbook* sitting on his desk that he wanted to send a finished book to every Stripe customer — that one project snowballed into a full publishing arm.
- The books are physically engineered for taste: **special "impregnated stock" paper** makes the cover color run all the way through so scuffs and wear don't show — because these books get carried around, thrown in backpacks, and are meant to look better worn, not pristine. "Reality has a surprising amount of detail" — even paper choice.

**Editing craft**
- **Cardinal writing flaw: trying to write about too much.** The narrower the scope, the sharper the piece — you don't need to capture the whole subject, just the most interesting sliver of it (her example: write only about the moment a hotel guest checks in, not the whole hotel experience).
- **Remember what the reader doesn't know.** As the author you know everything already, so it's hard to tell what needs foreshadowing, what's boring repetition to you but new to the reader.
- **Read your writing out loud / record yourself reading it.** If it "doesn't sound like you" when spoken, something's wrong — she keeps a "Darlings" doc for cut lines that were good writing but didn't fit.
- **Editing is like watching game film.** Athletes get better by watching tape with a coach who names what's happening in real time; writers need the same — someone to say "that's the swivel" the way a coach would.
- **Curmudgeons vs. critics.** Curmudgeons dismiss everything and offer no bar to aim for; real critics discern *why* something is good and can point toward it — that's the useful kind of harsh feedback.
- **Craft the "last 20%."** Citing the 80/20 rule and Steve Jobs, she argues most writers/orgs won't fund the final push that makes work exceptional — leaders have to explicitly authorize the extra time (a whole extra week, an extra person) or the work stays merely good.

**Magazines as a model**
- The New Yorker's Harold Ross was a "maniacal editor" obsessed with tone and style, not credentials — he ran the "Weekly Word Meeting" where writers pitched and got brutally trimmed.
- New Yorker profiles work via a "chain of obsession": the writer must be obsessed with the subject, and that obsession — not full biographical fidelity — is what selects which anecdotes make the cut.
- Vanity Fair's Tina Brown pioneered "high-low" — mixing lowbrow subjects (Britney Spears) with highbrow ones deliberately, because "the extreme is actually way more interesting" than staying safely in the middle.
- Great magazine covers/packaging matter as much as content: Harold Ross reportedly finalized the cover before the content was locked.

**Writing to one person**
- Modern writing often suffers from "view from nowhere" — writing to an abstract audience instead of a real person. Her friend's absurdly specific hospitality questionnaire (asking exactly how you like your pillows, drinks, mornings) is offered as a model of real audience-awareness.
- Old epistolary forms (Rilke's *Letters to a Young Poet*) stayed intimate because they were addressed to one real person — she argues the internet's "hit publish" reflex has eroded that filter between idea and audience.

**Taste**
- Her essay "Notes on Taste" was sparked by a founder claiming "taste" as a hiring criterion without being able to define it — she structured the essay explicitly as a list of "different things I believe about taste," modeled on Susan Sontag's "Notes on Camp."
- **Cultivating taste = building a personal "like/dislike meter"** and trusting it — being honest about what you actually love versus what you're "supposed to" love or hate, rather than performing sophistication.
- **Taste is vertical, not global.** She warns against trying to have refined taste in everything (she jokes she'd be "a monster" if she scored her coffee the way she does fashion) — pick a few lanes and go deep.
- **Editing and taste-cultivation are the same muscle.** Both require noticing "something's off," then doing the harder work of articulating *why*, not settling for "it's just a Spidey sense" — vague intuition has to graduate into language.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I trying to cover too much ground instead of narrowing to the sharpest sliver of the topic?
- Do I have a real "like/dislike meter" for the domains I write about, or am I echoing what I'm supposed to admire?
- Who is the one specific, real reader I'm actually writing to — and does my draft change if I name them?
- What's my equivalent of reading a draft aloud or recording myself — a step that surfaces when something "doesn't sound like me"?
- Am I funding the "last 20%" on my own work, or shipping as soon as it's merely good enough?
