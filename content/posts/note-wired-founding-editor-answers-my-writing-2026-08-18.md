## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Kevin Kelly — co-founder of Wired, former editor/publisher of the Whole Earth Catalog, and author of *Excellent Advice for Living* — about the craft and psychology of writing. Kelly describes himself as a "reluctant writer" who loves having written more than writing itself, and lays out his editorial philosophy ("amaze me," "don't be the best, be the only," structure over phrasing) alongside broader ideas he's known for: scenius (collective genius), 1,000 True Fans, and the moral duty to create and share. The conversation moves from the mechanics of his editing process to bigger themes — greatness as trade-off, marketing as part of the creative act, and why he keeps writing after fifty years.

## Key ideas
- **"Amaze me" was his editorial mandate at Wired.** Kelly told writers not to write down to a generic audience but to write to *him* specifically — someone who already knows the basics (DNA, etc.) and needs to be genuinely surprised: "Amaze me. Come amaze me. Tell me something that I'm gonna find surprising... Blow me away, fire me up."
- **He's a reluctant writer, not a natural one.** "I don't like writing. I'm a reluctant writer. I'm a born editor... I'm unhappy, I'm grumpy" while writing, but "I love having written." He's a slow writer, doesn't even type fast, yet keeps a steady editing rhythm — writing and editing the previous day's work in a back-and-forth loop rather than writing straight through.
- **Writing is how he discovers what he thinks, not how he communicates it.** He often doesn't know what he believes on a topic until he's forced a sentence out and it fails, then tries again — a painful, iterative process he compares to Picasso's bull lithographs, which only make sense as a *sequence* of simplifications. The idea is generated by the act of writing it, not before.
- **Two tests for honesty in a line of writing:** (1) Do I really believe this? and (2) Has this exact phrasing/idea been said before — could it have existed anywhere else? Writing that passes both feels distinct and "you're more likely to hit" real impact than by aiming for cleverness directly.
- **Editing is about structure, not phrasing.** The single most valuable thing an outside editor gives a writer is structural feedback — sequencing, pacing, where a piece jumps and loses the reader — not line edits. Good structure should feel invisible ("graceful, intuitive, that you don't notice it"); when a reader is confused rather than pleasantly surprised, it's usually a structural failure, not a sentence-level one. He cites John McPhee as someone who can do this for himself.
- **Scenius — collective genius of a scene.** Borrowed/expanded from Brian Eno: many "great" individual achievements (Xerox PARC, Yosemite in the '70s inventing modern climbing, Wired-era hacker culture) actually came from small, semi-permeable groups pushing and competing with each other, not lone genius. He sees the same dynamic today in YouTube niches, where creators iterate visibly on each other's videos in public.
- **1,000 True Fans and "don't be the best, be the only."** He describes the essay's origin as a hunch he wrote down before Kickstarter existed, later validated by platforms like Substack and Patreon. The deeper principle: aim to be so specific and undefinable ("there's no name for what it is that you're doing") that you have no real competition — that's a much higher bar than being "the best" at something with an existing name.
- **Deadlines, not more time, are what finish work.** "The work of creating is bottomless... you can always add more" — so external deadlines (a habit from Stewart Brand) are what force a stopping point. Once past a deadline he moves on quickly: "so many books are abandoned as far as I can tell" once the deadline passes, in the sense that authors stop caring.
- **Marketing is part of the creative act, not separate from it.** He came to believe roughly half the total effort of a book is in "communicating it" after it's written — packaging, structure, distribution — because publishers (Random House, Viking, etc.) no longer have direct relationships with readers/buyers. He's done things like offering to give a free talk to any office that bought 25 copies, and running small self-scheduled Zoom/podcast sessions with reader groups instead of a traditional press tour.
- **A duty to create and share.** Kelly frames withholding your genius as itself a loss — for you and for the world — citing a thought experiment: if Mozart or Hitchcock had been born before the technology existed to give their genius an outlet, "what a loss to us and to him." He feels "a moral obligation to enable everybody" to have the tools to express what's in them.
- **Greatness requires extremity, and extremity has a cost.** People he considers truly "great" are extreme along whatever axis makes them great, and that comes at the expense of other dimensions of life — family, relationships, balance. He explicitly declines the label for himself: "I'm not great... I'm much more kind of like a gadfly," someone broad rather than extreme in one direction.
- **All ideas are combinatorial, not solo.** He rejects the idea of the lone original genius, pointing to simultaneous invention (Alexander Graham Bell and a rival filing telephone patents hours apart) as evidence that ideas are "networks," and that copyright/IP protection should be brief — just enough incentive to create — before returning to the commons.
- **On fame and being recognized.** He distinguishes being "well known" (useful, opens doors, worth cultivating) from true fame/celebrity ("a burden... like having a billion dollars you can't spend"), and describes consciously managing which side of that line he stays on.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I don't like writing. I'm a reluctant writer. I'm a born editor. I love having written."
- "Amaze me. That's my first principle... Tell me something that I'm gonna find surprising... Blow me away, fire me up. That's what good writing is."
- "Don't aim to be the best, be the only."
- "The work of creating is bottomless. The amount of work, energy... is unlimited. You can always add more. So you need a deadline."
- "That's what we're doing when we're writing. We're giving... you have a duty to share your art, otherwise you're going to express your genius and that's [a loss]."
- "I question almost everything I'm writing... Do I really believe this? ...Should not have existed anywhere else."
- "I'm not great... I'm much more kind of like a gadfly."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I aiming to be "the best" at something that already has a name, instead of finding the undefinable niche where I'd have no competition?
- Am I discovering what I think through the act of writing, or am I only transcribing conclusions I've already reached — and would forcing myself to write before I feel ready surface better ideas?
- Do I have a real deadline mechanism, or am I letting pieces stay "bottomless" and never actually finish?
- When I get editing feedback, is it about structure/sequencing (the thing Kelly says actually matters) or just phrasing — and am I asking for the right kind of feedback?
- What's my own "amaze me" reader — a specific, smart, already-informed person I'm writing to, rather than a vague general audience?
