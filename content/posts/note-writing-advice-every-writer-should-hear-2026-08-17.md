## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Anne Lamott (*Bird by Bird*) about her new book with husband Neal Allen, *Good Writing*. Her core argument: writing isn't about waiting for inspiration or seeing the whole road — you drive by headlights, seeing only as far as the next scene, and that's enough. Writer's block isn't a real condition; it's emptiness (not enough attention paid to life) or an inner critic that needs to be named and sent to the library. Craft advice is concrete and repeatable: write a terrible first draft, then cut and strengthen it across three passes (child's draft, adult draft, dental draft), trust "said," use strong verbs, kill anything that sounds literary, and read dialogue aloud to catch what's fake.

## Key ideas
- **Writing is driving in fog, not seeing the whole road.** Lamott's opening image: driving to Monterey in thick fog, following only the taillights ahead. She borrows E.L. Doctorow's line that writing is like driving at night with headlights — you can only see a little ahead, but you can make the whole journey that way, going from point A to point B and letting B reveal what's next.
- **Writer's block isn't real — it's emptiness or a locked door with a deeper problem behind it.** When novelist Isabel Allende told Lamott she had writer's block, Lamott said: "I don't think you're blocked. I think you're really empty... all the sand has escaped from the burlap sack." The fix isn't accumulating more experiences (that adds pressure) — it's paying better attention. A priest friend told her: "The point is not to try harder. It's to resist less."
- **The "rag bag guy."** There's a figure at the center of every writer who collects scraps — bits of fabric, thread, tinsel — from daily attention, and eventually hands them over so you can assemble the quilt of an essay, novel, or memoir.
- **Name your inner critic and send it to the library.** Neal Allen taught her to personify the critic, ask "Who hired you? Why did I hire you?" (usually: fear of looking bad, dating back to childhood), and tell it, "I'll come get you if I need an ethical consultation... you go to the library and read."
- **Three drafts, from *Bird by Bird*.** The child's draft ("firm but friendly," like editing a two-year-old — keep it, but cut it here); the adult draft (fix, cut, find the stronger word); the dental draft (go tooth by tooth, floss, wiggle — some teeth are fine, some need work).
- **Craft rules from *Good Writing* (36 rules, co-written with Neal Allen).** Use strong verbs ("he stumbled/staggered/army-crawled down the hall" instead of "walked"); take out the boring stuff; if it sounds literary, cut it; never substitute for "said" (no "chuckled," "enthused," "proclaimed"); use nickel words, not 25-cent words — "a confused reader is an antagonistic reader" (quoting Shirley Jackson).
- **The thesaurus's real use is the middle category of words.** Not words you don't know (fancy, useless) but words you know and never use — surfacing "wept" instead of "crying" because it was already there, just buried.
- **Dialogue: write a ton, then cut most of it, and read it aloud.** People don't talk in clipped, witty, perfect sentences; you should identify a speaker by rhythm and vocabulary, not adverb-heavy dialogue tags.
- **Revenge is legitimate fuel.** Against her friend Ethan Canin's rule to never write out of revenge, Lamott tells students "the kids who teased you in seventh grade should eat it" — you just have to disguise identifying details to avoid liability.
- **The best writing prompt: "There was a tree."** Borrowed from her son's writing collective, awritingroom.com. A single image — a childhood tree, a tree you fell from, one where you buried a grandfather's ashes — can "unleash a whole book." Related prompts from Abigail Thomas: list 10 things you've forgotten, then 10 things you remember, then something you wish you hadn't said.
- **Two hacks for being stuck.** Skip ahead past the blocked scene to a later point where the outcome has already shifted, then work backward; or introduce a new character to "shake the snow globe."
- **The ABDCE structure**, taught to her by New Yorker writer Alice Adams: Action (something happens early), Background (why we're here), Development, Climax (something dies — literally or an illusion), Ending (readers exit the "pond" together).
- **Edit with a sandwich, and worship good editors.** She and Neal edit each other by leading with what works before naming what's broken — "trust good editors... they're saving you from yourself, from your blind spots."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The point is not to try harder. It's to resist less." — Anne Lamott, quoting her late priest friend Terry Ritchie
- "Perfectionism is the voice of the oppressor, the enemy of the people." — Anne Lamott, from *Bird by Bird* (quoted by David Perell)
- "A confused reader is an antagonistic reader." — Shirley Jackson, quoted by Lamott
- "Tell me a story. Make me care." — screenwriter Robin Swicord's friend Randy Mayem Singer, quoted by Lamott
- "If you've survived your childhood, you have enough to write about for the rest of your life." — Flannery O'Connor, quoted by Lamott
- "You are creating a happy or sad movie depending on when you decide to end it." — Orson Welles, quoted by Lamott
- "Write about the space between the notes." — Miles Davis, quoted by Lamott
- "Everything true and beautiful can be discovered on any 10-minute walk." — Neal Allen, quoted by Lamott

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's my equivalent of the "rag bag guy" — am I actually collecting fragments day to day, or waiting for a finished idea to arrive?
- If I named my own inner critic and asked "who hired you, and why," what would the honest answer be?
- Where in my writing am I reaching for a "literary" word instead of the plain, nickel one that would actually land?
- Do I read my own dialogue (or any first-person writing) out loud before calling it done — and what does it reveal when I do?
- What's my version of "there was a tree" — a single concrete image I keep circling back to that could anchor a much longer piece?
