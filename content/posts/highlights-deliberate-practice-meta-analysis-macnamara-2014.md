# Deliberate Practice and Performance in Music, Games, Sports, Education, and Professions—A Meta-Analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick, & Oswald

**Source:** Psychological Science, Volume 25, Number 8, 2014, pp. 1608-1618

## TL;DR
This influential meta-analysis aggregates research across diverse domains to test how much of performance variance deliberate practice explains. It finds that deliberate practice accounts for only a fraction of performance differences—ranging from 26% in games to less than 1% in professions—challenging the strong claims that practice is the overwhelmingly dominant factor in expertise acquisition. The results suggest innate ability and other factors play larger roles than Ericsson's theory implies, especially outside music and games.

## Key takeaways

1. **Domain-Specific Effect Sizes**: The meta-analysis reveals substantial variation in deliberate practice's explanatory power by domain: 26% of variance explained in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education, and less than 1% in professions. This challenges universal claims about practice dominance.

2. **Professions Show Minimal Effects**: In professional domains (medical diagnosis, management performance, psychotherapy outcomes), deliberate practice explains negligible variance. This suggests the deliberate practice model may be limited to concrete, feedback-rich domains with clear performance metrics.

3. **Expertise Not Solely Practice-Driven**: The finding that deliberate practice explains, at best, 26% of variance means that 74% of performance differences in games (and much higher percentages in other domains) are attributable to other factors including innate ability, experience, education, and unexamined variables.

4. **Critique of Ericsson's Claims**: The authors argue that Ericsson and colleagues have overstated the importance of deliberate practice. While practice matters, it is neither necessary nor sufficient for achieving elite performance in most domains studied.

5. **Music Shows Strongest Effects**: Music performance represents the domain where deliberate practice has the strongest relationship with performance (21% explained variance), suggesting domain-specific mechanisms rather than universal principles.

6. **Methodological Heterogeneity**: Studies included in the meta-analysis varied considerably in how they measured deliberate practice and performance, and in their quality. High-quality studies sometimes showed weaker effects than lower-quality studies, suggesting potential methodological bias in the literature.

7. **Age and Performance Timing**: In sports and games, some of the unexplained variance may reflect physical advantages, developmental windows (critical periods for certain abilities), and innate differences in processing speed or pattern recognition that practice cannot fully overcome.

8. **Educational Implications**: The weak effects in education (4% explained) suggest that simply increasing deliberate practice time in schools is unlikely to dramatically narrow achievement gaps. Other factors (prior knowledge, socioeconomic resources, motivation) matter as much or more.

9. **Call for Nuance**: The paper concludes that deliberate practice is important but not a "master key" to expertise. Future research should investigate which other factors matter most in different domains and how they interact with practice.

10. **Reframes the Debate**: Rather than "Does practice matter?" the more productive question becomes "In which contexts, for which kinds of performance, and in combination with what other factors does deliberate practice predict expertise?" This shifts from universalism to domain-sensitive analysis.
