## TL;DR
Adrian Tchaikovsky (author of *Children of Time*, 60+ books) argues that world-building comes first and everything else — plot, characters, even fight scenes — should grow organically out of a coherent world rather than being imposed on it. He plans worlds obsessively but refuses to plan endings, letting the "momentum" of the story dictate how it resolves. He's skeptical of universal writing rules (the hero's journey, "show don't tell") because every writer works differently, and he treats scientific/historical plausibility as a discipline — a "left wall" you can't cross — that gives speculative fiction its emotional weight.

## Key ideas
- **World-building comes from tabletop RPGs.** Tchaikovsky builds worlds "robustly" the way a GM builds a world players might break — the world must extend far beyond what's actually shown on the page.
- **He plans plots chapter-by-chapter but never plans the ending.** He'll write "this happens, this happens, this happens" in a Word doc, but "the one thing I've never done is actually work out the very last... ending" — he lets the momentum of the book at the final confrontation tell him how it resolves.
- **The "stone in a pool" method for generating a world.** Start with one "what if" (e.g., "a species of spider evolves into the dominant civilization"), then follow the logical ripples outward — "if that's true, then this must be true" — until an entire coherent world emerges.
- **A good idea usually comes from combining two half-ideas.** *Children of Ruin* came from wanting to write about uplifted octopuses (only "half a book") plus a separate idea about an alien creature interfacing with human neurology — together they made a full novel.
- **The "left wall."** In science fiction, what current science says is possible is a wall you can expand outward from but never cross — unless you explicitly opt out (e.g., deciding to allow time travel). In fantasy, you build your own left wall by defining what magic can't do.
- **The "one big lie."** You get one convenient falsehood to make the plot work (in *Children of Time*, it's the timescale of the nanovirus-driven uplift — tens of thousands of years instead of hundreds of millions), but everything else must be as true and rigorous as possible to support it.
- **Magic needs a price, or the story loses tension** — "why isn't everyone just doing everything by magic?" He cites Michael Moorcock's *Dancers at the End of Time*, where near-omnipotent characters are simply bored because their magic has no cost.
- **He distrusts universal writing maxims.** "Every writer does it differently" — he's watched fellow authors recoil at his own methods (never planning an ending) just as he'd recoil at theirs. He specifically rejects the hero's journey as a mandatory template, comparing it to over-simplified statistical models that become "worthless" once reduced far enough.
- **Fight scenes work through research you mostly hide.** Years of stage fighting, LARP, and historical broadsword training taught him which details are physically accurate (stab an enemy in the armpit, not through armor) — but the craft is to "put as little of that as possible on the page," since dumping research kills pacing.
- **Cities force consequence in a way travelogue fantasy doesn't.** In a travelogue (the Frodo-to-Mordor structure), characters "leave their messes behind." In a city, residents have pre-existing relationships with everyone, so their choices have to be lived with — "much more complex and interesting," though more work to prep (he compares it to a conspiracy corkboard of who-knows-who).
- **The three-sided knowledge structure**: what the author knows, what the character knows, and what the reader knows can each diverge. In *Dogs of War*, the reader recognizes the narrator-dog Rex is committing war crimes long before Rex, who lacks the moral context, understands it himself.
- **Endings are the most important part of the book** because they're what readers are left with — a good ending must feel like "the very logical result of what's gone before, but also... a surprise."
- **He reads peers, not "the greats."** He finds contemporary science fiction more useful to study than 1960s classics, since living writers are already building on the same accumulated tradition he is.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The one thing I've never done is actually work out the very last, the very ending... letting the trajectory and momentum of the book to that point then tell you how it should end has worked really well for me."
- "You get to the point of feeling that you are chronicling."
- "Monsters that tell us about us are always going to be more interesting than just a monster."
- "In order to support your one big lie, everything else needs to be true."
- "Once you are reducing something that far, then you're left with a model that is completely worthless... it's very much that idea when you're trying to reduce writing to, 'Well, these are the ways you write a book.'"
- "You need to learn how to put as little of that as possible on the page. Because the temptation... is I am just going to vomit all this stuff on the page to show how incredibly intensive my research has been. And at that point, pacing goes out of the window."
- "The ending needs to be the bit where everything that you intend to tie up in the book turns out to have been inextricably leading to that ending... even though while it was doing that leading, that wasn't remotely obviously where it was going."
- "It's the equivalent of making a table where all the legs touch the ground and it doesn't wobble."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "vomiting research onto the page" instead of letting a handful of well-chosen details imply the depth behind them?
- Do I have a "left wall" for the nonfiction/opinion pieces I write — some constraint I refuse to violate that gives the rest of the piece credibility?
- Am I over-planning endings/conclusions in a way that forecloses a better, more organic resolution the piece could arrive at on its own?
- Which "writing maxims" have I adopted uncritically that I should stress-test by trying to break them on purpose?
- What's my equivalent of Tchaikovsky's "stone in a pool" — a single premise I could let ripple outward instead of planning an entire structure up front?
