## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Steph Smith (a16z podcast host, author of *Doing Content Right*) about how creators actually stand out online. Her core argument: distinctiveness can't be copied from a formula — it emerges from consistently showing up as yourself until your natural quirks (voice, taste, obsessions) become recognizable. She pairs this with a practical case for distribution — SEO, visuals, cross-posting, cold outreach — arguing that craft without distribution goes unheard, and that "just show up" alone isn't enough advice either. A recurring theme is patience: real creative distinctiveness, like a 40-year door or a redwood tree, can't be rushed into being on a launch deadline.

## Key ideas

**On distinctiveness (can't be copy-pasted)**
- **Voice comes from volume, not instruction.** Smith argues distinctiveness is "not something we learn how to do in school" — it surfaces only through years of writing and getting feedback like "I like when you do that." She says it took her years to identify her own "adjective" (friendliness), and she only recognized it because others pointed it out repeatedly.
- **"What would everyone else not do?"** — her reframing of differentiation. Rather than copying a successful format, ask what the obvious, expected move is, and deliberately skip it. This is how she reads Barstool Sports vs. ESPN and The Skimm vs. traditional morning news: taking a "serious" domain and talking about it the way normal people actually talk.
- **Distinctiveness is being squeezed out by convergence.** She compares it to golf swings: modern instruction and video analysis have made professional swings nearly identical, killing the "kooky" swings of the 70s/80s. She wonders if writing/media is undergoing the same homogenization as everyone studies the same "best practices."
- **Visuals and graphics are underrated distribution/distinctiveness levers.** She cites Derek Thompson at The Atlantic using a data screenshot instead of a generic thumbnail, and "moving infographics" (a genre she says is genuinely net-new, not achievable pre-internet) as an emerging craft most writers ignore in favor of polishing text.
- **Branding decisions compound.** Referencing Tim Urban's "line thickness" quote — a single early stylistic choice "saves you a thousand decisions in the future" — she contrasts Jack Butcher's crisp, minimalist Visualize Value aesthetic against Tim Urban's goofy stick-figure Wait But Why, noting Urban actually ran an unremarkable blog for ~250 posts before Wait But Why found its voice — distinctiveness is discovered, not planned from day one.

**On patience and identity as a creator**
- **The "40-year door" metaphor.** At a friend's Japanese-style estate, a door "took 40 years to build" and looks like it — she uses this to argue creative distinctiveness (citing Kevin Kelly's deliberately eclectic, backpacking-fueled life and work) can't be manufactured on a deadline; it has to accumulate over "many, many years."
- **Financial pressure changes the clock.** Once creative work becomes your livelihood, the natural, unhurried "germination" period gets replaced by short-term metrics-chasing (followers, subscribers) — she frames this as a real trade-off, not a moral failing.
- **Reinvention is a legitimate cost of authenticity.** She uses musician Porter Robinson's arc — the acclaimed 2014 album *Worlds*, a depressive identity crisis, then the deliberately different 2020 album *Nurture* — as an example of an artist refusing to "be who they were" just to replicate past success, even at commercial risk.

**On distribution (craft alone isn't enough)**
- **SEO as market research, not just a growth hack.** She frames Google Trends/keyword data as "billions of people" revealing what they actually care about — useful even for writers who never optimize for search, because it shows real demand and phrasing, not guesses.
- **Roughly half your time should go to distribution**, not just writing — cross-posting to Hacker News, Lobsters, Medium, Dev.to, Product Hunt communities, and doing direct outreach to people whose books/work she referenced (a tactic she says drove real traction and retweets).
- **Hacker News cuts both ways.** A front-page post can net ~30,000 views but also blunt, sometimes harsh feedback — she frames that critical mass of reactions as still useful signal about what lands, distinct from parasocial engagement.

**On craft, meaning, and process**
- **Emails, not pageviews, are her real KPI.** She values messages where someone says a piece changed how they think about something meaningful (she cites religion specifically) over traffic numbers — "not page views, not likes, not bookmarks."
- **Kevin Kelly's line "radical honesty"** shaped her shift away from trying to sound impressive/knowledgeable toward writing "here's what I struggle with" — she says vulnerability, not authority, is what people resonate with.
- **Writing process: capture fast, let ideas ruminate for weeks or months**, often using a tool called Draftback (a Chrome plugin showing Google Docs version history) to review how a draft evolved. Her essay "How to Be Great" took weeks between the first idea and finding its actual hook/title.
- **Ideas are "in your forehead"** — a physical bit she does with Perell (pointing at your forehead, unable to see it) to illustrate that people often can't see their own best material until someone else reflects it back to them ("David, you're so passionate about that").

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's the "adjective" other people would use to describe my own writing — and have I actually asked anyone, or am I guessing?
- Where am I currently optimizing for a short-term metric (subscribers, views) in a way that's cutting off the slow "germination" Smith describes?
- Is there a piece I'm sitting on that's "80% done" but missing the hook — and would letting it ruminate longer (rather than forcing a deadline) actually help it?
- Am I spending anywhere close to 50% of my effort on distribution, or is my time overwhelmingly weighted toward writing itself?
- What's the equivalent of "the thing in my forehead" — the obvious-to-others, invisible-to-me thing I keep doing that I haven't recognized as my actual voice?
