## TL;DR
David Perell interviews crime novelist Michael Connelly (Harry Bosch, The Lincoln Lawyer) about how he builds vivid characters and places without heavy description. Connelly's core method is the "telling detail" — one small, specific observation that stands in for pages of exposition — combined with ruthless cutting during rewrites to preserve narrative momentum. He treats character as the purpose of every other craft decision (plot, place, dialogue, conflict), trusts readers to infer rather than explaining things for them, and draws his sense of place from decades as a crime journalist in Los Angeles, a city he treats almost as a character in its own right.

## Key ideas
- **The "telling detail" trick.** Instead of piling on five details, pick the one that reveals character. Connelly's example: a detective who clenches his jaw at crime scenes has worn a groove into the earpiece of his glasses from biting down on it — one physical detail that conveys the man's suppressed rage without narration.
- **Momentum over detail.** Connelly says the "key thing, maybe the key thing" in both writing and reading is momentum — "a lot of details create speed bumps." Cutting, not adding, is how a scene gets more vivid.
- **Rewriting is where detail happens, not first drafts.** He rewrites the previous day's pages every morning (printed on paper, marked up in pencil with personal shorthand like "RW" for rewrite-the-paragraph and "NSG" for not-so-good, a habit inherited from his mother's margin notes on his manuscripts), then does a full paper read-through at the end of a book.
- **Research serves speed, not accuracy for its own sake.** As a 14-year crime/courts journalist he has enough working knowledge to write without stopping; when he needs facts he texts a network of detectives, lawyers, and judges, or gives his researcher a narrow question — "I'm looking for a concise answer... not a page after page after page."
- **Character above all else.** "Realtors talk about location, location, location. For me it's character, character, character." Plot and place exist to deliver character — details of job, geography, weather, and history exist to plant a fictional person's feet in a real world for a "smart reader."
- **Place as character, not backdrop.** LA works for him because it's too vast to ever fully cover after 42 books and ~35 years of publishing — he deliberately sets each new book in a neighborhood he hasn't used before. He cites LA's segregation by freeway and mountain, its status as an entertainment capital full of people chasing "second chances" that don't always pay off, and the visible gap between haves and have-nots as sources of narrative friction.
- **Chandler's influence.** He rereads chapter 13 of Raymond Chandler's *The Little Sister* — a plotless five-page detour where Marlowe just drives around and describes LA — before starting every new book, calling it his personal definition of art: descriptions from 1939 that are "right on right now."
- **Dialogue must be cut, not embellished.** "You almost as soon as you write it, you go back and cut it in half." His newspaper training — fitting stories into "give me six inches" — taught him dialogue must carry information the reader needs, with no fluff, and that habit carried straight into fiction.
- **Character through restraint, not speech.** Harry Bosch is built to feel like an outsider despite carrying a badge, partly by *not* using dialogue — he nods instead of answering. An old editor once tallied that Bosch had nodded 540 times by page 200 of a manuscript.
- **Conflict on every page.** Connelly's favorite piece of writing advice, from Kurt Vonnegut: "Make sure on every page every character wants something even if it's only a glass of water." Early in his career he made Bosch a smoker in a smoke-free society specifically to guarantee low-grade conflict on every page.
- **Heroes over villains.** Connelly says he isn't very interested in villain psychology and leaves that to other writers; he's drawn instead to what it costs a hero to go into "moral darkness" and keep it from "metastasizing" inside them — that struggle, he says, is close to the actual thesis of his books.
- **The "slingshot" opening.** He used to believe you had to release narrative tension within 10 pages (later 25); now, decades in, he'll open a book with two chapters that don't touch the plot at all, trusting earned reader loyalty to carry them through.
- **Naming as metaphor.** Harry Bosch's name started in draft as "Pierce" (echoing Chandler's line that a detective must "pierce all veils of society"), then became "Bosch" after Connelly recalled painter Hieronymus Bosch and built a deliberate, half-hidden metaphor: LA as a modern *Garden of Earthly Delights*.
- **Books vs. TV.** Books can render interior thought; scripts can't — "when you write a script, you can never say what anyone is thinking." He stayed hands-on with early seasons of his TV adaptations (Bosch ran ~98 episodes) mainly because serialized streaming finally gave him the space to deliver full characters the way novels do.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I just try to watch for what I call telling details... it opens up a window of imagination for your reader." — Michael Connelly
- "Instead of five details, you pick the one that says something about the situation, the character." — Michael Connelly
- "Make sure on every page every character wants something even if it's only a glass of water." — Michael Connelly, quoting Kurt Vonnegut
- "My primary purpose, almost my sole purpose, is to deliver character to your reader. So never miss a chance to say something about character." — Michael Connelly
- "When you circle around a murder long enough you get to know a city." — Michael Connelly, quoting Richard Price
- "I eminently trust my readers to pick up on the nuances of conflict, especially in dialogue." — Michael Connelly
- "If you're going to be a writer, you got to write every day, even if it's only for 15 minutes... you're not going to lose the story. It's always going to be in your head." — Michael Connelly, recalling teacher Harry Crews
- "California, the department store state. The most of everything and the best of nothing." — Raymond Chandler, *The Little Sister*, ch. 13, quoted by Connelly

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I stacking multiple details when one "telling detail" would do more work and preserve momentum?
- Am I over-explaining conflict and subtext instead of trusting the reader/audience to infer it, the way Connelly resists his editors' notes to be "more explicit"?
- Does my current draft have Connelly's Vonnegut test satisfied — does every page/scene have someone wanting something, even something small?
- Could a name, place, or object in something I'm writing carry a hidden metaphor the way "Bosch" does, rewarding readers who dig without punishing those who don't?
- Do I have a daily "always be writing" structure — a fixed low-interruption block — or am I waiting for ideal conditions to start?
