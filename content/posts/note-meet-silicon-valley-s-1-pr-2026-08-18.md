## TL;DR
David Perell interviews communications strategist Lulu Cheng Meservey on why corporate speak exists (risk minimization masquerading as roles people "cosplay") and how to write with more honesty and risk instead. She draws heavily on counterinsurgency doctrine, religious evangelism (especially Christianity's underdog-to-triumph story arc), and behavioral psychology to explain how ideas actually spread: find centers of influence, build your own distribution, and frame messages so they slot into people's existing "cultural erogenous zones" rather than fighting to create new ones. Much of the conversation is about craft — writing in advance of a crisis, cutting for clarity, giving readers exactly three emotions, and being willing to take risks and defend yourself publicly rather than hiding behind institutional blandness.

## Key ideas
- **Corporate speak is risk minimization, not strategy.** Lulu argues bland corporate language exists because people entering new roles start "cosplaying" their corporate ancestors and optimize for not getting in trouble rather than for being understood — at the cost of missed opportunities.
- **Test writing out loud.** Her check for corporate speak: say the sentence like you'd say it to a friend. If you'd cringe saying it aloud, cut it.
- **Do the hard writing before the crisis hits.** Draft statements, doctrine, and decision authority ("who can say what without asking permission") in advance, so that when a crisis happens it's "basically like mad libs" — swap a few verbs and nouns rather than writing from scratch under pressure.
- **Give readers at most three emotions.** Citing advice she got, she treats three as the upper limit for what a piece of writing should make someone feel — more than that and nothing lands.
- **Companies should feel "of the people, by the people, for the people."** A company feels trustworthy when it culturally resembles the customers it serves — this builds affinity and familiarity, not just credibility.
- **The counterinsurgency and Christianity framework for spreading ideas.** If you're not the incumbent, you win hearts and minds by finding existing centers of influence, building your own distribution channel, and speaking directly to power rather than waiting for institutions to carry your message — the same underdog-to-global-movement pattern Christianity used.
- **The universal story shape: paradise, fall, quest, return.** Every person's mind, she argues, has "a hole shaped in the story of the ideal — something went horribly wrong, there's a solution, you have to do this thing, and once you do it you get back to where you were supposed to have been." Companies that map their narrative onto this shape resonate.
- **Depth vs. reach is a conscious trade-off.** Going mass-market (Joe Rogan-style reach) waters down resonance; going deep with fewer people creates something "tailored... incredibly deep and meaningful." Which to choose depends on the specific ask — a $1M investment needs deep trust with few people; a broad awareness goal needs scale.
- **Framing determines acceptance before content does.** Whether you're "giving" someone gold coins or "keeping" some of them changes acceptance even when the facts are identical — she connects this to the psychology lab work she did on game theory and behavioral framing.
- **Cultural erogenous zones — build the API, don't fight the culture.** People already have receptors for certain ideas; your job is to shape your message to fit those receptors (or build a bridge/"gateway drug") rather than trying to create new receptors from scratch. Example: Kamala Harris connecting national defense to K-12 reading levels to make people who don't care about education suddenly care.
- **"Tit for two tats," not tit-for-tat.** In repeated-game situations (unlike a one-shot prisoner's dilemma), she gives people two chances before publicly striking back — and does it dispassionately, only after taking time to remove emotion from the response.
- **It's easier to defend others than yourself.** Lulu deliberately built a public voice before she needed it, because a third party defending you is always more convincing to bystanders than self-defense, which reads as sheepish.
- **Simplicity is a discipline, not a talent problem.** She explicitly rejects trying to write like Hunter S. Thompson; her goal is intelligibility — short words, no jargon, no assumed prior knowledge, writing for a "five-year-old" (or the 4-14 range).
- **Choose a smaller, truer audience over scale.** She compares this to Jesus not maximizing to 12,000 disciples — a small list of true believers who'll do the work (share, defend, invest hours) beats a large list of lukewarm subscribers.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "cosplaying" a role (blogger, professional, expert) instead of just saying the thing plainly — and would reading it aloud expose it?
- What's the paradise → fall → quest → return shape of the story I'm actually telling in my current draft, and am I skipping straight to "quest" without establishing the fall?
- Am I choosing depth-with-few-readers or reach-with-many, and is that choice deliberate or just default behavior?
- What existing "cultural erogenous zone" does my audience already have that my idea could attach to, instead of me trying to manufacture interest from nothing?
- Do I have any pre-written "doctrine" for how I'll respond to a bad-faith attack or crisis, or would I be writing it from scratch under stress?
