## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Basecamp/37signals CEO Jason Fried about his approach to writing. Fried's core belief, captured in his line "don't write, communicate," is that writing should feel like talking, not like performing — you follow a "flow" or "hole" rather than grinding through struggle, and you stop and start over rather than force a piece to work. Most of the episode dissects two of Fried's founder letters (for Once and Hey) line by line to show the mechanics behind his style: short punchy sentences, "bounce" and rhythm, alliteration used sparingly, and paragraphs that end with a summarizing punch. He also connects this philosophy to how 37signals runs internally — hiring on cover letters instead of résumés, banning AI-written "heartbeat" updates, and preferring long-form writing over meetings.

## Key ideas

**On process — struggle is a signal to stop, not push through**
- Fried treats writer's struggle as a signal to abandon the draft, not fight it: "when I struggle with writing, it's a signal to stop writing... come back to a blank page and start over."
- He writes fast and throws work away readily rather than editing a bad draft into a good one — "I'm not a big fan of starting with something and working it to death until I get there."
- He uses sports metaphors (a running back finding a hole in the line, skiing a line) to describe flow: "I found a hole and I need to run because that hole will close in 12 hours."
- Writing is a "400 meter" sprint for him, not a marathon — his books (Getting Real, Rework, Remote, It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work) are all collections of short essays, never long-form chapters, because that's his natural length.

**On imperfection — don't sterilize the writing**
- He deliberately doesn't chase precision: a Navajo rug weaver's explanation (mistakes in the pattern aren't fixed, "you just keep going") shaped how he thinks about typos and awkward-but-felt word choices.
- Editors who "sterilize" writing by swapping in the technically correct word destroy the personality — he wants writing that feels "lived in," like a patinaed old room, not a sterile modern one.
- He doesn't ask for feedback before publishing and will fix typos after the fact rather than delay: "I don't care that 340 people saw it wrong."

**On why business writing is bad**
- He blames MBA/business-school writing culture (fake business plans, BS-ing) and companies that "have a lot to sell" but "nothing to say."
- He rejects the word "content": "it's communication... What do you want to communicate? Not what do you want to put out there for SEO."
- Most companies follow each other and copy styles instead of finding their own voice, producing writing so generic "you can replace this logo with that logo and it'll be the same thing."

**Anatomy of the founder letters (Once and Hey)**
- Both open with a deliberately provocative first line ("Something happened to business software") designed to plant a question in the reader's head without asking one directly.
- Structure: establish the old/broken reality first so the reader nods along ("Yeah, yeah, yeah"), *then* pivot to the new solution — get agreement before you ask for anything.
- He builds extended metaphors deliberately but lets the specific phrasing emerge by accident — "landlord-tenant" for SaaS came from free-associating off the word "owning," not from planning.
- He uses rhythm devices intentionally: alliteration ("avalanche of automated," "payers and prayers to the church of recurring revenue"), and paragraphs that end on a short punchy summary line ("Boom, you're evicted").
- These rhetorical flourishes are used *sparingly* against a backdrop of otherwise short, plain words — the rarity is what makes them land, "like coming across a lizard on a walk."

**On distillation ("the writing class I'd like to teach")**
- His dream class: take a 5-page piece and distill it to one page, then one paragraph, one sentence, one phrase — because adding words is easy, cutting without losing meaning is the real skill.
- Distillation is "emergent," not mechanical — the final one-word summary ("Once") may not even be a word that appeared logically from shrinking the piece; it surfaced from the writing process itself.

**Internal communication at 37signals**
- Cover letters, not résumés, are the first hiring filter — a generic cover letter is disqualifying even if well-written, because it wasn't written *to them*.
- No meetings for status updates; every team lead writes a "heartbeat" every 6 weeks summarizing their team's work, searchable and archived — deliberately never AI-generated, because personality and individual voice ("I know Rosa wrote that") carry real information.
- Long-form writing builds institutional knowledge that a 40-hour video recording of meetings never will — "if you weren't there you missed it."

**On marketing copy and websites**
- His test for copy: "would I ever say that out loud to anybody?" If not, cut it — cleverness that wouldn't survive being spoken aloud is dishonest.
- He dislikes typical website design (chopped-up blocks of text and images) because it isn't readable the way a letter is; that's part of why he writes founder letters instead of standard landing-page copy.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "efforting" toward a contrived line instead of following an actual flow — and would throwing the draft away and restarting be faster than fixing it?
- Do I have a natural "length" the way Fried has a 400-meter cap — and am I fighting it by trying to stretch pieces longer than they want to be?
- What's my equivalent of the "would I say this out loud?" test for anything I write that's meant to sound impressive rather than communicate clearly?
- Could a distillation exercise (cut a piece to one page, one paragraph, one sentence) reveal what a current draft is actually trying to say?
- Am I sterilizing my own writing — smoothing out the specific, slightly-off phrasing that carries personality — in the name of correctness?
