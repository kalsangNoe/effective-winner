## TL;DR
Ward Farnsworth (law professor, author of *Classical English Style* and *Classical English Rhetoric*) argues memorable prose isn't about being clear and concise — that's only "lesson one." Memorable writing comes from deliberately exploiting contrast: between simple Saxon words and fancy Latinate/French ones, and between patterns of repetition. He walks through three core rhetorical devices — Saxon vs. Latinate diction, anaphora/epistrophe (repetition at the start/end of clauses), and chiasmus (ABBA reversal) — using close readings of the King James Bible, Churchill, Lincoln, JFK, and Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. His larger point: these tools work through contrast, not through a single rule applied relentlessly, and the way to internalize them is immersion and imitation-then-discard, not conscious deployment.

## Key ideas

**1. English is "bilingual" — every important word has a Saxon and a Latinate twin.**
Old Germanic (Saxon) words came from Anglo invaders; French/Latin (Latinate) words arrived ~500 years later with the Norman aristocracy. Pairs: create/make, acquire/get, permit/let, illumination/light, final/last. Latinate words can take a "-tion" ending (acquisition, termination); Saxon words generally can't. Saxon words tend to be short, one-syllable, and land in the gut; Latinate words sound polite/impressive but appeal more to the mind (kill vs. execute/terminate).

**2. Rule one, if you only get one: prefer Saxon words** — but the real power is in contrast, not purity. Genesis 1:3 ("Let there be light") is 11 Saxon words straight. Churchill's "We shall fight..." runs 32 Saxon words in a row — Churchill wrote about rhetoric and said Saxon words "strike deepest." But the more sophisticated move is setting up a Latinate opening and closing in Saxon (or vice versa) for contrast: "Never in the field of human conflict [Latinate] was so much owed by so many to so few [Saxon]." Always start analyzing from the end of the sentence — that's the part that rings in the ear.

**3. The passive voice can be the better choice.** Churchill's "so much owed by so many to so few" is passive; rewritten active ("so many have owed so much to so few") loses the rhythm and the tight pairing of "many" and "few." Farnsworth's master rule for all of writing: no fixed rules, "just make sure you've got a good reason for what you're doing."

**4. Saying it twice, in two languages.** Lincoln loved restating a Latinate clause in Saxon immediately after: "I do not expect the union to be dissolved [Latinate] ... I do not expect the house to fall [Saxon]." One version appeals to the mind, the other hits the gut — Farnsworth frames it as literally speaking to the reader in both of English's "languages."

**5. Anaphora vs. epistrophe.** Anaphora = repeating words at the *start* of successive clauses (Churchill's "We shall fight... we shall fight..."; MLK's "I have a dream"). Epistrophe = repeating at the *end* (Lincoln's Gettysburg Address: "...what we say here... what they did here"; "government of the people, by the people, for the people"). Because both the start and end of a sentence carry the most rhetorical weight, these devices work by loading the strongest positions. The best speakers often reverse mid-passage — Churchill's "to the utmost" line moves from epistrophe ("giving all, daring all, enduring all") into anaphora, and that switch itself is another form of ear-grabbing contrast.

**6. Chiasmus (ABBA reversal).** JFK's "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" is a pure chiasmus (country/you/you/country). Farnsworth's practical test for *when* to reach for one: use it when someone "has it backwards" — the relationship people assume runs A-to-B actually runs B-to-A (debt, credit, blame). Chiasmus doesn't have to be literal word-repetition — it can be structural/conceptual, as in Lincoln's "I claim not to have controlled events, but confess plainly that events have controlled me," or the Lord's Prayer's "lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" (simple words bookending fancy ones in the middle — a chiasmus built from the Saxon/Latinate contrast, not from repeated words).

**7. Rhetoric is a double-edged tool.** Beautifully constructed sentences *sound* more true regardless of whether they are. Studying rhetoric is partly defensive — "inoculate yourself against the charms of people who put things beautifully."

**8. Practical advice: use these devices sparingly, and don't imitate directly.** Overuse reads as trying too hard (a hallmark of bad political speech). Lincoln never sounded like he was imitating the Bible or Shakespeare despite absorbing both for thousands of hours — the goal is influence, not imitation. Farnsworth separates "imitation" (copying someone's voice, which makes you sound like a fool) from "influence" (immersion that quietly shapes your natural voice).

**9. His ideal writing curriculum.** Lesson one — most writing courses stop here — is efficiency: cut needless words, be clear and concise. Lesson two, which he thinks is neglected, is what separates merely-clear writers from memorable ones like Lincoln. His classroom exercise: have students rewrite modern Supreme Court opinions in the style of Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., not to keep imitating but to force close attention to what makes a style work — then discard the imitation and keep what was learned. He also writes out quotes by hand rather than copy-pasting, because transcribing forces you to notice the writing's mechanics.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "If you have to have one rule is... prefer Saxon words to romance words or Latinate words." — Farnsworth
- "There's no rule except make sure you've got a good reason for what you're doing. As far as I'm concerned, that's the master rule." — Farnsworth
- "Anything that's great tends to be greater when it's set off against what it isn't, because that prepares the ear for it." — Farnsworth
- "If they notice the technique, it's bad technique." — Farnsworth
- "It's very true that when you state something very eloquently... it admits of no interruption or objection. It can really make what you say sound more true... things that are beautifully said sound more true. No reason to believe they are." — Farnsworth
- "Nobody's writing sounds right unless... writing sounds best when you're basically speaking the words onto the page." — Farnsworth
- "Good writing is the most efficient writing. End of story. That's what I think is the sort of ethos behind most books on writing... but you can be clear and concise and mesmerizing, inspiring and memorable, or clear and concise and tedious and boring and nobody cares." — Farnsworth

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- In my own writing, do I default entirely to Latinate/"professional" vocabulary — and where could swapping in Saxon words at a sentence's end give it more punch?
- Where in a piece am I already repeating an idea implicitly — could I make that repetition structural (anaphora/epistrophe) instead of accidental?
- Am I ever "trying it backwards" — is there a claim I'm making where the relationship is actually reversed from how I've framed it, which could become a chiasmus?
- What am I currently immersing myself in that's shaping my "natural" voice — and is it good rhetoric or, per Farnsworth's warning, the low-grade rhetoric of social media?
- Which of my own pieces could I test with Farnsworth's "start at the end" method — read only the last line of each paragraph and see if it lands?
