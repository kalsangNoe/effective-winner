## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Jack Carr, a former Navy SEAL turned #1 New York Times bestselling thriller author (The Terminal List, adapted by Amazon with Chris Pratt), about his concrete writing process. Carr's method is systematic: a one-page "executive summary" for every book idea, a one-word or one-sentence theme he keeps on a sticky note, a three-act outline, and a dedicated notebook and computer per book to eliminate distraction. He argues craft rules (short chapters, simple sentences) are irrelevant — the only thing that matters is serving the story — while specificity in physical detail (gear, watches, vehicles, eye color) is what makes characters and places feel real. He's equally blunt that writing today is inseparable from building an audience and treating authorship as a business, not just an art.

## Key ideas

**On drawing from lived experience**
- Carr doesn't need to "get back into" his SEAL deployments to write combat — he says it's as natural as a former car salesman writing a car-buying scene. He transplants remembered *feelings and strategic situations* (why they couldn't enter a building in Baghdad, an ambush in Ramadi) directly onto fictional scenes (an ambush in Los Angeles) without needing to interview anyone.

**The Executive Summary method**
- Before starting a book, Carr writes several one-page (sometimes shorter) "executive summaries" — like the back-cover copy of a paperback — for competing ideas, lays them on the kitchen table, and picks the one that would make a stranger in an airport bookstore want to spend their limited time on it.
- Each book also gets one guiding word or short phrase as its theme (e.g., "revenge without constraint" for The Terminal List), inspired by a garbled memory of something Steven Pressfield said. He writes it on a yellow sticky note by his desk. The theme isn't binding on every scene but keeps the story "directly or indirectly" on track.
- The executive summary is then built into an outline, and only the outline becomes the actual manuscript — the executive summary itself is discarded.

**There are no writing rules**
- Carr explicitly rejects reader-optimization advice: no thinking about chapter length, sentence length, or "what's palatable to readers these days." His only checklist item is story. He never revises based on isolated reader complaints, only patterns.

**Outlining without perfectionism**
- He deliberately leaves gaps in the outline rather than stalling for weeks on a single plot problem (e.g., "how does he get out of this cave in China"), trusting he'll solve it during the year of actual writing. Unlike a real firefight, a plot problem can wait — "I can sleep on it."

**Three-act structure**
- Most of his books fall into three parts, which he attributes to imprinting from the books he read as a kid (age 10–20 is the formative window, in his view) rather than a deliberate formula.

**Making characters and places feel alive through specific objects**
- Carr treats physical details — what someone drives, wears, or carries — as compressed backstory, the same way he reads a stranger's holster and belt in a gun store in under a second. He cites Ian Fleming's Bond as an influence for describing branded, specific products (weapons, watches, cars) to build character.
- His first research stop for weapons is the Vickers Guides (Larry Vickers/James Rupley photo books); he sends drafted gear descriptions to Rupley to fact-check.
- Counterintuitively, he avoids over-describing settings (e.g., not fully detailing a room) so readers project their own memories onto it — he cites "white walls, fireplace, Hemingway star" as enough specificity to anchor the scene without closing it off.
- Eye color/description matters a lot for protagonists because it's a recurring identity marker across a long series.

**Research and immersion**
- He tries to physically visit at least one location per book (Mozambique, Kamchatka, Ukraine, Morocco); COVID forced him to research Israel entirely from books, photos, and maps — a family of three generations in Israel read the manuscript and couldn't believe he'd never been.
- For Cry Havoc (set in 1968 Vietnam), he surrounded his office with period maps, dictionaries, National Geographics, and era-appropriate watches and knives to "transport myself back in time," because characters in the story can only draw on knowledge available in 1968, not 50 years of hindsight.
- Every book gets its own physical notebook and its own dedicated computer with no email or other distractions, just a private email address for sending himself notes.

**Mantras**
- "Give yourself permission to write a bad sentence, a bad chapter, a bad book — then go back and make it great." Don't stall comparing your first draft to a finished, published book.
- "You just need to do the job" — writing is a profession, not a hobby, invoking Pressfield's line that the dentist doesn't get dentist's block.
- Distinguishes "profession" from "career" (drawing on the novel *Once an Eagle*) — he never thought of the military or writing as a career, only a profession he's obligated to keep improving at.
- "Move the genre forward" — his stated goal for every book is that it be better than the last, and he name-checks genre landmarks (Most Dangerous Game, Rogue Male, First Blood, The Spy Who Came in from the Cold, Brotherhood of the Rose, Term Limits) as books that didn't just retell a spy story but changed it.
- "Never pay attention to the odds" — people love reciting statistics about how hard something is (getting published, becoming a Navy SEAL); he ignores them because someone always beats the odds.

**Editing process**
- No editor involvement until the very end. He sends drafts to five trusted beta readers (not literary agents) — two of them attorneys, whom he says are unusually sharp readers/editors. For his first book he used ~25 readers and set a threshold: if only 2–4 flagged the same issue he might ignore it, but 5+ meant he had to fix it.
- His actual editor, Emily Bestler, gives two kinds of notes: explain things for readers without 20 years of military background, and don't assume readers will infer twists you've been sitting with for a year and a half.

**Writing as a business**
- Carr is blunt that today's author has to be "CEO, CFO, CMO" simultaneously — building an audience is not optional the way it might have been in 1985. He builds relationships with readers year-round through free value (gear lists for holidays, book-specific gear lists) so that by the time a new book launches there's already trust.
- Podcasts, in his view, drive the fast growth of audiobooks because listening to a podcast primes people to want to "listen" to a book the same way.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Give yourself permission to write a bad sentence, give yourself permission to write a bad chapter, give yourself permission to write a bad book, and then go back and make it great." — Jack Carr
- "I'm not sure that you can learn anything better than at the end of the day you just need to sit down and just write." — Jack Carr
- "The dentist doesn't get dentist block. A truck driver doesn't get truck driver's block. You sit down and you write. You're a professional." — Jack Carr (paraphrasing Steven Pressfield's *Turning Pro*)
- "If it was easy everybody would do it. But somebody did it... I can do it too. Why not me?" — Jack Carr, on ignoring the odds
- "It is all about the story... I haven't made any sort of concessions to 'people only like short chapters' or 'these shorter sentences because Hemingway did it.'" — Jack Carr
- "White walls, fireplace, Hemingway star — we have specific [detail] there, but everybody else is going to bring their personal experience to what a fireplace looks like." — Jack Carr, on the value of under-describing
- "You have to know the history of the genre... so you can honor those who went before who essentially opened the door for you." — Jack Carr

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I have an equivalent of Carr's "executive summary" test before committing to a long piece — would a stranger spend limited time on this if they read the pitch cold?
- Where am I over-describing instead of leaving room for the reader/viewer to bring their own experience, the way Carr deliberately underspecifies rooms?
- Am I stalling on unsolved structural problems instead of trusting I'll figure them out mid-process, the way Carr refuses to get stuck on outline gaps?
- What's my equivalent of a "theme on a sticky note" to keep long projects from drifting?
- Do I treat the audience-building/distribution side of my work as seriously as Carr treats it, or am I assuming the work alone will find readers?
