## TL;DR
David Perell interviews ESPN writer Wright Thompson (author of *The Barn*, profiler of Michael Jordan, Tiger Woods, Caitlin Clark) about the mechanics of nonfiction storytelling. Thompson's core claim: after 25 years, writing stopped being about words and became about architecture — and the thing that actually unlocks good writing is reporting, not prose style. He explains how to make a subject's interior life visible through earned exterior detail, why endings should stay "elliptical" rather than resolved, why place-writing is really self-understanding in disguise, and why he distrusts both outlining-as-dogma and brainstorming meetings.

## Key ideas

- **Writer's block is a myth; "writer's vomit" is the real disease.** Bad writing is the easiest thing in the world. The danger isn't an empty page, it's the "artifice" writers build between themselves and the truth once they've learned enough craft tools. All writing is "trying to say something new that's true that is both specific and universal."

- **Writing is architecture, not words.** Thompson wishes someone had told him early that a career of professional writing is about structure — "only when you really understand how things fit together and move can you then sort of like actually be thinking about the words." He built that understanding through reps: 1,200-word stories, then 2,500-word stories, then five years of 3,600-word pieces at the Kansas City Star, then 7,200–8,500-word ESPN Magazine features, then 12,000–15,000, then books.

- **A story idea isn't inherently good or bad — obsession is the filter.** He used to chase "white whales," stories that would impress bosses. Now the only question is whether he's "really really interested in" it and willing to spend the time.

- **Interiority is the whole job of a profile.** Quoting Gary Smith: a profile is "figuring out what is the central complication of somebody's life and how on a daily basis do they go about solving it," and almost all of that happens inside. His example: once you know Michael Jordan falls asleep to Westerns because that's where he feels closest to his late father, you can later just describe him watching a Western and the reader carries the emotional weight without explanation.

- **"Overwritten" really means "underreported."** Purple prose is a symptom of a reporting hole you're trying to write your way around, and you can't. He cites Baxter Holmes' ESPN story on the origin of Kobe Bryant's "Black Mamba" persona — other writers had heard the same rumors for years, but Holmes got people on the record with real detail. Detail example: Tiger Woods' two boats, moored a few slips apart, named *Privacy* and *Solitude*.

- **Endings should be elliptical, never a "kicker."** "Don't hammer the door shut. A story is supposed to open a door" — not close it. His Yankee Stadium piece was built backward from one image: Lou Gehrig's widow dying in the 1980s with no one at her funeral. His Caitlin Clark profile answers "how did she become Caitlin Clark" but deliberately leaves "what happens next" unresolved, because that mirrors real life.

- **Place-writing is a proxy for self-understanding.** "Me trying to understand other places are just proxies for me trying to understand my own place." For Thompson that place is the Mississippi Delta; *The Barn* — set at the site where Emmett Till was killed — grew out of trying to "excavate the deep, deep history of one place," the way Faulkner's *Absalom, Absalom!* does.

- **Brainstorming meetings optimize for the wrong thing.** "In most brainstorming sessions the goal for the individuals... is to look good, not to make the thing good." He'd ban chairs from meetings and require tacos after 15 minutes. His favorite line in years of TV sense-of-place essays came unplanned, from a freezing cameraman in Scotland: "Scotland, where the cows wear coats."

- **Every format demands different craft.** TV writing is "tell, don't show," because the camera already shows. Documentary writing is best as no writing at all — "if you're writing, you failed" — just sound, with sparing "skinny track" narration. A magazine story is a "dispatch... a letter from a time and a place"; a book "is trying to make" a universe rather than capture one. The first draft of *The Barn* tried to hold everything a book needed inside magazine-story architecture — it ran 280,000 words and he cut 173,000.

- **He doesn't track how pieces perform.** His favorite piece, on Archie Manning, "no one read." A story about a Houston rapper he shadowed for one World Series night "melted the internet." His conclusion, echoing the *post hoc ergo propter hoc* fallacy: "we have no idea what people want to read and why."

## Quotes worth keeping

- "All writing is is trying to say something new that's true that is both specific and universal and that helps the reader understand something they didn't understand before, preferably about themselves." — Wright Thompson
- "I wish someone had told me a long time ago that if you're going to be a professional writer for decades, writing is not going to be about words but is going to be about architecture." — Wright Thompson
- "Every time I feel like I can sort of write myself out of... a hole in my knowledge. It's just bad. All overwritten really means is underreported." — Wright Thompson
- "Don't hammer the door shut. A story is supposed to open a door. Like not close it." — Wright Thompson
- "The reader gets a vote... I'm trying to share a human experience with another human being. And if they're not getting it, it's not their fault, it's mine." — Wright Thompson
- "In most brainstorming sessions the goal for the individuals in the brainstorming session is to look good, not to make the thing good." — Wright Thompson
- "You are what you do when the lights come on... there's nowhere to hide. There are no lies." — Wright Thompson
- "A great detail will do the work of, you know, 50 shitty sentences." — Wright Thompson

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own drafts am I writing pretty sentences to paper over a reporting or research hole, instead of going and finding the missing detail?
- Do my endings resolve too neatly — am I "hammering the door shut" instead of leaving an honest, elliptical question open?
- For the people or subjects I write about, what is their "central complication," and am I showing it through earned exterior detail rather than stating it outright?
- Is there a "Mississippi Delta" of my own — a place or subject I keep circling — that I should excavate deeply instead of chasing new topics for novelty's sake?
- How much am I letting audience data or traffic quietly steer what I choose to write, and is that actually a reliable signal at all?
