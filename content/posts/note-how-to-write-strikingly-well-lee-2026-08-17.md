## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Lee Child, author of the Jack Reacher series (200M+ books sold), about how he actually writes: no outlines, one book a year on a fixed calendar, and a career-long obsession with "propulsion" — sentence rhythm engineered to pull both hardcore and casual readers through the page. Child argues writing requires believing two contradictory things 100% at once (it's art; it's a job), that plotting is easier than people think because humans are hardwired to chase an implied question, and that dialogue, violence, and beginnings/endings all work through illusion rather than realism. He closes by saying writing itself isn't teachable, only learnable — through decades of reading before you start.

## Key ideas

**Sense of place starts with temperature, not research.** Child never researches a location for a specific book. He picks a "key" the way a composer picks G major (cheerful) versus E-flat minor (melancholy) — hot/cold, hard/soft — drawn from places he's already visited, and lets that dictate what's plausible in the story ("it gives you a stage on which certain action is inevitable and certain is implausible"). He set Reacher in America specifically because British crime fiction (Barbara Vine, Ian Rankin) is geographically tiny and internal; he wanted the "mysterious stranger" myth, which needs a frontier-sized geography Britain can't supply.

**No outline, ever.** "If I were to plan a story... even if I did a two-page outline with two lines per proposed chapter, then I've told myself the story, and I'm bored with it at that point. I want the next story." He starts from a location and improvises from there — but says this is "not quite as naked as it sounds" because decades of reading (tens of thousands of books) give him an internalized library of plots, characters, and structures to draw on unconsciously.

**Writing is a job, not just a muse.** He started the series right after being fired from British TV, and treats craft and commerce as equally binding: "You've got to believe it's artistic... you've also got to believe it is a job that your family's income depends on... you've got to believe both things 100%. Which is mathematically impossible but you've got to do it." The obligation started financial (pay the bills) and became emotional (never disappoint readers who've stuck with the series).

**Propulsion is the whole craft.** Because a book is "however many thousand sentences in a row," Child spends most of his effort making each sentence's rhythm "tripping forward, forward, forward" — comparing it to a pop song that imperceptibly speeds up over its runtime (he cites Ringo Starr live). Readership isn't monolithic; he pictures it as "the rings of Saturn," with habitual, literate readers at the center and casual once-a-year readers at the outer edge. The prose has to satisfy both — propulsion is how he keeps his hand "gently on the back" of the casual reader, pushing them through without their noticing.

**Cliffhangers come straight from TV.** Child logged 40,000 hours in British television. He traces the modern chapter-ending hook to the arrival of the remote control (widespread by 1990, rare in 1980): once viewers could change channels instantly, shows had to bait them with an implied question to survive commercial breaks — the same trick as a baseball broadcast's trivia question. "Humans are hardwired to want the answer to a question... plotting in that sense is way overestimated in terms of difficulty."

**Dialogue is an illusion of naturalness.** Real speech is "incoherent... stop start... full of placeholders," yet books that reproduce that literally would be unreadable. The trick is faking coherence while preserving how emphasis lands — Child avoids italics and instead builds sentence rhythm so stress falls on the right word naturally, a skill he says comes only from listening to real people talk.

**Don't design characters to be liked or hated.** "You absolutely can't have a character designed to be hated. You absolutely equally cannot have a character that's designed to be liked, because the more you design it the worse it gets." His test for Reacher was simply whether he himself liked him — reasoning that readers share enough common culture that if the author is honestly compelled, a large audience will be too.

**Start in media res; readers want the answer, not a prompt.** "Don't start where when the earth cooled" — skip backstory and open on action. He tried withholding resolution twice (once leaving a character's fate unstated) and both times "readers hated it... they wanted your version rather than it's not like a writing prompt."

**Violence and clothing are both shorthand, not realism.** Real violence is brief and disabling, unlike the back-and-forth brawls in fiction. Child thinks readers — typically thoughtful, law-abiding people — want violence in books precisely because they support civilized restraint in real life and need fictional release from it ("even though you are good citizens... you all have a list of 10 people you would cheerfully shoot in the head"). Clothing and teeth work the same way: fast character-typing symbols an internal "comparative database" in the reader already recognizes.

**Writing isn't teachable, but it's learnable.** Child would turn down a university teaching gig — he thinks the business side of writing (agents, publishers) is teachable, but the writing itself isn't. It can only be learned, mainly by reading for years before attempting it: "Read for 20 years and then do it."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "explaining" instead of trusting rhythm and implication to do the work — am I over-signposting the way pre-1990 TV did?
- Do I have a "temperature" or governing key for a piece before I start, the way Child picks hot/cold before plot — or do I start from plot and let tone drift?
- Am I designing characters (or arguments) to be liked, and is that making them worse the way Child says over-designed characters backfire?
- Where do I open with backstory instead of in media res, and what would it cost to cut straight to the action or claim?
- What's actually in my own "internal database" from reading/consuming — and am I trusting it enough to work without an outline, or am I outlining out of fear of running dry?
