## TL;DR
Andrew Stanton — Pixar's second animator, director of Finding Nemo and WALL-E — argues that Pixar's formula isn't a formula at all: there are no 22 rules, no consensus process, and no writing "for kids." Instead the craft rests on a few repeatable moves: find the one-sentence premise (character + conflict + conclusion) that unlocks a story, keep the audience "clamped" to the narrative the way a cable car clamps to its cable, and treat every draft as disposable until you strike the true version through relentless rewriting. Good stories are uncovered like an archaeological dig, not planned in advance, and the willingness to blow up a near-finished structure (as with Nemo's prologue) separates Pixar from studios that "blink" when a late discovery contradicts the plan.

## Key ideas
- **Never write down to kids.** Pixar has "never written for kids," only for themselves — Stanton says he's "never worried for 5 seconds about a kid understanding what we're doing," because kids are expert readers of tone and body language. He worries more about adults being "too dumb to figure it out."
- **Test screenings are read by behavior, not by what people say.** The Q&A afterward yields little; the real signal is watching the audience in the dark — laughing, leaning forward — since "when the lights come up," people often lie about whether they liked it.
- **The premise sentence drives structure.** Borrowing from Lajos Egri's *The Art of Dramatic Writing*, Stanton hunts for a sentence with a character, a conflict, and a conclusion. For *Finding Nemo* it took about three years to land on "fear denies a good father from being one"; for *WALL-E*, "irrational love defeats life's programming."
- **Characters need a "spine."** A fixed core trait that doesn't change regardless of plot — borrowed from acting coach Judith Weston's example of Michael Corleone's spine (please his father). WALL-E's spine was "to find the beauty."
- **Stories are uncovered, not invented.** Stanton's recurring metaphor is an archaeological dig: you can take credit for choosing where to dig and how hard you dig, but not for what comes up. Pixar's edge over other studios, he says, is the willingness — even in "the 11th hour" — to admit the dinosaur is a Stegosaurus, not the Tyrannosaurus Rex they promised, and rebuild the ending around it.
- **The Nemo prologue rewrite as case study.** The barracuda attack that kills Nemo's mother was originally scattered as flashbacks throughout the film. Co-director Lee Unkrich suggested compressing it all into a single opening prologue. Stanton resisted ("that is so dark... like I'm going to kill Bambi's mom right at the top") but it worked — audiences suddenly had empathy for the father, and complaints about nearly every other scene disappeared. Lesson: "I would have changed the wrong thing for the wrong reasons."
- **Drama = anticipation mingled with uncertainty (William Archer).** Stanton's own analogy: a San Francisco cable car clamps onto a continuously moving underground chain. Good storytelling means staying "clamped" — never letting the audience unclamp — from the first scene to the last. A cruder version of the same idea: the "beach ball" thrown into a concert crowd — once it drops, people go back to checking their watch.
- **Great endings are surprising yet inevitable** (paraphrasing Aristotle) — the audience shouldn't see it coming, but once it happens, "there's no better answer."
- **WALL-E's origin: futility and loneliness first, romance second.** The Brain Trust (John Lasseter, Pete Docter, Joe Ranft, Stanton) started from "what's the lowliest thing" — a trash-compactor robot too dumb to know its job is futile, alone on an abandoned planet. It was called "Trash Planet" for years before Stanton and Lasseter decided it needed to be a romance between opposites, since "opposites always helps" — they create conflict.
- **Silent film as a formatting philosophy.** Researching WALL-E, the team watched Chaplin and Keaton films weekly and found they conveyed sophisticated ideas with almost no title cards. Stanton modeled WALL-E's script on a spare, haiku-like Walter Hill draft of *Alien* — one word to a line, forcing the reader to slow down to the film's actual pace rather than skimming to dialogue.
- **The Pixar Brain Trust is a methodology, not a fixed roster.** Originally five people (Lasseter, Ranft, Docter, Stanton, Unkrich) who kept meeting after the studio had to split into separate movie teams post-IPO. Stanton says it loses power above ~6 people because larger groups create an audience effect — people "act differently" and hide. His advice to other productions: "find your own brain trust."
- **Writing is rewriting — "just write the bad sentence."** Stanton's rule for people stuck on an ending or a middle: write the bad version now, because you don't get to the good sentence without passing through drafts one through eight or nine. He invokes "whistling on the steps of Carnegie Hall" — most people talk about the symphony they'll write more than they write it.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "We've never written for kids. We've just written for ourselves. So I guess we're just immature." — Andrew Stanton
- "Tell me what you do, not what you say." — David Perell, on reading test-screening audiences
- "Fear denies a good father from being one." — Stanton's premise sentence for *Finding Nemo*
- "I would have changed the wrong thing for the wrong reasons." — Stanton, on nearly rejecting the Nemo prologue rewrite
- "Drama is anticipation mingled with uncertainty." — William Archer, quoted by Stanton as "the best definition of drama I've ever heard"
- "When did we accidentally unclamp? Or did we ever clamp?" — Stanton's cable-car test for whether a story is working
- "I'd rather have a really bad joke told well [than a really good joke told poorly]." — Stanton, on why the telling matters as much as the content
- "So just write the bad sentence... you're just delaying getting to the nice sentence." — Stanton

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's my one-sentence "premise" (character + conflict + conclusion) for the piece I'm currently writing — and have I actually tested whether the draft follows it?
- Where in my own work am I "dulling out" the intense, honest version of something (like Nemo's opening tragedy) because it feels too dark or too much, when foregrounding it might actually earn more empathy?
- Do I have a "brain trust" of fewer than six people I can safely admit "I don't know what I'm doing" to — and if not, who could fill that role?
- Where does my writing lose its "clamp" — the point where a reader could look away without missing anything?
- Am I whistling on the steps of Carnegie Hall on anything right now — talking about a piece instead of writing the bad first sentence of it?
