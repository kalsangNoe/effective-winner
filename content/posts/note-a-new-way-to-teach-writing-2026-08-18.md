## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Michael Dean, a former architect who built "Essay Architecture," a framework that claims writing quality is measurable through 3 dimensions (idea, form, voice), 9 elements, and 27 recurring patterns — modeled on Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language*. Dean argues great writing isn't pure taste: he scores essays 1-5 on each pattern and shows how radically different essays (David Foster Wallace's "Consider the Lobster" and Orwell's "Shooting an Elephant") both score near-perfect while solving the same structural problems in opposite ways. The back half pivots to AI: Dean predicts AI writing quality will become "extraterrestrially good" but argues personal writing survives because it's the record of a specific life AI can't originate — and that writing is valuable less for its output than for what it does to the writer's mind.

## Key ideas

**The 27-pattern framework**
- **Idea** (material, thesis, title): material is your raw stories/references; thesis is what you cut around; title compresses the thesis into an entry point.
- **Form** (paragraphs, structure, tension): paragraphs are "the atomic unit of composition" (E.B. White); tension replaces "storytelling" as the term, since ideas — not just characters — can carry suspense.
- **Voice** (spirit, sound, sight): spirit is subtext/tone across paragraphs (what AI struggles with most); sound is rhythm/rhyme/repetition heard when read aloud; sight is concrete imagery.
- A pattern is "not a solution, it is a question" — there's no single correct answer, but the quality of any given answer can still be scored objectively.

**"Pick one volcano"** — Umberto Eco's advice (from *How to Write a Thesis*): a student wanting to write about volcanoes is told to narrow to active volcanoes in Mexico, then to just one — Popocatépetl — because zooming into the specific, emblematic case teaches you more than surveying everything. Wallace does this by narrowing "animal ethics" down to "the 2002 Maine Lobster Festival."

**Every paragraph needs a hook and a punchline** — the opening sentence should carry mystery, the closing sentence a subtextual "explosion." In Wallace's lobster paragraph, the innocuous phrase "sometimes each other" quietly plants the word *cannibal* without saying it.

**Maximalism as controlled variance, not randomness** — Wallace's 37-example paragraph works because he alternates general nouns ("the big parade") with hyper-specific ones ("the William D. Atwood memorial crate race") and mixes in numbers, creating rhythm through resolution-shifting rather than a flat list.

**Experience has three parts: biography, interiority, outlook** — biography is externally observable life (as if filmed); interiority is what only literature can render (inner monologue); outlook is a stated belief, but it only lands if earned by the biography and interiority that precede it — stating the belief first makes writing "preachy."

**Tension has two organizing models** — fiction/narrative essays run on intention → obstacle → consequence → climax (Orwell); idea-driven essays run on a hierarchy of implicit questions and answers (Wallace structures "Consider the Lobster" around one core question, three mid-level questions, and paragraph-level sub-questions never asked aloud).

**Sound is measurable even in nonsense** — Orwell's "football field," "referee," "laughter" share phonetic threads (F's, S's) that make "hideous laughter" land harder; every syllable breaks into onset/nucleus/coda, which is why "Snakes on a Plane" works phonetically (symmetrical "a" nucleus) even if it's weaker on title "mystery."

**"Practice analytically, perform intuitively"** — the essay's throughline maxim. The Grateful Dead look purely intuitive on stage, but Jerry Garcia drilled scales for 10 hours a day and Phil Lesh studied music theory; Jack Kerouac wrote a million plain, disciplined words before *On the Road*; Picasso mastered classical technique before inventing his own style.

**Feedback like a stand-up comedian tests a joke on a circuit** — Dean sends drafts to readers separately (so they can't see each other), collects granular reactions (highlight what you like, flag confusion), and overlays responses into a heat-mapped master document — dark green where multiple readers independently reacted to the same line, unmarked stretches signal cuts.

**AI's current strengths and blind spots** — good at structural compression (summarize a draft at different lengths), generating volume/possibilities, and matchmaking research; bad at synthesizing a long list of instructions into one coherent piece, at judging its own quality (it rated an AI-slop rewrite of *Paradise Lost* over the original), and at writing slowly/recursively — it "blazes forward" at ~400 words/minute with no ability to stop mid-piece and revise sentence two.

**The writer's apocalypse thought experiment** — pushed to the extreme (20 years out): AI writing quality becomes unimaginably good, it dominates mass taste and even parts of the literary canon, and personal "replicas" trained on your own logged memories could converse as you at scale. Dean's response: he tested whether he'd still write if all three came true, concluded yes, and treats that as proof his reasons for writing aren't threatened by the technology.

- Michael Dean: "Editing is not rewiring words, it's rewiring your synapses." (quoting S. Kelly Harrell)
- Michael Dean: "LLMs are amazing association machines, but what they don't really have is a true sense of judgment of quality."
- Michael Dean: "I don't think there's a limit to AI producing the funniest thing you've ever imagined... it could be extraterrestrially [good]."
- Michael Dean: "I'm not really interested in AI doing the writing or thinking for me. That's not really fun. I'm interested in finding the truth and writing something of quality."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Which of the three voice elements — spirit, sound, or sight — am I weakest in, and does that match Dean's claim that most people default to outlook/belief-stating and skip the biography/interiority groundwork?
- Am I "scope-ballooning" in my own drafts — trying to cover a whole topic instead of finding my own "one volcano"?
- Could I run a stripped-down version of Dean's comedian-style feedback loop (separate readers, no cross-visibility, granular reactions) on something I'm drafting?
- If I ran Dean's "writer's apocalypse" thought experiment on my own writing motives, would I still write if AI output became unimaginably good and personal replicas were common — and if not, what does that reveal about why I actually write?
- Do I have anything like Dean's "logging" practice — a low-stakes, no-feedback place to find my voice "when nobody is watching"?
