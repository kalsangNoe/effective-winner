## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Dan Wang, author of the annual China letters and the book *Breakneck*, about how he builds voice, texture, and insight into his writing. Wang's method rests on slow accumulation (one essay a year, notes dumped into Apple Notes all year, written in a hasty 10-day sprint), deliberate craft influences (Stendhal, Mozart's comic operas, copying New Yorker sentences the way he once copied Beethoven scores), and a refusal to let travel writing or history writing collapse into cliché or "just-so stories." The conversation also covers his critique of complacent American elites, China's propaganda and censorship apparatus, and his use of AI as a conversational "sparring partner" rather than a writer.

## Key ideas

**Writing process**
- **Annual deadline, not daily habit:** Wang commits to publishing his letter every January 1st (missed twice), so his whole year is organized around that single deadline rather than a frequent posting cadence.
- **Apple Notes as raw material:** He continuously dumps observations, overheard lines, and stray beautiful sentences into his phone's Notes app all year, then in the last ~10 days before deadline distills a "highly disorganized pile of notes" into a structure.
- **85% is the ceiling:** He accepts imperfection — "no writer would ever get above 85%" — citing conversations with New Yorker essayists.
- **Copying scores to learn craft:** As a former aspiring clarinetist, he used to copy out Beethoven quartets and Mahler symphonies by hand to internalize a composer's choices; he later did the same with New Yorker articles, retyping sentences to get inside a writer's intentionality.

**Style and influences**
- **Two touchstones:** Stendhal's novels (*The Red and the Black*, *The Charterhouse of Parma*) for humor and storyline, and Mozart's Italian comic operas (opera buffa, with librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte) for cadence, irony, and "ornament for its own sake."
- **Beethoven, not Mozart, as self-description:** Wang says his own writing process shows visible effort and struggle, closer to Beethoven's labored string quartets than Mozart's effortless perfection.
- **Texture over tectonic plates:** He deliberately avoids "big theme" China writing (US-vs-China, Thucydides trap) in favor of entry points like a bowl of soup in Kunming, mixing the formal/official China (party speeches, the magazine *Seeking Truth*) with the informal, messy daily life underneath it.

**Trade books vs. academic books**
- Both genres have structural pathologies: trade books risk being ghostwritten "airport books" chasing sales; academic books risk being written only to satisfy tenure requirements and defend a discipline rather than answer big questions. The best authors (he cites Stephen Kotkin's Stalin biography) transcend their genre's incentives.

**Travel writing and history**
- Most travel writing is self-indulgent and un-insightful because writers convey only their own ecstatic experience or a single expert's take, rather than triangulating data, literature, and multiple sources. His method in China: go without much agenda, walk between three or four meals a day, and let insight arrive through accumulated small observations (e.g., Chongqing's cliffside architecture, a subway running through an apartment building).
- His gripe with history books: they lapse into "just-so stories" that make outcomes feel inevitable in hindsight (Napoleon, Hitler, the fall of the USSR), when good historians hold onto contingency and counterfactuals.

**Critique of American elites**
- Being a self-described outsider (from Guangxi rather than Beijing/Shanghai, then Ottawa rather than the US) shaped his disappointment with elites at Yale Law, Stanford, and in the Biden administration, whom he sees as smart and credentialed but coasting on in-group networks rather than performing well.

**China's propaganda and censorship**
- Explains the CCP's three levers of Leninist rule: propaganda, the organization department (personnel/promotion), and coercion — with propaganda arguably primary, delivered via the nightly 7pm newscast and the biweekly magazine *Seeking Truth*.
- Censorship has intensified over the last 10–15 years to the point of eliminating independent journalism entirely; his own personal website was blocked in China in 2022 for unclear reasons, with no channel to appeal or ask why.
- A comedian's joke referencing the PLA in 2023 led to his detention and the shutdown of Shanghai's entire comedy-club scene for months — illustrating what Wang calls the state's inability to tolerate mockery.

**AI as a tool**
- He avoided AI while writing *Breakneck*, wanting sentences to "come from within myself," but now uses it heavily for music, art, and literary discussion — treating ChatGPT like an always-available version of his mentor Tyler Cowen for instant reactions to a novel or piece of music.

**On books and Substack**
- He's wary of Substack's tendency (like all publishing tech) to push writers toward higher frequency and shorter posts; he wanted to "bring down the average" by publishing once a year.
- Not everyone should write a book, but people with deep domain expertise who haven't should be pushed to.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I really just try to capture what was important about China in two respects... the incredible informality of general society [contrasted with] rigid, officious official China."
- "I think it is absolutely valid to try to construct an entire essay around a single beautiful sentence."
- "Every event feels kind of impossible before it takes place and then right after it takes place it feels obvious and necessary."
- "I don't think that there should be more books, but I do think that we need more books from the right people."
- "The way that I treat ChatGPT is as my friend Tyler, who I maybe see once every couple of months... if I really wanted to have an instant Tyler-like reaction to a novel that I've just read or a piece of music that I've just heard, I just ask."
- "No writer is ever very satisfied... I feel like I am perhaps 85% satisfied with my book."
- "This is just how insensitive the engineering state is. They don't understand a lot of things. They really don't want to have any jokes or mockery."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Could I run my own version of Wang's "notes app all year, one hard sprint" process instead of trying to write consistently?
- What's my equivalent of "copying the score" — a writer or work I could retype sentence-by-sentence to learn structural choices?
- Where in my own writing am I defaulting to "big tectonic plate" abstractions instead of finding a concrete entry point (a soup, a joke, a specific street)?
- Am I using AI as a genuine sparring partner for consumption/thinking, or am I letting its sentences leak into my own writing?
- What's the "just-so story" version of events I'm currently writing about, and what contingency am I flattening out?
