## TL;DR
Brandon Stanton, creator of Humans of New York, explains his craft as a compression of interviewing, photography, and editing that all serve one goal: getting past a stranger's rehearsed "persona" to their earned truth. His method is mechanical and repeatable — find the struggle, and you find the plot, the transformation, and the wisdom — but it only works because of total presence, zero preparation, and relentless daily repetition (four interviews a day, for years). He argues editing is really the discipline of finding what a story cannot do without, and that spoken, unpolished language carries more truth than "distilled" writing. Above all, he insists a writer must judge themselves only by the act of showing up, never by reception or talent, because that's the only variable actually in their control.

## Key ideas

- **Personas vs. persons.** Everyone opens an interview presenting a "business card version" of themselves — the story they've crafted to move through the world. Sustained attention and follow-up questions unpack that persona; truth "gets dug up one spoonful at a time," which is why it comes out slowly, with pauses.
- **Find the struggle, find everything else.** Stanton's core interviewing heuristic: "If you can find somebody's struggle, you will find a plot." A struggle also implies transformation (you can't fight something for a long time without being changed) and wisdom (the thing they've chewed on most is their genius).
- **"Following the heat."** He works with zero preparation and no notes — just curiosity and active listening. The "lean in" moment when he's surprised by something he's never heard before is his proxy for what the reader will feel, and it tells him where to dig.
- **Energy over technique.** He credits presence and intensity — not question lists — as the real engine of a good interview. He contrasts himself with journalists who show up with a fixed article structure already in their heads; his total presence is what "unlocks" people. He's a self-described intense, emotional interviewer: "No tears in the writer, no tears in the reader."
- **Voice vs. story.** Some subjects have singular events (great stories); some have a singular way of describing ordinary things (great voices, like Forrest Gump). When both combine — his example is "Tanqueray," a woman he interviewed for months — it's "absolute magic" and can justify going far longer than the usual short post.
- **Writing is editing toward singularity.** Clichés and generalities put a writer "in a pool of other people." Great writing is the sentence only one specific person could have written. He tests every interview against 10,000 prior ones: is this person telling him something he's genuinely never heard?
- **Plot needs a character who wants something.** Borrowing from Aaron Sorkin: plot is the clothesline, and everything else (exposition, poeticism, character) are the clothespins hanging off it. Without a character's need or desire, "there is no forward track" to carry the reader — and the desire can be as small as wanting your son to enjoy dinner.
- **The oral tradition shapes his sentences.** All his material arrives spoken, so he consciously writes with the rhythm, repetition, and lack of "ornamentation" of speech rather than the polished, distilled prose of the page — "I feel like I hear a story, I don't read a story."
- **Freed by the book from the algorithm.** 75% of the stories in his book *Dear New York* were never posted online; escaping the platform's incentives let him write shorter, more oblique, more poetic lines that wouldn't survive on social media, where success is measured by time-on-post.
- **Empathy as counterfactual, not identification.** His formulation: empathy isn't "I am like this person," it's "if I had lived their exact inputs, I would likely be like them" — which is why he foregrounds backstory (a trailer, addicted parents) rather than surface judgment.
- **Discipline as identity, not talent.** After flunking out of college, he rebuilt his self-worth around effort rather than ability — reading 100 pages a day, then photographing 30–40 people a day, then doing four interviews a day, without missing a day, for years. The *Dear New York* prologue went through 180 drafts.
- **Judge yourself only by showing up.** His closing creed: "If you wrote for an hour a day, you're a writer... if you've won Pulitzer Prizes but you haven't written in a year, there's a 16-year-old girl writing in her journal that is more of a writer than you are." Effort is the only variable fully within a writer's control.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Truth is often spoken haltingly, with pauses, like it's being dug up one spoonful at a time from somewhere deep. Truth feels heavy. It has gravity. It's usually not floating on the surface."
- "If you can find somebody's struggle, you will find a plot... find what this person has overcome, and you will have a story with a plot. Then you will also have transformation."
- "My stripper name was Tanqueray. Back in the 70s I was the only black girl making white girl money." (quoting his subject, on why a singular voice can carry a story in ten words)
- "It's the people who have so much to say, but none of it is very deeply earned... I would much rather take the insecure person over every single time, because that is the person who is not so puffed up that they've created a false version of themselves."
- "When you start to view the tools of writing as weapons that you have to create a singular expression, that's very freeing... anything is allowed if you do it well."
- "You have to root it in the doing of it... If you wrote for an hour a day, you're a writer. Congratulations... It's the process of doing it. It's the only thing I've ever been able to judge myself on because that's the only thing that I know 100% positive I can control."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I still leaning on a "persona" version of the material — the tidy, pre-packaged version — instead of digging for the spoonful-at-a-time truth underneath?
- What's the equivalent of "following the heat" for written (not interviewed) material — how do I notice, mid-draft, that I've hit something surprising rather than something rehearsed?
- Do my pieces have a Sorkin-style clothesline — a character with a clear want — or am I hanging good sentences on nothing and hoping tone carries the reader through?
- Am I judging my writing practice by output/reception, or by the more controllable metric of whether I showed up and did the work today?
- What would it look like to deliberately write in a more spoken, repetitive, "unornamented" register instead of always reaching for the most distilled version of a sentence?
