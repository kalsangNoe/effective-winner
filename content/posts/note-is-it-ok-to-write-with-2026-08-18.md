## TL;DR
David Perell compiles 12 short interviews with writers, journalists, and builders answering whether AI is good or bad for writing, and the answers split less along "pro/anti-AI" lines than along what part of the process each person treats as sacred. Ezra Klein, Yann Martel, and Robert Macfarlane guard the *grappling* — the slow, embodied time spent researching, feeling, or crafting sentences — as the irreplaceable source of insight and soul. Tyler Cowen, Jimmy Soni, and Marc Andreessen treat AI as an accelerant for the "middle" work (research, editing, pushback, prep) while insisting the vision and final judgment stay human. A third group (Sam Altman, Maria Popova, Steven Pinker) focuses on structural and legal consequences: copyright doesn't cover AI-generated text, generic writing dies first, and readers will keep wanting a human name on the book even if AI could write it better.

## Key ideas

- **Ezra Klein — outsourcing the wrong part.** He uses AI as a Google replacement but refuses to let it summarize a book or paper for him, because "it's the time I spent researching that was the most important time." His fear: AI is better at the parts of writing he *doesn't* need help with than the parts he does.
- **Maria Popova — the "matrix theory of the mind" is wrong.** She used to imagine knowledge as something you could download (like plugging a cable into your brain in *The Matrix*). Now she thinks understanding only comes from "grappling with the text, making connections" — and that process can't be speedrun or summarized away. "What knowledge is supposed to do is change you."
- **Tyler Cowen — AI as the new secondary literature.** He doesn't let AI write for him, but uses it heavily when *reading* — interrogating LLMs about history books instead of buying 20-30 secondary sources, and asking questions chapter-by-chapter about novels like *Wuthering Heights*. His critique of bad AI users: they "ask it questions that are too general" and don't put in enough of their own time generating context. He predicts generic corporate writing dies first, but biography and memoir survive because readers want a real life behind them.
- **Tom Junod — art requires suffering, AI can't fail.** "AI will never write the great American poem... because it hasn't suffered... even if you try to make it suffer, meaning write a command that is to execute failure, it'll already be succeeding at executing failure." He separates intellectual change (which AI can deliver) from emotional change (which requires "feeling and time," which AI has neither of).
- **Sam Altman — mostly declines to use AI for prose, but leans on it for background.** He asked "01 pro" for legal context on a column about declassified documents, used what he learned but not the AI's actual words: "I don't let it write for me... it's like my little baby."
- **Steven Pinker — AI-composite prose is smooth but soulless.** LLM output resembles a morphed composite face — individually bland inputs averaging into something "not beautiful, but clear." He credits reinforcement learning and human fine-tuners (not the raw training data) for why AI prose reads as competent, unlike most human bureaucratic writing.
- **Jimmy Soni — AI as sharpest knife, not the cook.** He uses Claude as an always-on editor and as an adversarial critic ("write a takedown of this op-ed... do not hold back") to find blind spots. He invokes a framing from Aravind Srinivas: AI is "not end to end, it's middle to middle" — humans set the vision and verify the output, AI fills the middle. He also credits AI (via Whisper Flow dictation) with lowering the anxiety barrier to a bad first draft.
- **Riva Tez — AI lacks embodied, diverse intelligence.** She rejects the premise that language-model fluency equals general intelligence, comparing it to a spider's web-building or bird murmuration — different, non-human forms of intelligence that get erased by anthropomorphizing chatbots. She warns synthetic biology is a scarier existential risk than "a giant language calculator."
- **Yann Martel — refuses AI writing outright.** "Why would I? It'd be like hiring someone to have sex for you." He'll use AI for non-creative tasks (a partner's legal paperwork) but wants imperfection and human connection in art, and predicts audiences will tire of AI-generated creative work even if it's technically good.
- **Marc Andreessen — AI as accelerant, not author; a wedding-toast betrayal story.** He describes learning a heartfelt wedding note was AI-written and feeling it as "a violation and a betrayal" — sincerity, even flawed, beats polished insincerity. He predicts writing (like coding) is shifting to a continuous human-AI dialogue akin to pair programming, with humans staying "in charge of the overall product."
- **Robert Macfarlane — only uses AI as spellcheck, and resents even that.** His prose deliberately violates grammatical norms (he demonstrates by reading a verbless, five-paragraph description of a river), and Grammarly's disapproval is "a badge of success." His view: tools like Grammarly make average writing better but "hurt a kind of write" — flattening the distinctiveness that underlies great writing. He calls for resisting AI's use in writing partly because of unlicensed use of authors' books in training data.
- **Jon Yaged (publisher) — copyright is the practical fault line.** US copyright law doesn't protect AI-generated content, so a fully AI-written book has "zero barrier to entry" — anyone could republish it. Publishing contracts already require authors to represent that a work is "their sole creation" and disclose borrowed material (quotes, lyrics); he expects that framework to extend to AI use rather than be replaced. His practical advice: using AI as a research assistant on material you already have rights to is "Google on steroids" and fine; the ambiguity is at the level of prose generation.

## Quotes worth keeping
- Ezra Klein: "It's just it's very hard to not be outsourcing the part of the work I need to do the most. AI is better at the things I need to do than the things I don't need to do."
- Maria Popova: "What knowledge is supposed to do is change you. And it changes you because you make connections to it."
- Tom Junod: "It will never know what it's like to collide with its own impossibility. So AI can only ever succeed. And without suffering, what kind of true art can there be?"
- Tyler Cowen: "A lot of people who thought they would be writers, I will predict they won't be. Just like the number of jobs for certain types of computer programmers is plummeting."
- Jimmy Soni, quoting a friend: "AI is the sharpest knife that an engineer has ever been given... but it's still a knife. I've still got to cook the meal."
- Yann Martel: "Why would I? It'd be like hiring someone to have sex for you."
- Marc Andreessen (on learning the wedding note was AI-written): "I would have so much rather you written something that's half as sincere as that, filled and littered with typos, than to have AI write that. It felt like a violation and a betrayal."
- Robert Macfarlane: "Grammarly does not approve, which I take to be a badge of success... it makes the average piece of writing much better, but it hurts a kind of write."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own process is the "grappling" (Klein/Popova's term) actually doing the work — and am I at risk of letting AI quietly take that part over because it's the tedious part, not because it's the unimportant part?
- Do I treat AI as "middle to middle" (Soni/Srinivas framing) — vision and verification staying mine — or have I let it creep into either end of that spectrum without noticing?
- If copyright genuinely doesn't protect AI-generated prose (per Yaged), does that change how I think about publishing anything I didn't substantially write myself?
- Would I feel betrayed, like Andreessen at the wedding, if I learned something addressed to me personally was AI-written — and does that tell me anything about what I owe readers of my own writing?
- Macfarlane's claim that tools like Grammarly "hurt a kind of write" by flattening distinctiveness — is there a version of that same flattening happening in how I use AI for my own sentences?
