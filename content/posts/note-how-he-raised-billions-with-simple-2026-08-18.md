## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Howard Marks (co-founder of Oaktree Capital, ~$190 billion AUM) about how he writes his famous investment memos, which have made him Warren Buffett's favorite author. Marks' core argument is that clarity in writing is a discipline of thought, not decoration: if you can't explain an idea simply, you probably don't understand it well enough, or you're using jargon to erect a professional "barrier" between yourself and the reader. He walks through his actual process — collecting unconnected ideas over long stretches, outlining, drafting, then repeatedly editing for diminishing but real returns — and argues that his memos endure not because they chase timeliness but because they wrestle with recurring, hard-to-reconcile questions (like what makes something "mispriced") rather than restating what everyone already knows.

## Key ideas
- **Writing should teach the reader something new, or it's not worth publishing.** Marks says if a piece "squares with what people already know, then I'm not contributing much" — his test for a good reader response is "I've told them something they didn't know."
- **No fixed writing schedule.** He writes "less than quarterly," with no self-imposed requirement, and gets "Are you dead?" emails when he goes quiet — he writes only when he has something to say.
- **Ideas come from serendipitous connections, not scheduled research.** His mind links "unconnected things which produce inspirations" — an article on one pile, an unrelated one on another, until they connect (he traces this directly to how the "Getting Lucky" memo came together from a tweet/article he read in a Riyadh hotel room).
- **Giving away his "secrets" isn't risky, because insight isn't the bottleneck — implementation is.** He argues most people who read what's important still fail to act on it, so sharing ideas openly doesn't threaten his edge.
- **His editing process has diminishing but genuine returns.** First pass gets you far, each subsequent pass makes it "10% better," until further editing "can't really make it any better" — but he keeps refining because he enjoys it.
- **He crowdsources nitpicking.** Borrowing the idea of "irregulars" (a term from a New York Times writer describing his ragtag band of readers), Marks has four or five trusted people review drafts for both substance and style (down to hyphen and comma preferences).
- **His writing goal is explicitly anti-jargon.** He calls professional jargon a way for "certain professionals" to "reinforce the barrier between themselves" and their audience, and rejects that: "You're a person. We're both people."
- **Two Wharton literature courses under a professor (Saunders, an "Oxford don" type) permanently changed his writing** — heavy red-pen edits (marking passages "REP" for repetitive, "RED" for redundant) turned him from an indifferent to a strong writer starting around 1965, though he didn't put it to real use until starting his memos in 1990.
- **He advised his son (entering the business) that quality is the one lever fully in your control.** Since so much in investing/business is outside your control, "you should have a simple goal" — do the highest-quality work you can, and evaluate yourself by that standard, not by outcomes.
- **He thinks visually and uses vivid analogies to distill ideas** — e.g., comparing the economy to a beach ball held underwater by Fed stimulus (release it, and it snaps back), and admiring Buffett's "you only find out who's swimming naked when the tide goes out" for the same reason: vivid images compress complex ideas.
- **A memo's durability, not its topicality, is what he values.** Rereading old memos and still agreeing with them convinces him an idea is "not a fad" — a topic like "what does it mean for something to be mispriced" can be revisited for decades because it's a genuinely hard, recurring reconciliation, not a one-off take on the news cycle.
- **The essence of good investment writing is "reconciliation"** — finding the one incongruency between two things that are supposed to fit together (e.g., what you learned as theory in school vs. what actually happens in markets) and explaining why they diverge. He frames this via a Yogi Berra line: "in theory there's no difference between theory and practice, but in practice there is."
- **He credits Peter Bernstein as his greatest hero/influence** — an "investment philosopher," author of a major history of probability, whose ideas he built directly into his own risk memos ("Risk," 2006; "Can We Reduce Risk to a Number," etc.), sometimes literally quoting Bernstein in italics within his own memos to mark the borrowed content.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "If I'm writing a book and it squares with what people already know, then I'm not contributing much."
- "I don't have a requirement to write... I write when I feel like it, and I tend to get emails saying, 'Are you dead?'"
- "I think being a logical, literal person, and the inference is that my writing is simple."
- "A profession is a conspiracy against the laity... certain professionals want to reinforce the barrier between themselves and the person, and I'm not interested in that. You're a person. We're both people."
- "The first time you do it, you make it good. Then you make it 10% better than that. And then you can't really make it any better."
- "For the simplicity on this side of complexity, I wouldn't give you a nickel. But for the simplicity on the far side of complexity, I'd give you anything I have." (Oliver Wendell Holmes line Marks cites as his standard.)
- "In theory there's no difference between theory and practice, but in practice there is." (Yogi Berra, used as Marks' framework for what makes writing worth doing.)
- "Quotes and adages and books wouldn't remain if they weren't valuable... why reinvent the wheel in an inferior way?"

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I using jargon as a "barrier" rather than because it's genuinely the clearest word available?
- Do I have a Marks-style "reconciliation" test — am I only writing about things that resolve a real tension, or am I restating consensus?
- What would my equivalent of "irregulars" look like — a small trusted group who nitpick both substance and style before I publish?
- Am I editing to real diminishing returns, or stopping either too early (leaving it rough) or too late (polishing past the point of improvement)?
- Could I trace one of my own ideas back to its origin the way Marks traces "Getting Lucky" to a specific article in a specific hotel room — and would writing that origin story make the idea land better?
