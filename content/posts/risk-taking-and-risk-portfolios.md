Contrary to the "fearless founder" myth, the people who successfully champion original ideas are usually more risk-averse than assumed — they balance big bets in one domain with deliberate caution in others, rather than going all-in everywhere.

## Key Concepts
- **Entrepreneurs are more risk-averse than the general population**, not less — this holds across income, age, gender, and experience, and across multiple independent studies.
- **Keeping a day job while building the risky thing outperforms quitting.** Entrepreneurs who kept their day jobs had 33% lower odds of failure than those who went all-in immediately. Pattern held for Phil Knight (Nike), Steve Wozniak (Apple), Larry Page & Sergey Brin (Google), Sara Blakely (Spanx), Henry Ford, and Bill Gates (who took a formal leave of absence, not a dropout).
- **The "balanced risk portfolio" model** (from psychologist Clyde Coombs): take extreme risk in one domain, offset it with extreme caution in another — not moderate risk everywhere. Financial security in one area buys the freedom to be original in another.
- **De-risking mechanisms compound original bets.** Warby Parker's free home try-on program is a case study: it let the founders test an unproven assumption (people will buy glasses online) cheaply before committing fully.
- **Risk portfolios explain within-person paradoxes** — Branch Rickey broke baseball's color line but refused to work Sundays or drink; T.S. Eliot published *The Waste Land* but kept his bank job for three more years.

## Details
The mechanism isn't caution for its own sake — it's sequencing. A stable base (day job, savings, an unrelated safe commitment) removes the pressure to rush a half-baked version of the risky idea to market, and removes the psychological tax of "everything depends on this." This is also why strategic delay pairs naturally with risk portfolios: security buys the time needed for [[Timing & Strategic Procrastination|patient timing]] to work in your favor rather than against you.

The same logic shows up at the family level: laterborn children who take outsized physical risks (see [[Parenting for Originality]]) are often shielded by an older sibling's presence, which functions as its own kind of safety net enabling risk elsewhere.
