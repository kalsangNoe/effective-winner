## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Mark Forsyth, author of *The Elements of Eloquence*, about the ancient figures of rhetoric — named formulas (chiasmus, anaphora, diacope, progressio, etc.) — that make lines of writing memorable. Forsyth's core claim: memorability isn't about talent or inspiration, it's mechanical — these are 2,500-year-old formulas anyone can learn and apply, and they show up everywhere from the Bible to Katy Perry to JFK to advertising. He argues modern writing education abandoned rhetoric because Romanticism cast writing as pure passion rather than craft, but the greatest passionate writers (MLK, Shakespeare, the Beatles) used these devices constantly, often deliberately. The names don't matter; the patterns do.

## Key ideas
- **Progressio (long antithesis chains) signals "pay attention."** Katy Perry's "Hot n Cold," Dickens's "it was the best of times, it was the worst of times," and Ecclesiastes' "a time to weep and a time to rejoice" all use repeated back-and-forth opposites. Forsyth compares it to a soccer commentator's voice rising before a goal — the rhetorical "fire building" cues the reader to lean in.
- **Diacope (a verbal sandwich) makes single lines unforgettable.** "A horse, a horse, my kingdom for a horse" (Richard III); "Free at last, free at last, thank God Almighty, we are free at last" (MLK). It's so powerful it creates false memories — everyone remembers the Wicked Witch saying "Fly, my pretties, fly!" though the film only has her say "Fly, fly, fly, fly."
- **Chiasmus (mirror-reversal) reads as instantly true.** "Ask not what your country can do for you, ask what you can do for your country" (JFK); "with money on my mind and my mind on my money" (Coolio); "if we cannot bring our enemies to justice, we must bring justice to our enemies" (George W. Bush). Forsyth: humans love symmetry the way we love the Taj Mahal, so a symmetrical sentence makes the brain say "of course that must be right."
- **Anaphora/epistrophe (repeated sentence starts/ends) builds a list into a drumbeat.** Churchill's "we will fight them on the beaches... in the fields... in the cities" (anaphora); Obama's "yes we can" (epistrophe).
- **The periodic sentence delays the main verb to build suspense.** Kipling's "If—": a long chain of "if" clauses before the payoff, "then you'll be a man, my son."
- **Anadiplosis (last word of one clause opens the next) chains ideas together.** "Fear leads to hate, hate leads to anger, anger leads to the dark side" (Yoda). Forsyth cites Paul McCartney's manuscript for "Here, There and Everywhere" — McCartney wrote the lyrics, then went back and deliberately inserted the anadiplosis, proof the effect can be engineered after the fact even without knowing its name.
- **Rule of three (tricolon) makes lists feel complete.** "I came, I saw, I conquered"; "any time, any place, anywhere" (even though "any place" and "anywhere" mean the same thing); Blood, Sweat, Toil and Tears — misremembered as just "Blood, Sweat and Tears" because three is the shape our memory wants.
- **Concision isn't always the goal — specificity is.** Forsyth pushes back on "quick and clear" business-writing advice: saying "everybody" is too concise to make a reader stop and think, but "black men and white men, Jews and Gentiles, Protestants and Catholics" (MLK) forces the reader to dwell on each pair, dramatizing the fullness of the idea.
- **Establishing your voice early is like an establishing shot in a film.** A reader has no tone of voice, face, or gestures to go on — only the words. Forsyth's example: he read a "dull" novel until he hit the word "drugstore" and realized the writer was American, which retroactively made the prose come alive. Changing "a man was walking down the street" to "a chap was walking down the street" (English/posh) vs. "a dude" (American) sets the reader's ear for the rest of the piece within the first paragraph.
- **Shakespeare was "a wonderful thief."** His famous barge passage in *Antony and Cleopatra* is lifted almost directly from a Plutarch translation — Forsyth says all Shakespeare really added was meter and alliteration ("full fathom five thy father lies" vs. the flat "your father is 9.8m underwater").
- **Forsyth's own process: practice separately from writing, then write fast.** He compares it to a jazz musician drilling scales for years before improvising on stage — "the improvisation is coming off a very well-trodden head." His personal drill for meter is walking down the street improvising nonsense in iambic pentameter ("the chair is on the carpet in the room"). He writes fast because "if it flows out of you, it will flow into the reader," and edits minimally — if a sentence reads wrong, he deletes and rewrites the whole thing rather than tinkering, comparing it to a long-jumper who fails a jump and goes all the way back to the start of the runway rather than adjusting mid-run.
- **He doesn't memorize the Latin/Greek names — he built himself a cheat sheet.** "None of these names — anadiplosis, chiasmus — mean nothing to me... I came up with little names for myself... the technical names do not matter at all, and indeed the ancient Greeks couldn't even agree on half of these technical names."

## Quotes worth keeping
- Forsyth: "There's something deep in our souls which longs for this kind of beauty in these memorable, measured, stately lines."
- Forsyth on MLK: "He could just have said 'everybody,' but instead he said black and white, Jews and Gentiles, Protestant Catholic — and that really makes you stop and think."
- Forsyth: "Our minds have memory receptors in certain shapes... there's these little rhetorical tactics we can do to basically shape our words and ideas so that they fit into the slot of the human brain." (Perell's framing, which Forsyth calls "exactly it.")
- Forsyth on Shakespeare: "He's sort of like a thief who steals your socks and then darns them — breaks into your house and tidies it up."
- Forsyth on editing: "Editing I find can kind of kill it... if it reads wrong, the best thing is delete the whole thing and go again... you run up, you do the jump, you fail, go all the way back, run up again."
- Forsyth: "The technical names do not matter at all — it's the formulas and the tactics that really make the difference."
- Perell, closing on Seinfeld's NYT intro: "It's an odyssey, it's a roller coaster, and it's a series of setting expectations and immediately breaking them."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Which two or three rhetorical formulas (progressio, chiasmus, diacope, tricolon) could I deliberately drill into my own writing the way Forsyth drills iambic pentameter on walks?
- Am I defaulting to "concise and clear" in places where dwelling — naming the specific pairs instead of saying "everything" — would actually land harder?
- What's the "establishing shot" of my own writing voice — does my first paragraph give the reader enough to hear my tone before I need it to matter?
- Do I over-edit sentence by sentence instead of trusting the first fast pass and rewriting wholesale when something's off?
- Where in my own drafts is there a McCartney moment available — a line I could go back and deliberately re-shape into anadiplosis, chiasmus, or a sandwich structure after the fact?
