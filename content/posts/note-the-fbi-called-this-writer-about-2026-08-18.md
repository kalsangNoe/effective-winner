## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Neil Strauss (author of *The Game*, ghostwriter/profiler for Kevin Hart, Jenna Jameson, and others) about the craft of writing honestly and interestingly. Strauss's core argument: vulnerability alone isn't good writing — you have to start from "nobody cares," hook the reader immediately, and then earn interest through specificity, editing, and structure, not raw confession. He walks through his own methods: a "vomit draft" written only for yourself, a second draft written for the reader, and only then bringing in "the critic." He illustrates the whole approach using the real opening of his podcast *To Die For* — a call he got from the FBI in 2011 asking him to train intelligence agents in seduction.

## Key ideas
- **Start from "nobody cares."** Strauss's writing philosophy begins with the premise that readers don't care about you or your life by default — your job as a writer is to earn that attention line by line, not assume it.
- **The three-draft method.** Draft one is the "vomit draft" — write it assuming no one will ever see it, so you can say the unsayable. Draft two is written for the reader: you go back and make the vulnerable material *interesting*, not just raw. Only in draft three do you bring in "the critic" — worrying about accuracy, legal risk, or what people (like Andrew Huberman, in his example) might say.
- **Vulnerability isn't enough on its own.** Strauss says many writers mistake "vomiting half-baked diary entries" for vulnerability. The real skill is being vulnerable *in a way that's interesting* — turning a private thing into insight or story, not just disclosure.
- **The FBI story as a live case study.** In June 2011, Strauss got a call from someone wanting to fly him to Washington, DC to train "several dozen intelligence agents." When Strauss asked "train them in what?", the answer was "seduction." He'd written *The Game*, about the pickup-artist subculture, and the FBI wanted him to teach rapport and attraction techniques used in recruiting spies and confidential informants. He uses this exact anecdote as the cold open of his podcast *To Die For* (about a Russian seduction spy) to demonstrate pacing and hooks in practice.
- **Writing for audio vs. page.** He describes literally engineering pauses after lines like "In 2011..." before "I received a call from the FBI" — one piece of information at a time, so "the mind" has to lean in, plus deliberate music cues (a "slow staircase," then a "drop") to control tension the way editing controls prose.
- **The house/room metaphor for openings.** A good opening isn't a full explanation — it's getting someone through the front door. You want just enough intrigue ("that next room's probably going to be cool") to pull the reader further in, not to cram the whole idea into the first sentence.
- **Famous first lines share one job: disorientation that pulls you in.** He cites Orwell's *1984* ("the clocks were striking thirteen"), Camus's *The Stranger* ("Mother died today. Or maybe yesterday; I can't be sure"), and Zora Neale Hurston's *Their Eyes Were Watching God* — each creates a small unresolved question the reader needs answered.
- **Keep openings crisp; save decoration for later.** A common student mistake is loading up the first lines with adjectives and description before establishing why the scene matters — "inflating a moment with so much unnecessary air."
- **World-building and consistency.** The first chapter or first paragraphs set rules the reader unconsciously absorbs — tone, how much they'll be told, whether the writing is playful or serious. Breaking those rules later (skipping steps, jarring shifts) knocks the reader out of the story.
- **The brutal edit exercise.** In his writer's workshop, Strauss has people take their best chapter, cut out everything except what's essential, then compare it to the original — often the stripped version is stronger. He connects this to Elon Musk's principle of ruthlessly deleting parts before deciding you actually need to add them back, and to musicians who record 20 versions of a song and cut down rather than build up.
- **Never delete — archive.** He keeps everything he cuts (including material later reused as "bonuses" for readers who order the book), because a cut passage should be treated as removable, not destroyed — this makes writers less precious and more willing to cut.
- **Being editable is a skill, distinct from being a good editor.** He describes learning early (at the Village Voice) to actually absorb notes rather than defend the work, and stresses that writer and editor share the same goal — get the piece published well — so disagreements are usually about whether the editor's *specific solution* is right, not whether the underlying problem they flagged is real.
- **Deadlines and constraints beat waiting for inspiration.** He credits years of forced weekly deadlines (with "nothing to write about") for producing some of his best work, and argues writer's block is really "performance pressure" — he opens his teaching syllabus with a three-minute free-write with no goal, to prove people can write without needing an idea first.
- **Notice things immediately, before memory reshapes them.** He journals within 24 hours because memory is "plastic" — it changes every time you access it. He cites Rick Rubin's habit of jotting a few words in the moment (as a mnemonic peg back to the emotion) rather than transcribing everything live, since taking notes during a real conversation breaks the connection that makes the material worth writing about.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own drafts am I skipping the "vomit draft" and jumping straight to a polished-but-guarded version — and is that costing me honesty or just costing me a rewrite step?
- Do my openings actually withhold information the way Strauss's FBI story does, or do I front-load context out of a fear of confusing the reader?
- What have I permanently deleted from a piece that I should have archived instead — and would keeping it change how freely I cut in the future?
- Am I treating "being interesting" and "being vulnerable" as the same move, when Strauss argues they're two separate crafts that both need deliberate work?
- Do I have a Strauss-style discipline for capturing observations within 24 hours, or am I relying on memory that's quietly reshaping the material before I ever write it down?
