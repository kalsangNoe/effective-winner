## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Morgan Housel (Psychology of Money, The Art of Spending Money) about how he collects and tells stories. Housel's core claim is that good writing isn't about new ideas — almost nothing he writes is original — it's about compression, hooks, and knowing when you've lost the reader. He describes a deliberately unstructured creative process (no scheduling, wide reading funnel, obsessive note-taking), a hard-won shift from writing for an audience to "selfish writing," and a growing reverence for prose style itself, sometimes over the quality of the underlying ideas. He's skeptical of LLMs as a writing aid but uses them heavily as a research tool.

## Key ideas

- **No points for difficulty.** If a reader has to reread a paragraph to get it, that's the author's fault, not the reader's. Contrast: philosophers who are deep but hard to read vs. Erik Larson, who tells in a page and a half what other historians take 17–46 pages to say.
- **Get to the point, always, but more so today.** Fifty years ago a boring book was still the only entertainment in the room. Now readers have a phone in their pocket, so the tolerance for slack writing has collapsed. Mark Twain tested this by reading aloud to his family and cutting wherever he saw them lean back.
- **Collecting stories is the actual job.** Housel keeps "several short stories" posts as a dumping ground for anecdotes that don't fit anywhere else. His rule: "the person who says the most in the fewest words wins" — a great story can be three lines.
- **Hooks over data dumps.** Compare "wealthy families go broke sometimes" to "the Vanderbilts had the equivalent of $300 billion and within 60 years had basically no money." Specificity and a concrete number does the hooking. He applies the same logic to why individual stories generate empathy while statistics about groups generate apathy (riffing on the Stalin "one death is a tragedy" line).
- **ChatGPT: great research tool, useless/undesired as a writing aid.** He uses it constantly (roughly hourly) for research — "Google on steroids" — but deliberately refuses to let it draft or structure his prose, because the act of writing a sentence is what generates the next thought: "if you're using any kind of LLM to do any of the even the broad structure for you, you're not actually thinking." He also says it's surprisingly bad at catching typos/grammar.
- **Wide funnel, tight filter (borrowed from Patrick O'Shaughnessy).** Start almost any book that seems 1% interesting (use free Kindle samples), but be merciless about abandoning it if it's not working — he finishes only a "single digit percentage" of books he starts. Not finishing a bad-fit book isn't a character flaw.
- **Style comes from reps + feedback, not talent.** Writing ~4,000 blog posts with open comment sections gave him constant signal on what worked, even though the comments were often vicious ("bad comment, bad comment today," his wife would say reading his face at the door).
- **Selfish writing came later, as its own era.** Early career = read every comment and adjust. Later insight: you can't please everyone, so pick one audience to please — himself. His best work came when he stopped thinking about the reader entirely.
- **Compression is the real craft, not length.** He criticizes the "page minimum" school of writing education for teaching kids to pad. His example of true concision at length: Doris Kearns Goodwin's ~800-page *No Ordinary Time*, where "not a single word can be stripped," versus 500-word essays where 450 words didn't need to exist — concise doesn't mean short.
- **Books are the Super Bowl.** Social media = spring training, blogging = regular season, books = the Super Bowl — stakes are permanent (a bad tweet is forgotten; a bad book follows you). He uses Bill Buckner's World Series error as the analogy for how visible a fumble becomes at that level.
- **You don't need new ideas — you need to say them better.** He describes real shame turning in the *Psychology of Money* manuscript because nothing in it was original. His resolution: originality doesn't matter if you "said it in a different way." He puts Ken Burns, Yuval Harari, and Nassim Taleb in this camp — Burns famously edits scripts so a musical beat lands on a specific word.
- **Ideas that are good are easy to write; ideas that are bad cause writer's block.** His proudest posts took a couple of hours; the ones he labored over for a week and disappointed him were the ones with a flawed underlying idea.
- **Observation is trained, not innate.** A friend's tip: play "I Spy" alone in public — name 20 things you notice — to build the habit of observation. He also constantly interrupts conversations to jot down things people say, a habit from the last 5–10 years.
- **Psychology of Money was rejected by every US publisher** and was only picked up by the British publisher Harriman House. He holds no grudge — writing success is inherently unpredictable ("you really have no idea what's going to work").

## Quotes worth keeping
- "There's no points awarded for difficulty in writing... if you have to reread the paragraph four times because it's deep writing, it's not bedtime writing. That's the author's fault." — Morgan Housel
- "Just get to the point. Get to the freaking point. That has always been the case, but it's way more today." — David Perell (opening line)
- "It's always the case that the best story wins... it's always too that the person who says the most in the fewest words wins." — Morgan Housel
- "If you're using any kind of LLM to do any of the even the broad structure for you, you're not actually thinking. And so you stripped out everything that was good about writing." — Morgan Housel
- "I think most people who say they're not a good reader... it's because they force themselves to read bad books... a lot of books are not right for me and not right for you." — Morgan Housel
- "Concise doesn't necessarily mean short... You can write an 800-page book but it's just lack of fluff." — Morgan Housel, on Doris Kearns Goodwin's *No Ordinary Time*
- "You don't need to say something new. You just need to say it well." — Morgan Housel
- "Every time that I felt like I had a good idea, it was easy to write. And every time I got writer's block, what actually happened was the idea sucked." — Morgan Housel

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I rewarding myself for difficulty instead of clarity — passages I'm proud of that actually just made the reader work?
- Do I have a "wide funnel, tight filter" system for input (reading, videos, sources), or do I force myself through things out of obligation?
- Am I still writing for an imagined audience's approval, or have I found my own version of "selfish writing" — a piece I'd be proud of even if no one liked it?
- When I get stuck on a piece, is it actually a sentence-level problem, or — per Housel's theory — a sign the underlying idea is weak?
- What's my equivalent of "I Spy" for building observational material to write about, outside of scheduled work sessions?
