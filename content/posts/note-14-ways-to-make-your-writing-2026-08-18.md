## TL;DR
Ward Farnsworth (law professor, author of *Classical English Rhetoric* and *Classical English Style*) walks David Perell through three rhetorical tools — Saxon vs. Latinate diction, anaphora/epistrophe, and chiasmus — using 14 famous examples from the King James Bible, Churchill, Lincoln, Holmes, JFK, and others. His core claim: memorable prose isn't produced by simplicity alone, but by **contrast** — mixing plain Germanic (Saxon) words against fancier Latin/French ones, or alternating rhetorical patterns, so the ear is struck by the change. The techniques are old and mostly unconscious in great writers, who absorbed them through deep immersion (Lincoln read the Bible and Shakespeare obsessively) rather than deliberate imitation. Farnsworth's practical advice: use these devices sparingly, write naturally in your own voice, and study rhetoric partly to inoculate yourself against being fooled by people who use it to make false things sound true.

## Key ideas

**1. English has two vocabularies, and that's the whole game.** English fused Old German (Saxon) with French/Latin after the Norman invasion, so almost every important word has a plain version and a fancy version: *make* vs. *create*, *get* vs. *acquire*, *let* vs. *permit*. Saxon words are shorter, older, and "sound different" — often unable to take Latinate suffixes like *-tion* (you can't say "getion" for *acquisition*).

**2. Rule one: prefer Saxon words — they hit the gut, not just the mind.** Latinate words (*execute*, *terminate*) feel polite/formal; Saxon words (*kill*) feel blunt and visceral. Genesis 1:3 ("And God said, let there be light: and there was light") is 11 Saxon words and nothing else — Farnsworth argues that's why it still lands as strength and power rather than needing "fancier" language.

**3. But the real power is in contrast, not purity.** Churchill's "we shall fight on the beaches..." runs 32 Saxon words in a row — rare and deliberate (Churchill wrote explicitly that old Saxon words "strike deepest"). "Never in the field of human conflict was so much owed by so many to so few" opens Latinate (*human conflict*) and lands Saxon (*owed... so few*) — the fancy setup primes the ear to be struck by the plain finish. Farnsworth compares this to a chord change in music: the interest is in the shift, not either pole alone.

**4. Start-at-the-end is the master placement rule.** The beginning and end of a sentence are the positions that "ring in the ear" — but the end matters most because it's the last thing heard. Great rhetorical endings are almost always Saxon and short, even when the sentence opened Latinate (e.g., Holmes: "...but freedom for the thought that we hate").

**5. Lincoln often says the same thing twice, once in each language.** "I do not expect the union to be dissolved [Latinate] ... I do not expect the house to fall [Saxon]." He restates ideas once conceptually (appeals to the mind) and once plainly (appeals to the gut) — Farnsworth calls it "speaking two languages" to reach two different capacities in the listener.

**6. Anaphora vs. epistrophe — repeat at the start, or repeat at the end.** *Anaphora*: repeating words at the start of successive clauses ("we shall fight... we shall fight..."). *Epistrophe*: repeating at the end ("...what we say here... what they did here" — Gettysburg Address; "of the people, by the people, for the people"). Combining or reversing between the two (epistrophe into anaphora, as in Churchill's "giving all, daring all, enduring all... to the utmost, to the end") creates the same contrast effect as switching vocabularies.

**7. Chiasmus (A-B-B-A) works for reversal, backwards relationships, and mismatches.** JFK's "ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" is a literal chiasmus (country/you/you/country). It's the right tool when you're telling someone "you have it backwards," expressing give-and-take, or pointing at a mismatch. Chiasmus doesn't have to be literal — it can be structural/conceptual, as in Lincoln's "I claim not to have controlled events, but confess plainly that events have controlled me" (I→events→events→me) or the Lord's Prayer's "lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil" (simple words outside, Latinate words *temptation*/*deliver* inside).

**8. Use these devices sparingly, and never imitate — absorb instead.** Overusing repetition or reversal reads as "trying too hard" (a hallmark of bad political speech). Lincoln never imitated the Bible or Shakespeare; he immersed himself in them until their instincts became his own, then wrote as a person of his own time. Writing should first sound like natural speech in your own voice — force a borrowed style onto it and it "never sounds right."

**9. The real curriculum has two lessons: efficiency, then eloquence.** Lesson one (most writing advice stops here): be concise, cut needless words, write clearly. Lesson two — Farnsworth's actual interest — is that clear-and-concise writing can still be tedious; what separates memorable prose is the deliberate, technical use of contrast and pattern. His classroom exercise: have students rewrite modern Supreme Court opinions "as Oliver Wendell Holmes would have written them" — not to produce usable imitation, but to force close attention to what made the original work, which then gets discarded once internalized.

**10. Copying out quotes by hand (not copy-paste) reveals structure that reading alone doesn't.** Farnsworth writes out every example he analyzes for his books rather than pasting it, because the act of transcription surfaces the mechanics of the sentence.

**11. Beautiful rhetoric is a double-edged tool.** Studying it helps you construct more persuasive, memorable prose — but it should also inoculate you against being fooled: something stated in a tidy, closed rhetorical pattern (like a chiasmus) "admits of no interruption or objection" and can sound more true than it is, regardless of whether it actually is.

## Quotes worth keeping

> "In English there's basically two almost always two words for everything... prefer Saxon words to romance words or latinate words... prefer simple words to fancy ones." — Ward Farnsworth

> "Some people think you need fancy or latinate words to create a sense of strength and power in your writing... it turns out that when you really know what you're talking about, one way to know is you can explain yourself in Saxon words. That's how you really know when you understand something well enough." — Ward Farnsworth

> "There's no rule except make sure you've got a good reason for what you're doing... I follow all rules of style and grammar and encourage others to, but if you need to break a rule, well, just make sure you know why you're doing it." — Ward Farnsworth

> "It's like in music — you know, music is about chords, chords aren't that interesting, what's interesting is the chord change in music. And it's like that in English... if you want to get beyond just being efficient to being memorable and eloquent, you've got to be a student of contrast." — Ward Farnsworth

> "People don't sit around thinking about how impressed they are by your use of a technique — if they notice the technique, it's bad technique. What they might do though is be struck: that's well said." — Ward Farnsworth

> "Good writing is the most efficient writing, end of story — that's what I think is the sort of ethos behind most books on writing... but you can be clear and concise and mesmerizing, inspiring and memorable, or clear and concise and tedious and boring and nobody cares." — Ward Farnsworth

> "One reason you study rhetoric in good English is to give yourself a break from the lousy rhetoric in English that you're surrounded by in social media... social media is like a campus where rhetoric is taught — it's not taught well." — Ward Farnsworth

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- In my own recent writing, where do I default to Latinate words out of habit (or to "sound smarter"), and where would a plainer Saxon word actually hit harder?
- Do I ever set up contrast deliberately — fancy phrasing followed by a plain, punchy close — or do I write in one register the whole way through?
- Am I paying attention to what's in the *last* position of a sentence or paragraph, given that's what "rings in the ear"?
- Which writers or texts am I currently immersing myself in, and is that immersion actually shaping my instincts, or am I just reading passively?
- When I encounter a beautifully phrased argument (online, in a pitch, in politics), am I separating "sounds true" from "is true," or letting the rhetoric do persuasive work I haven't verified?
