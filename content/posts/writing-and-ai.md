The central insight from contemporary writing practitioners is counterintuitive: AI is most useful precisely where writers are weakest, and most dangerous where they're strongest. Unlike previous writing technologies (the word processor, spell-check, Grammarly), AI doesn't merely surface what you already know — it proposes. That proposal can accelerate good work (finding examples, testing tone, amplifying research) or hollow out the core: the suffering, the conviction, the stakes that make writing matter.

## AI's Fundamental Limitations

**AI can't hold conviction under pressure.** Jimmy Soni observed that when asking LLMs to refine manifestos or opinion pieces, the system "cleans up" the wrongness — the controlled anger, the unflinching claim — that makes writing persuasive. Conviction requires stakes; AI only succeeds, never collides with impossibility.

**Outsourcing the wrong part kills the work.** Ezra Klein: AI is better at parts he doesn't need help with (making prose sound "correct") than at the parts he does (grappling with an idea, deciding if it's worth saying). The danger is using AI to skip the grappling, which is exactly where thinking happens.

**Art requires suffering; AI can't.** Tom Junod framed it starkly: "without suffering, what kind of true art?" AI only succeeds. A writer collides with impossibility, fails, tries again. Without that friction, the work becomes competent performance without authenticity. Human suffering is not romantic; it's structural. Every art form requires it.

**The Matrix theory of mind doesn't hold.** Maria Popova: understanding doesn't come from consuming information faster; it comes from "grappling with text, making connections," letting material change you over time. AI's promise to speedrun knowledge is false — knowledge supposed to change you, and speed is the enemy of transformation. Institutions (libraries, monasteries) have always known this: slow, embodied practice builds understanding that scanning never can.

**LLM prose is bland because it's composite.** Steven Pinker's observation: machine writing avoids jargon and has orderly structure ("good writing" by his own standards), yet is instantly recognizable as generic. It's like morphing hundreds of faces into one — clear but not beautiful, the way composites are more attractive than any individual input.

## What AI Is Actually Good For

**Middle-to-middle work, not end-to-end.** Jimmy Soni's hard-won position: humans set vision and verify/fact-check; AI does the middle work — research amplification, finding wrong words, adversarial red-teaming. Giving AI the entire task (vision + execution + verification) produces generic work. Integrating it into the workflow where you're strongest (vision, judgment) and weakest (exhaustively finding counterexamples) is where the multiplier lives.

**AI as tone-critic.** Tyler Cowen: "I ran my writing through Claude asking what readers find obnoxious; it correctly flagged condescension." Many managers use it to "make it warm" before sending. Cowen also uses it to interrogate historical claims ("tell me chapter questions about novels") rather than buying 20–30 books — new secondary literature, free of human agenda.

**Voice dictation defeats self-consciousness.** Jimmy Soni using Whisper Flow (voice-to-text) gets a bad first draft out fast via voice before self-consciousness can block. The transcription step bypasses the internal editor.

**Adversarial red-teaming surfaces blind spots.** Soni's technique: have AI write a 700-word takedown of your own op-ed ("do not hold back") to find blind spots before publishing. Structured skepticism beats generic feedback.

**Research amplification, not generation.** Use AI to expand a seed idea once you have a distinct point. Tyler Cowen: ChatGPT helps find examples and stories once you know what you're looking for; if you ask it to generate the idea itself, you get blob. Long dictated context (1.5–3 minutes) followed by short follow-up questions (not 10 things in one prompt) maintains quality.

**Never let AI write your prose.** Tyler Cowen's principle: "I don't let it write for me... like my little baby." Idiosyncrasy is what differentiates; AI is secondary literature for research, not final copy. Even a flawed sentence that sounds like you is better than a perfect sentence that sounds like everyone.

## Prompting Technique & Workflow

**Give long dictation first, keep follow-ups short.** Tyler Cowen's practice: voice-dictate 1.5–3 minutes to set context, then ask short follow-ups. Asking 10 things in one long prompt degrades quality; context exhaustion is real.

**Don't ask meta-questions; ask specific ones.** Cowen: "What should I ask person X" produces bland output; specific, detailed, historical questions out-of-left-field get novel insights. The system does better with constraints than freedom.

**Ask "how could this be misunderstood" instead of "how could this be understood."** John Lennox's editing principle: this exposes assumptions and unstated context that readers won't share. It's more generative than the normal direction.

**Using AI as secondary literature.** Rather than buying 20–30 books to fact-check a claim, interrogate LLMs with chapter questions about novels or historical figures. It's research acceleration without replacing primary sources, provided you treat it as a tool for exploration, not verification.

## Publishing Shifts & Distribution

**Fewer books, more high-frequency writing.** Tyler Cowen: predictive books (2–4 year lead) become obsolete; writers should shift to blogs, Substack, daily posts for time-sensitive work, save books for "frozen in history" where lead time doesn't matter. AI accelerates the disadvantage of slow-publication books.

**Open silos vs. closed silos.** Sam Corcos: the real distinction isn't sync vs. async; it's findable information later. Email/DMs = closed silos; long-form docs with comment threads = open silos anyone can search. AI's strength is surfacing what's already written, not writing from scratch.

**Loom + written memo pairing.** For long docs (200 pages), pair with 20-minute Loom summary at 2x; most won't read the whole thing but need context. Written stays; video provides the bridge. This hybrid survives AI's pressure on text.

**Email as owned distribution.** Tim Ferriss and others: social platforms are "renting your audience"; email survived repeated "email is dead" predictions. Own your distribution channel because AI will make algorithmic feeds increasingly generic.

## Ethical & Philosophical Concerns

**Sincerity, even flawed, beats polished insincerity.** Marc Andreessen: a wedding note being AI-written felt like "violation and betrayal"; he'd prefer "half as sincere, littered with typos." The polish signals deception. Authenticity means visible fingerprints — effort, struggle, personal voice.

**Grammar-violation as badge of success.** Robert Macfarlane: Grammarly disapproval is a success signal. Flattens distinctiveness underlying great writing. The tools that make prose "correct" also make it generic.

**Copyright is the practical fault line.** Jon Yaged: AI-generated content has "zero barrier to entry"; the same framework applying to AI use as to borrowed material. Training on copyrighted work without consent and selling the output is intellectual theft, even if legal liability is unclear.

## The Deeper Argument: Why This Matters

The pattern across practitioners is consistent: AI works best as a *thinking tool*, not a *writing tool*. It can help you think (red-teaming your own ideas, finding examples you hadn't considered, testing tone), but it can't replace the parts of writing that matter — conviction, specificity, earned insight.

**Writing is how you think.** Paul Graham's observation: "You should expect 80% of the ideas in an essay to come after you start writing the piece." Incomplete ideas are solved by *writing*, not thinking alone. If you outsource the writing, you outsource the thinking. That's the real loss.

**Suffering and time can't be compressed.** Maria Popova: art requires information plus time plus vulnerability. "Even if you try to make [AI] suffer... it'll already be succeeding at executing failure. It will never know what it's like to collide with its own impossibility." The pressure that makes you choose one word over another, that makes you sit with a problem for weeks, is what builds the work that lasts.
