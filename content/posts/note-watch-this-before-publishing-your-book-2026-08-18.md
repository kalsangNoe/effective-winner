## TL;DR
David Perell interviews David Shelley, CEO of Hachette (US/UK), about what publishers actually do and how authors should navigate the industry. Shelley's core message: publishing is a power-law business where most books lose money and most authors never earn out their advance, so writers should stop optimizing for the biggest check and instead optimize for editor chemistry, a publisher's vision, and honest communication — because a bidding war winner with no plan can sink a book while a modest advance with a passionate editor can produce a bestseller. He also argues that money and art aren't in tension so much as they require different playbooks: know which game you're playing (wide readership vs. lasting artistic worth) and don't blend the two mid-project. Along the way he covers the economics of covers, formats, genres, and international markets, and ends by pitching Hachette's range of business models (traditional advance deals vs. Bookouture's no-advance/high-royalty model) as itself the advice: different writers need different structures.

## Key ideas
- **What publishers actually do beyond printing:** make people want the book (bookstore buy-in, cover, press, advertising), make the book itself as good as it can be, then physically ship it — Hachette ships ~150M books/year out of a 400-person Indiana warehouse to 220 countries.
- **Production craft is a real differentiator:** paper/type quality, "sprayed edges" (spredges) as a romance/fantasy design trend, and deckled edges — the kind of physical detail self-publishing struggles to match.
- **Advice to unpublished writers — don't be deterred by rejection:** "publishers don't know everything." Digital publishing (imprints like Bookouture, self-publishing) has lowered barriers versus the old gatekept slush-pile era; some of Hachette's biggest authors (e.g., Kelly Hart's *Quicksilver*) started self-published and were later signed.
- **Agents aren't oracles either:** "agents don't know" — their hit rate isn't meaningfully better than publishers'. Their real value is matching books to the right editor/house, negotiating rights (film, translation), and sometimes mediating author-publisher disputes.
- **How to handle disagreement over covers/packaging:** be radically candid rather than "British" indirection — reframe the argument away from personal taste ("I don't like green") toward "how do we sell the maximum number of copies." James Patterson's rule of thumb: "I see it, I want it" — average dwell time on a bookstore table is 2–3 seconds, so a cover is a Trojan horse, not necessarily an accurate portrait of the book.
- **Money vs. art is a spectrum, not a binary:** contrasts Ronald Firbank (1920s experimental queer novelist who self-financed knowing there was no market) with commercially-driven writers like Lee Child. Both are legitimate; the danger is a "pure artist" diluting their work by chasing commercial cues, or a commercial writer being precious about art when the aim is reach. Decide which game you're in and commit.
- **Choosing an editor matters more than the advance:** in a bidding war, meet every editor — look for passion, chemistry, and whether they "get" the book, since you'll spend more time with them than with friends over a multi-book relationship. Two illustrative cases: a celebrity memoir chased the biggest all-cash offer and "the book sank"; a thriller author took a smaller advance from an editor with a clear fix for the ending and the book sold a million copies.
- **Marketing is split three ways:** what the publisher does natively (distribution, end-caps, billboards), grassroots/guerrilla tactics only the author can do (James Clear's 80-podcast launch week, BookTok/Twitter), and paid amplification for authors with money to spend. Shelley flags a "red flag" if a publisher tells an author to hire their own marketing team — that's supposed to be the publisher's job.
- **The industry runs on a power law:** a truism that ~80% of books lose money; DOJ merger filings cited show >85% of authors never earn out their advance, and books with $250k+ advances are 2% of acquisitions but 70% of advance spending. Penguin Random House's Markus Dohle comparison: publishers as angel investors funding "thousands of ideas and dreams" for a few hits (*Where the Crawdads Sing* had a modest advance and became one of the best-selling books of the last 20 years).
- **Format and market differences:** US readers favor hardcovers (bigger author royalties), UK readers favor paperbacks; the US mass-market paperback format has largely died out; France is a distinct, more cerebral/philosophical market where translation success often doesn't transfer elsewhere. Gen Z/Gen Alpha are driving a swing back to physical books (cites *Heartstopper*) for beauty, signaling, and deliberate analog/mental-health reasons, even as audiobooks (not ebooks) are now the fastest-growing format and are pulling in younger male readers who weren't previously book buyers.
- **A strong moral sense predicts staying power:** the most enduring IP (Narnia, Lord of the Rings, Harry Potter) has a coherent moral universe; genre conventions like the villain always being caught in mystery reinforce this, while horror is the exception — a "subversive genre" with almost no rules, which is why it's a natural space for feminist/queer reinvention.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "I think of it like a Trojan horse... if people don't pick your book up, they won't experience it." — David Shelley, on covers
- "The author went with the lots of money option and the book sank." — David Shelley
- "There's a truism in the industry that 80% of books lose money." — David Shelley

- "Just because a publisher hasn't paid a ton of money for you does not mean your book might not be an enormous bestseller." — David Shelley

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I optimizing my own writing/publishing choices for the biggest short-term payout, or for the collaborator most likely to make the work land with readers?
- Where in my own work am I trying to be Ronald Firbank (art for its own sake) versus Lee Child (deliberately reverse-engineering what connects) — and am I being honest with myself about which one I'm actually doing?
- Do I have a moral/thematic throughline in what I write that a reader could sense even if I never state it directly, the way Shelley describes Sally Rooney's subtext?
- If 80% of books lose money and advances cluster on a tiny fraction of acquisitions, how should that reshape my expectations and risk tolerance if I ever pursue traditional publishing?
- Is there a "Trojan horse" packaging problem in how I present my own work — am I choosing the accurate framing or the one that gets picked up in 2–3 seconds?
