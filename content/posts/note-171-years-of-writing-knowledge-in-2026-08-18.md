## TL;DR
David Perell compiles his favorite writing lessons from a year of podcast interviews with novelists, rhetoricians, founders, and copywriters. The throughline across guests: great writing isn't about following one style — it's about specificity over cliché, formula plus intuition, and rigorous revision. Guests cover concrete craft techniques (diacope, kiasmus, delayed predication), storytelling mechanics (stakes, the "5-second moment of change"), and the discipline of editing ruthlessly rather than chasing distraction-driven culture. The episode closes on note-taking, rhetoric, and lessons from Peter Thiel and Paul Graham on clear thinking as the root of clear writing.

## Key ideas
- **Amor Towles — write from the child's specific perception, not the cliché landmark.** Instead of writing "the Beatles played on the radio" and "frozen peas from the Frigidaire" (clichés that signal a decade without evoking it), ask what a seven-year-old would actually notice — like the sensation of a frozen pea brick sliding out intact. Write the first draft purely for yourself; edit by "turning the lens around" to the reader's perspective and cutting whatever only serves your own whims.
- **Mark Forsyth — rhetorical formulas make lines unforgettable.** Diacope (a "verbal sandwich," e.g. "Bond, James Bond," "free at last, free at last") and kiasmos (mirrored reversal, e.g. JFK's "ask not what your country can do for you, ask what you can do for your country") work because humans love symmetry — the same reason we love the Taj Mahal. These formulas are so powerful they can implant false memories (everyone "remembers" the Wicked Witch saying "fly, my pretties, fly" — she never says it).
- **Ted Gioia — "dopamine culture" is a new third category beyond art and entertainment: pure distraction, engineered like junk food to be addictive.** His advice to writers: don't chase it. Editors who told him to "dumb it down" are now out of work along with the outlets they wrote for, because distraction content builds no long-term relationship with an audience — a viral 2-million-view clip earns him no readers, while long engagement builds a career. He draws a parallel to organic food replacing processed food: "have natural ingredients, do organically, be true to quality."
- **Lulu Cheng Meservey — whoever frames the story first wins; a defense built on statistics loses to an attack built on story.** Human memory is shaped like stories, not facts — "one death is a tragedy, a thousand deaths is a statistic." In negotiations, whoever holds the pen for the first draft holds power; if you're reacting to someone else's frame, you've already lost.
- **Sam Altman — take notes on a cheap spiral notebook you can rip pages from and discard.** He never keeps a completed notebook — he rips pages out, crumples them, and throws them away once processed. Tools: Uniball Micro .5 pen or Muji dark-blue ink; a notebook with a hard cover that fits in a pocket. He burns through one every 2–3 weeks.
- **Scott Galloway — interrupt your own cadence to create connection.** A quick one-sentence anecdote lands better than a long, telegraphed story ("okay now sit down, I'm going to tell a story" kills it). He deliberately breaks rhythm with jokes, odd asides, or personal photos — tactics he admits are "emotionally manipulative" but effective, because being genuinely yourself (including being "profane and vulgar," which costs him some corporate clients) is what people actually connect to.
- **Shaan Puri — every story needs stakes, and every story is a 5-second moment of change.** Stakes don't require sharks or extremity — just genuine emotional risk (e.g., embarrassment in front of your mom). Every story, from romcoms to Dark Knight, hinges on one transformative moment, usually rock bottom, where the character becomes the opposite of who they were at the start: "if you don't know what's the 5-second moment of change for the main character, you don't really have a great story."
- **Jason Fried — breaking down his "Once" and "Hey" launch letters line by line.** Structure: open with a line that plants a question without asking one ("Something happened to business software"); establish a shared reality the reader nods along to ("payers and prayers," "you should own that by now") before pivoting to the new offer; end paragraphs with a punchy summary line ("boom, you're evicted"). The name "Once" itself emerged mid-sentence from writing "once upon a time." He deliberately alternates short, plain words with occasional multi-syllable ones ("avalanche of automated emails") because rarity makes them stand out.
- **Harry Dry — building an ad through ~25 rewrites over two days, always inside the actual design tool.** He starts from a "seed" idea (a tweet: going from 1% to 2% conversion is a 100% improvement), finds a visual "conflict" to structure it against (a Volkswagen ad's before/after layout), then iterates relentlessly — including a free-associate "spit-balling" phase to find the right header ("throw money and pray") without judging ideas as they come. He never writes copy separately from design — "copy and design are one and the same."
- **Robert Greene — every story needs a theme, and the universal lives in the particular.** Physical, sensory detail (colors, smells, sounds) makes a scene come alive; the emotional throughline (facing death, envy, grandiosity) is what makes it universal. He connects this to his own mission: making history and classic literature "hip" again, since books gave him an imagination-expanding "magic carpet" as a child that he wants today's kids — raised on pre-fed content — to rediscover.
- **Richard Powers — English speakers have "built-in bilingualism" (Anglo-Saxon vs. Latinate vocabulary) that shapes register.** "Mansion" sounds richer than "house" because Latinate words (via the Norman conquest) carry higher socioeconomic connotation. At the sentence level, where you place the predication (subject + verb) controls emotional effect: front-loaded ("he pointed the gun") creates shock; delayed predication (piling up modifiers before the subject) creates suspense — literally hiding the subject the way a hidden character is hidden in the room.
- **Sam Altman — on Peter Thiel and Paul Graham.** Thiel pairs original thinking with radically compressed, evocative statements — a rare combination. His biggest investing regret wasn't a loss but skipping Facebook's Series A: "find what is working and go aggressively after it... quadruple down." Most business books fail because they stretch three good ideas into 300 pages, when readers want three good ideas in one page. Graham's essays taught Altman by example, not instruction — writing as a tool for thinking clearly, since a muddled pitch often reveals thinking that was never actually clear.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I reaching for a "landmark" cliché (a stock image or phrase) instead of the specific, sensory detail a real observer would notice?
- Do my openings plant a question in the reader's mind without literally asking one — or do I explain too much up front?
- Am I building any of my sentences around deliberately delayed predication for suspense, or do I default to subject-verb-up-front every time?
- Whose "frame" am I reacting to right now — could I be setting the frame instead of defending against someone else's story?
- If I stripped my current draft down to "three good ideas in one page," what would survive?
