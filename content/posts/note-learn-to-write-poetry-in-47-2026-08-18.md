## TL;DR
David Perell interviews former U.S. Poet Laureates Ada Limón and Joy Harjo about what poetry actually requires: not cleverness but sustained, embodied listening. Their core argument is that writing is primarily receiving rather than producing — attending to rhythm, silence, and resonance in the world before translating any of it into language. They connect this to how modern life (phones, texting, "clock time") flattens attention and language into purely utilitarian tools, and argue poetry's job is to restore metaphor, mystery, and the sense that language can cast a spell. Both resist trend-chasing and insist a poet's voice isn't found but uncovered by getting to know yourself and the world with sustained curiosity.

## Key ideas

- **Writing is receiving, not making.** Limón: "so much of writing is receiving... much of what we do as artists is to receive the world, to look, to listen, all the senses alive." Her best poems come from listening to the world rather than immediately translating it into her own experience.
- **Everything is held together by rhythm.** Harjo: rhythm runs through organs, cells, the heart, the earth's systems — "we're held together by rhythm." Poets (and mechanics, gardeners) are people especially attuned to it; she describes being able to hear what's wrong with an engine because she grew up around mechanics.
- **Silence isn't empty, it's loud.** Harjo describes a group silence exercise in Patagonia, Argentina: at first it seems quiet, then "it is loud" — eagles, waterfalls, glacier melt. Limón adds: "As if silence is depth, not nothingness."
- **Fast media breaks the ability to listen.** Harjo says her dreams — historically long and novelistic — started "flipping quickly by like videos, like reels or TikToks," and she recognized this was interfering with lengthy thought, so she pulled back from that kind of media consumption.
- **Voice isn't found, it's already there.** Harjo tells students looking for their voice: "it's already there... your voice is there." What they're really after is what's unique to their own expression, which comes from getting to know yourself and the world with curiosity — where your mind travels, where it feels safest.
- **Resist trend-chasing.** Harjo deliberately keeps her voice from being "caught in a particular fashion of the times," even when that made her work harder to be seen or understood in a contemporary-poetry world she describes as youth- and trend-focused.
- **Poetry resists "clock time."** Harjo frames colonization and modern industrial time as recent and small against the eternal time her people's culture has existed in. Limón extends this to attention: clock time (2:28 vs. 2:30) and phones "eat time" and make sustained, cultivated focus hard to reach.
- **The line break is the instruction manual for how to read the poem.** Limón: poetry starts at the level of sound and syllable before word, clause, and sentence — the opposite of prose, which starts at the sentence. "The line break tells you how to read the poem... which are the pauses or the silences." Everything — punctuation, phrasing, rhythm — has to live on the page because, unlike a song, there's no music coming later to carry it.
- **Poetry, dance, and music are historically inseparable.** Harjo, who trained in hula in Hawaii, ties poetry to land, star patterns, and cultural memory — dance and song are "reinforcing a template of cultural philosophical understanding," part of how a culture knows who it is.
- **Language as utilitarian tool vs. language as spell.** Limón: everyday language becomes "a blunt object"; poetry reimagines it as "an ecstatic, ethereal, transitory thing... more like spell or magic." Texting resists metaphor almost structurally — it's built for getting information across, not for meaning.
- **Metaphor is poetry's essence.** Harjo: "Poetry is essentially metaphor" — holding contradictory readings at once ("it's this and it's also this") rather than resolving to one literal meaning, unlike "clock time writing" (texts, emails) which is purely transactional.
- **Trust the sound over the grammar.** Harjo recounts overruling a New Yorker editor's suggested word order on "Shape Shifter" because it flattened the sentence: "No, this is sound sense" — the poem's rhythm can outrank standard grammar.
- **Being a poet means being willing to trust-fall into emotion and stay there long enough to bring it back in words** so other people can experience its depth — and, per Limón, sometimes it's not falling into the feeling but simply recognizing you're already in it.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I imposing a structure prematurely instead of listening for the rhythm the material already has?
- What's the "clock time" equivalent in my own habits — the thing that flattens my attention before I even sit down to write?
- Do I revise for grammatical correctness in places where "sound sense" (rhythm, feel) should actually win?
- What would it mean to treat silence as data rather than an absence — as something worth sitting inside before producing anything?
- Am I chasing a trend-driven "voice" instead of trusting that mine is already there and just needs uncovering through sustained curiosity?
