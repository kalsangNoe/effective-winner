## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Henrik Karlsson, the writer behind *Escaping Flatland*, about the mechanics behind his essays. Karlsson describes a five-step process — explore for a year or more, collect notes until an "animating question" forms, cluster the material, order it by feeling rather than logic, then write the core fast and edit last — and insists writing quality comes from looking at reality more than at the page. Underneath the method is a philosophy: young ideas are fragile and need protection from critique, boring/obvious truths are the deepest ones and need to be pierced through with sequencing and story, and the goal of writing is not communication but transformation of the writer, and eventually of a "culture" of readers who find each other through the work.

## Key ideas

**Step 1 — Explore for a year before writing.** Karlsson never writes right after getting excited about an idea; he lets it sit "at least like a year," reading, taking notes, having conversations, and revisiting them months later until something "coalesces" into an *animating question* — a question he needs answered because it matters to him personally (e.g., "how could this help me become a better father").

**Step 2 — Collect thoughts around the question, which acts like a magnetic field.** Once the question is alive, notes and stories start visibly organizing themselves around it — "that argument from Aristotle could be used," etc.

**Step 3 — Order the ideas by feeling, not logic.** He describes essays as "geometrical objects," clusters with color and emotional charge that he moves around — sometimes lying on a sofa — until he feels "energy released in my body," which signals the right sequence. The point of an essay isn't just conveying ideas but putting the reader in a specific emotional/cognitive state.

**Step 4 — Write the core fast, then edit.** He writes (or dictates lying down, eyes closed) quickly to capture the emotional momentum, sometimes talking through a draft multiple times until it "snaps into place." Beginnings and endings are written last — forcing an opening early makes it "pretentious" and it "doesn't hold" once reread; instead the right opening usually already exists somewhere in the draft (e.g., a throwaway Gertrude Stein line became the opening of "Looking for Alice").

**"Suck reality into the writing."** Karlsson tests every claim against 3–4 concrete case studies from his or his friends' lives, like a painter doing a still life — writing, comparing to reality, rewriting. Most of that material never appears in the final essay ("this iceberg underneath that the reader doesn't even see"). His wife, as editor, pushes for concrete examples rather than line edits: "let's throw that out, but that's interesting — what's a concrete example of this?"

**Look, don't think.** Borrowing Wittgenstein's line, he practices "still life" writing exercises — staring at a single flower or plant for a long time and redescribing it — to break past habitual abstractions ("it's a red dress" vs. the actual 10,000 shades of red). He cites Delacroix's diaries, where the painter notices "the shadow was yellow" because he actually looked.

**Protect young ideas from critique.** Referencing Jony Ive's eulogy for Steve Jobs, Karlsson says early ideas are as fragile as a small bird and are easily "squished" by social judgment — some of his best ideas start as almost nothing ("it should feel pink") and need to live privately in a diary before they can be shared.

**Lower your promises to write more freely.** Big-promise pieces (e.g., his "Childhoods of Exceptional People," built from 40 books) become "slave work" because he has to deliver on the promise. Pieces that start from a small, honest observation ("almost all my favorite books were not meant to be published") let him wander and "overdeliver," which he finds more fun and more alive.

**Boring truths are the deepest ones.** The most important ideas (be kind, be a good father) sound trite if stated flatly, so most writers avoid them for something clever instead — but the real craft is sequencing story and image so the obvious truth "pierces through to your heart." He references Werner Herzog's idea of "ecstatic truth": Herzog invents small emotional beats (a fake Pascal quote, staged door-opening in a documentary) not to lie, but to prime the reader to feel a deeper truth.

**Write toward a pre-linguistic, confused state.** Karlsson deliberately destabilizes his own mental models — writing something down, critiquing it, finding counterexamples — comparing it to accelerated U.S. military training that keeps trainees confused so they don't form premature "knowledge shields." Confusion, followed by a "collapse into simplicity," is where new insight comes from.

**Writing as a search query that summons a culture.** His breakout realization (echoing his own essay title, "a very long and complex search query to find fascinating people") is that publishing honestly and weirdly — rather than simplifying for a mass audience — attracts the specific people who resonate, turning writing into a way of finding a community where "being your full idiosyncratic self" is normal.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "When I get excited about something I can't write about it — I have to like give it a year." — Henrik Karlsson
- "Don't think, look." — Karlsson, citing Wittgenstein
- "I want the piece to end up smarter than I am." — Karlsson
- "You're going to take this thing that is actually like a cloud or a net or a network in your head and then you're going to turn it into a string, a line — because writing is one word after the other." — Karlsson
- "The thing that sustains me is how transformative it is to really give yourself time to sit with something... and put your own words to it." — Karlsson, on why he writes
- "Poetry is language for which we have no defenses." — Karlsson, quoting the poet David Whyte
- "Most people only read the title, so you might as well put all of the important information in the title." — Karlsson, on long titles
- "It's an enormous privilege to be allowed to live like that, because everything is integrated into everything else." — Karlsson, on writing, farming, and fatherhood being intertwined

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I give my own ideas the "year" Karlsson insists on, or do I rush from excitement straight to the page — and what would change if I deliberately delayed?
- Where in my writing am I stating an obvious truth flatly instead of sequencing story and image to make it "pierce through"?
- Am I protecting fragile early ideas from premature critique (mine or others'), or am I exposing them too soon and letting them get squished?
- What would it look like to write from a genuinely "confused" state rather than from an already-settled opinion — and am I mistaking early clarity for depth?
- Is my own writing simplified for a broad audience in a way that's actually preventing the right, specific readers from finding me?
