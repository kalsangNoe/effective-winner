## TL;DR
David Perell interviews investing writer Michael Mauboussin (Counterpoint Global, Columbia Business School) about how he turns dense financial and scientific research into writing that non-specialists can read. The throughline is that writing is fundamentally an act of understanding, not transcription — Mauboussin forces himself to write in order to discover the gaps in his own knowledge, then fights the "curse of knowledge" by keeping language concrete, story-driven, and jargon-free. He also describes his research method as synthesis rather than original discovery: borrowing frameworks from economics, biology, and philosophy (increasing returns, the wisdom of crowds, neural pruning) and applying them to markets. The conversation closes on craft mechanics — outlining around one core sentence, working with editors, and deliberately injecting personal stories and vulnerability into otherwise dry analytical writing.

## Key ideas
- **Writing is understanding, not output.** Mauboussin says the core purpose of writing for him is to force himself back to first principles — if he can't explain something clearly, he doesn't understand it yet. Learning and teaching are inseparable for him.
- **The paradox/curse of knowledge is the main enemy.** Experts sit on an "iceberg" of knowledge and only communicate the visible tip; the job of the writer is to retain analytical depth while making the surface radically simple — his test is "could a high schooler read this."
- **Simplicity is not a trade-off with sophistication.** He argues elite, technical readers (he names writing for people like Jim Simons) actually respond just as well to clear, concrete writing as general readers do — there's no dumbing-down cost.
- **Classic style ("Pinker" style).** Referencing Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style*, Mauboussin favors "classic style": writer and reader as equals looking together at something in the world ("let's talk about what we see"), as opposed to a lecturing or ornate register.
- **Concreteness beats abstraction.** He deliberately uses numbers, sports analogies (golf, deliberate practice), and specific images rather than abstractions, because concrete detail is what readers remember and share.
- **Practice analytically, perform intuitively.** Borrowed from a golf lesson, this became one of his recurring pieces — you build skill through analytical, broken-down practice, but execution/writing flow only works once that groundwork lets you stop thinking about mechanics.
- **The wisdom of crowds needs three conditions.** Diversity of views, aggregation, and incentives (rewards for being right) — he treats this as a reusable framework he applies across writing topics, not just markets.
- **The Grossman–Stiglitz "Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets" insight, reframed for writers.** If gathering information is costly, prices (and by analogy, ideas) can never be perfectly "priced in" — meaning there is always unclaimed opportunity for someone willing to do the work. His line: "your ignorance is my opportunity," echoing Bezos's "your margin is my opportunity."
- **Most great writing is synthesis, not invention.** He explicitly says the future for a nonfiction/business writer is less about a single "divine revelation" and more about connecting existing ideas from different fields (e.g., combining Brian Arthur's work on increasing returns with a strategy framework) — citing Paul Krugman reviving "done" trade theory by applying an outside framework as proof a "finished" topic can still yield a Nobel.
- **Editors matter enormously.** He describes his relationship with editor Lawrence (a longtime line editor with no finance background) as invaluable precisely because Lawrence catches jargon and technical terms that a "simpler term would do" for — the ideal editor is smart but domain-naive.
- **Outline around one sentence.** His drafting process is to find a single sentence that captures the idea, then treat every other paragraph as either orbiting that core sentence or getting cut — "if it relates to that one sentence... if not, I cut it."
- **Injecting personality is a competitive edge, especially against AI.** He says finance writers tend to suppress their own voice and personal stories, making their writing "dry and stale" — and that being more human/personal is now a way to differentiate from AI-generated writing. He deliberately opens pieces with personal anecdotes (a job story, a trash-can story) because readers remember and relate to them.
- **Systems thinking recurs across his work** — he pulls the "wisdom vs. madness of crowds," complex adaptive systems, and a neurobiology analogy (childhood synaptic pruning as a metaphor for industry consolidation, e.g. the dot-com bust of "50-pound bags of dog food" companies) as recurring lenses for explaining markets.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What is the single sentence my current piece orbits around — and how much of the draft would survive if I cut everything that doesn't relate to it?
- Where am I hiding behind jargon or abstraction instead of finding the concrete image or number a reader would actually remember?
- Am I treating "I don't have anything original to say" as a blocker, when synthesis of existing ideas (per Mauboussin/Krugman) is often where the real value is?
- Where in my writing am I suppressing personal experience or voice because it feels unprofessional — and is that making the piece drier than it needs to be?
- Do I have a "Lawrence" — an editor smart enough to catch bad writing but naive enough about my domain to flag jargon I no longer notice?
