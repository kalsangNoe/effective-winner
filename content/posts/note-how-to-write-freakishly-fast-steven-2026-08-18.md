## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Steven Levitan, co-creator/showrunner of Modern Family (and creator of Just Shoot Me), about the craft mechanics behind writing hundreds of sitcom episodes fast and well. Levitan's speed comes from a TV-reporter background and being a "fast-twitch" writer who'd rather finish and fix than deliberate. The bulk of the conversation is about story engineering: story is king over jokes, character dynamics are literally designed as "lines" between points that must "crackle," every episode needs a clear theme, and comedy ages badly when it's just setup-punchline but ages well when it's rooted in real, observed behavior. He closes with a nuanced, personally conflicted take on AI in Hollywood — useful as research and production tool, dangerous for jobs, but structurally missing "conviction."

## Key ideas

**On speed**
- **Speed is a trained habit, not a talent.** Levitan's first job was as a TV reporter, where he'd have to report, write, and file a script for the 6 o'clock news same-day — that pace became his baseline, so a "one week" TV writing deadline later felt like "an eternity."
- **Fast-twitch vs. slow-twitch writers.** Levitan identifies as fully fast-twitch: "you can go back and fix," so he prefers to keep momentum rather than deliberate upfront. He values having slow-twitch collaborators in the room even though their pace frustrates him.
- **Finishing is driven by anxiety relief**, not love of the process: "I'm anxious to get the process over with... there's a giant relief for me when it's finished."

**On story over jokes**
- **"Story is king."** The hardest and most important part of the job is breaking story, not writing jokes — once you have a great story, "the drafts are easy." He references the old joke about the screenwriter who's "90% done" after finishing the outline.
- **Comedy dates; character-driven story doesn't.** Old setup-punchline joke writing ("oh shucks 1960s 1970s") ages badly. The best laughs "come out of character" and natural behavior, which is why some newer comedy writers are "allergic to jokes" that feel forced or "sitcomy."
- **Jokes should be thrown away, not landed.** He told actors to deliver jokes like a drug dealer dumping evidence out a car window while fleeing cops — underplay them so the audience catches them on a rewatch rather than telegraphing "here comes a joke."

**On character design**
- **"Two points in a line" theory.** Every character is a point; the relationship between two characters is a line, and the job is to make that line "crackle like a high-power electrical line" by stacking contrasting attributes (Oscar/Felix, Sam/Diane, Frasier/Niles) rather than settling for generic "they're friends."
- **Character work is upfront investment that compounds for hundreds of episodes.** He and Christopher Lloyd spent six weeks purely on Modern Family's character dynamics before writing. Skipping this ("I didn't do the homework") is, in his experience, exactly what causes writers to be stuck rewriting a character at 2am for years.
- **Conflict is "the tinder for the flame."** A goal plus an obstacle is what generates comedy; when a story feels flat, the fix is often "can we make the conflict bigger" — not louder, but more meaningful/consequential.

**On process and structure**
- **A concrete production week:** Monday–Tuesday rewrite (with half the writers, while the other half breaks future stories) → Wednesday table read → rewrite finished by Friday → shoot starts Monday, on a loop, while also running the previous week's edit and next week's prep in parallel.
- **Tone is 10,000 small yes/no decisions**, quoting Tom Ford: "I just said yes, no, yes, no... 100 thousand times, and that's how Tom Ford became Tom Ford." The most important trait in a second-in-command is that they share your tone instinctively.
- **Every episode needs a theme**, ideally known before writing starts — e.g. "15%" was about whether people can really change; "Caught in the Act" literally and figuratively about being caught. Multiple storylines in an episode should feed the same theme.
- **"Does it feel real?" is the top-line filter.** Grounding stories in lived, mundane detail (his own family fights literally became dialogue) makes universal, timeless material — which he credits for Modern Family "aging well" even as period-specific comedy dates fast.

**On AI (closing chapters)**
- **AI has volume, not conviction.** "AI can't give you conviction. It can give you ideas. It's sycophantic... there's a conviction that only humans can have." He finds AI genuinely funny now (post-GPT-4.5) but says it can't yet chart the path a show needs.
- **He's ambivalent, not anti.** Production uses (generating a matching exterior shot, cutting an $11M pilot to $6M) are inevitable and could let more shows get made; but he doesn't "love" watching specific crew jobs disappear, and worries about musicians, drivers, and other below-the-line workers.
- **Hollywood's fear is economic, not just cultural**: "if the studios can do certain things for a lot less money, just as well, then they're going to." He frames the broader AI race as accelerationist geopolitics outrunning society's ability to ask "what's good for us."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I settling for a generic relationship/connection between ideas instead of forcing them to "crackle" the way Levitan demands of character pairs?
- Am I front-loading enough "character/theme" investment before I start drafting, or am I skipping the six-weeks-of-thinking step and paying for it later in endless revision?
- What's my equivalent of "does it feel real?" — a single blunt test I could apply to a draft before shipping it?
- Levitan says jokes/insights should be thrown away, not landed with emphasis — where am I over-signaling my best points instead of trusting the reader to catch them?
- His ambivalence about AI (useful tool, real conviction gap, real job-loss cost) is more nuanced than typical "pro/anti" framing — does my own view of AI in my work hold both halves at once, or am I flattening it to one side?
