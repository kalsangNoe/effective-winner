## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Steven Pinker at Harvard about why writing is so often bad and what makes it good. Pinker's central diagnosis is the "curse of knowledge": writers forget what it's like not to know what they know, and this — not deliberate obfuscation — explains most academic and bureaucratic bad writing. He gives concrete remedies (show drafts to real outsiders, favor concrete/visual language, pair generalizations with examples, cut ruthlessly for rhythm and clarity) and closes with his take on why LLM prose reads as competent but bland, and how large-scale pattern extraction has changed how he'd think about writing and cognition if starting his books today.

## Key ideas

- **The curse of knowledge, not malice, causes bad writing.** Pinker rejects the popular theory that academics deliberately obscure banal ideas to seem sophisticated ("Hanlon's razor: never attribute to malice what can be explained by stupidity"). His TED-talk anecdote: a brilliant molecular biologist lost an entire audience within seconds by never explaining what problem he was solving — oblivious that "not everyone knows what you know."
- **Get a real outsider to read your draft.** Pinker showed drafts to his mother — not because she was unsophisticated, but because she was highly intelligent yet not a cognitive psychologist. He notes even academics are dangerously insular: colleagues in adjacent subfields, or students immersed in a five-person lab, become unintelligible outside their tiny circle.
- **Writing should be visual.** His second rule: since understanding isn't "a bunch of words" but ideas that are often visual, motoric, or sensory, good writing lets the reader form a mental image. Don't say "stimulus," say "bunny rabbit." Abstractions like "paradigm" or "framework" can't be pictured.
- **Older prose feels more vivid because it had to be.** Before centuries of academic jargon accumulated, writers had to reach for shared images (Pinker's example: "the spirit of the hawk needed into our flesh" instead of "aggression"). Abstractions are efficient shorthand within a field but strip out the visual quality that makes prose gripping.
- **Speaking is natural; writing is hard — because writing has no shared context and no feedback loop.** Conversation partners share common ground and can use "this," "that," "she" and be understood; a reader picking a book off a shelf shares nothing with the writer. Speakers also get real-time feedback (furrowed brows, fidgeting) that lets them self-correct; writers get none of that.
- **Generalizations need examples, and examples need generalizations.** Both alone are close to useless — a generalization erases the detail needed to pin down what's meant, and an example without a generalization leaves the reader wondering "why did you just tell me that?" He frames this as a trade-off between context (examples) and compression (generalizations).
- **Compounds and idioms are linguistic fossils.** Pinker riffs on Richard Lederer's list (adultery/adultery, olive oil/baby oil, fingers that don't "fing") to show that word compounds don't follow one logical relationship and often preserve meanings from processes that died out centuries ago — "fossils of processes in the language that are long dead."
- **Aesthetics matter: sound, rhythm, brevity.** Pinker reads his own prose aloud to check it can be spoken smoothly, pays attention to sentence rhythm ("not perfectly tick-tock like a metronome, but there are beats"), avoids too much sibilance in a row, and uses alliteration sparingly for "a little spark of pleasure."
- **"Brevity is the soul of wit" and "omit needless words."** Both maxims are self-demonstrating — brief statements about brevity. Pinker notes that being forced to fit an 800-word limit for a magazine piece "improves the prose as if by magic," partly because shorter text is less cognitive load for the reader.
- **Children's explanations are fresh because they lack accumulated jargon.** His grandson's "clouds are water vapor, smoke is fire vapor" delights him precisely because kids haven't absorbed decades of clichés and abstractions — they describe what they can actually see.
- **Informalization has stripped some grandeur from prose, for better and worse.** A long historical process — the same one behind men no longer wearing hats, first-name address replacing "Mr./Mrs.," profanity entering ordinary speech — has pushed prose toward the conversational and away from the "elevated" style of writers like Rockefeller, Edison, or Darwin, who wrote beautifully despite not being professional writers.
- **LLM prose is "sound" but bland.** Pinker says LLM output avoids jargon and has orderly structure — "in that sense it's good writing" — but is generic, prosaic, and instantly recognizable as machine-written. He speculates it may resemble a facial composite: averaging out flaws produces something clear but not beautiful, the way morphed faces read as more attractive than any individual input face.
- **LLMs would change how Pinker frames his own field, but don't validate "the mind is an LLM."** Given the power of pattern extraction from massive input, he says he'd give it more weight in a rewrite of his cognitive-science books. But he pushes back on the analogy to human learning: a child would need "30,000 years" of listening at an LLM's data scale to produce a first sentence, and children learn interactively, situated in a world — not by ingesting raw text corpora.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Never attribute to malice that which can adequately be explained by stupidity." — Steven Pinker, on Hanlon's razor as applied to bad writing
- "The curse of knowledge... is the difficulty that we all have in knowing what it's like not to know something that we know." — Steven Pinker
- "Generalizations without examples and examples without generalizations are both useless." — Steven Pinker
- "In writing you're wrenched from the context... someone's picking a book up off the shelf and they've never met you and they may be living in a different country. You might be dead." — Steven Pinker
- "The first rule of writing is omit needless words... it's a beautiful example of itself because there are no needless words in it." — Steven Pinker, on E.B. White's *Elements of Style*
- "[LLM output] is so generic and prosaic and you can almost recognize the output of a large language model. It's so banal." — Steven Pinker
- "It would be the equivalent of a child listening to language for 30,000 years before they could put their first sentence clearly." — Steven Pinker, on why LLM training doesn't validate the mind-as-LLM analogy

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I assuming shared context or jargon that a reader "wrenched from the context" wouldn't have?
- Do I actually show drafts to a real outsider before publishing, or do I only imagine what they'd think (which Pinker calls "not good enough")?
- When I reach for an abstraction (framework, system, paradigm), is there a concrete image I could substitute instead?
- If Pinker's "composite face" theory of LLM prose is right — clear but not beautiful — what specifically would I need to add back in (rhythm, alliteration, a genuine visual metaphor) to make AI-assisted writing distinctive rather than average?
- Am I imposing artificial length constraints on my own drafts to force the "omit needless words" effect Pinker describes?
