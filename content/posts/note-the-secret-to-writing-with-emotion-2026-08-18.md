## TL;DR
David Perell interviews writer Ava Huang about how she creates emotional intimacy on the page without cliche or oversharing. Her core method is to distrust any phrase that already feels "packaged" — cliches are pre-digested language that let both writer and reader off the hook — and to instead dig for the specific, personal version of a feeling, on the theory that "the personal is universal." She talks through practical craft (dialogue as distilled essence rather than transcription, minimalism vs. maximalism, writing from mood rather than intellect), the emotional mechanics of publishing (rejection, indifference, hatred, and why criticism is rarely about you), and her own writing process (highlighting instead of note-taking, journaling as the seed of essays, and treating writing as a way to metabolize experience rather than dwell in it).

## Key ideas

- **Avoid cliches on principle.** Her rule: "never write anything that you know is a cliche" — whenever she catches herself reaching for a familiar phrase, she stops and finds another way to say it. Cliches feel "already packaged" and washed of meaning because they're almost copy-pasted from everyone else's writing.
- **The personal is universal.** She believes most people share "the same threads through emotional experience," so writing about her own specific experience — rather than generic, self-help-jargon language — is actually what lets readers connect with it.
- **Difficult emotions are easier to write well than happiness.** Pain and grief are "really easy to analyze," while happiness and contentment are harder to render on the page without going flat or vague.
- **Writers don't need extreme lives to write well.** Using Ann Patchett and Murakami as examples, she argues some writers "have like a pretty serene life" and can dip into emotional extremes in their imagination without living them — discipline and appreciation of experience can substitute for chaos.
- **Dialogue should distill essence, not transcribe reality.** Real conversation, recorded verbatim, "probably wouldn't" make a good novel — good dialogue captures the emotional intensity and point of a moment rather than literal speech (she contrasts Woody Allen's stylized dialogue with more "shockingly real"-feeling prose from writers like Sally Rooney).
- **Know your natural register, then choose whether to fight it.** She says she naturally writes complex, maximalist sentences and consciously edits toward simplicity — "take out everything" that doesn't earn its place.
- **Poetry vs. prose is about primacy of feeling.** She thinks poetry "comes from this kind of very primal" place, more mood-first than intellect-first, and that most writers "need more words" than a poem allows to convey a feeling — hence her own drift from poetry into essays.
- **Writing is deconstruction.** "People write because they ruminate" — the impulse to write comes from wanting to deconstruct experience you can't otherwise make sense of; writing "holds more" than the mind can hold on its own, letting her advance an idea the way a dissertation can't be held entirely in your head.
- **On rejection, indifference, and hatred:** she distinguishes three audience reactions to published work. Indifference ("crickets") is the hardest for most people; actual hatred is rare and usually says more about the reader's own thin skin than about the piece — "people only hate you if you're saying something that's different from theirs."
- **Notes and journaling feed the work.** She's "a big highlighter," not a heavy note-taker — she marks passages to revisit later rather than transcribing full thoughts, and treats her Substack/journal as the raw material that later gets expanded into essays (her essay on "taste" started as a scattered notes page).
- **Repetition plus adjustment, not blind repetition, builds skill.** She's skeptical that raw talent alone explains outcomes; many successful writers from her MFA program weren't the most naturally gifted in the room. "You can attain perfection through extreme repetition," but only if you also make adjustments based on how the work is landing.
- **The muse is a story you tell yourself about your own patterns.** She and a friend debate whether inspiration is something "beyond you" or just "the patterns that your mind creates" — either way, showing up consistently is what creates the conditions for inspiration to arrive.
- **Writing as release, not just record.** She describes writing about something difficult as a way to move through it rather than hold onto it — once she publishes a piece about an experience, "it's actually no longer part of me," because putting it into words makes it safe to let go.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Never write anything that you know is a cliche... whenever I feel myself putting down [something familiar], I just try to like find another way to say it."
- "I really believe that... the personal is universal... there's almost any aspect of my experience [that], if you're a human, nothing human is [foreign]."
- "It's easier to write about difficult emotions... really easy to analyze, whereas happiness... is actually, to me, harder to write about."
- "People only hate you if you're saying something that's different from theirs — it doesn't mean [it's about you]."
- "I think the impulse to write comes from... thinking obsessively about particular things... people write because they ruminate."
- "You can attain perfection through extreme repetition. Like there needs to also be some [adjustment] — I can't just repeat blindly."
- "Once I publish it, [the experience is] actually no longer part of me... because now it's safe."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Which of my own recent sentences would I flag as "already packaged" if I reread them as a stranger?
- Do I write more honestly about pain than about contentment — and if so, what would it take to render a genuinely happy moment with the same specificity?
- When something I publish gets silence rather than praise or criticism, do I treat that as data about the work, or do I spiral the way Huang describes readers doing?
- Am I journaling/highlighting in a way that actually feeds future writing, or just archiving without ever returning to it?
- Where in my own writing am I repeating without adjusting — grinding the same sentence patterns without checking whether they're landing?
