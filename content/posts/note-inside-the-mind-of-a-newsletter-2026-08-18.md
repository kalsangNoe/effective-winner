## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Sam Parr (founder of The Hustle, now Hampton) about how he built a media company on copywriting instinct, ruthless math, and stolen phrasing. Parr's core method: obsess over words the way a copywriter does, test phrases live on people's faces, exploit email over websites because attention is portable, and go where an audience already congregates (Reddit) rather than build traffic from scratch. He also lays out how he trains writers, why "boldness" and speed matter more than polish, and how deep research (Robert Caro, Nick Bilton) creates trust with a reader. The conversation ranges from AIDA copywriting frameworks to rhetorical devices from *Elements of Eloquence* to how he reads and researches company history via the Wayback Machine.

## Key ideas
- **The founding math of The Hustle was a napkin calculation, not a vision.** In 2014 Parr reasoned: get a million email subscribers, email daily, 50% open rate → 15 million reads/month → roughly $2M/month. He pitched this to people (including a media-company CEO) who called it "nonsense," but the math held and became the company's one core metric: opens, not pageviews.
- **Go where the audience already is instead of building traffic from zero.** Rather than fight for organic blog traffic like BuzzFeed, Parr seeded viral blog posts on specific subreddits (self-publishing, Soylent) matched to his target demo (young men), then let it cascade outward. First 150K subscribers came almost entirely from this siphoning strategy.
- **Copywriting is built by testing phrases on people's faces, not writing in isolation.** He'd get on calls and say candidate phrases out loud, watching for a physical reaction ("their eyebrows moved") — like a comedian testing a bit — and kept only the ones that visibly landed (e.g., "business group therapy").
- **AIDA (Attention, Interest, Desire, Action) as the underlying framework** for popups, footers, and onboarding emails — grab attention, build interest with a story, create desire with a concrete benefit, then ask for the action.
- **"The Forgotten Text" — the neglected touchpoints are where the highest-leverage copy lives.** Post-signup thank-you pages and onboarding emails are usually generic boilerplate; Parr wrote an elaborate, personality-driven welcome email (bell rings in the office, staff does push-ups) because most companies waste that moment.
- **Research a company's origin story via the Wayback Machine, not its current state.** Compare it to studying a pro athlete's training at age 13, not their current highlight reel — look at a company's phrasing/positioning right before a funding announcement, when they still had something to prove, not after they'd "made it" (Shopify, Morning Brew examples).
- **Depth of research builds reader trust implicitly, not through credentials.** Citing Nick Bilton's *American Kingpin* (describing the exact direction of shadows/wind when Ross Ulbricht was arrested) and Robert Caro moving to Texas Hill Country for years to write about LBJ — Parr's advice to new writers: don't state your credentials, let the specificity of your observations imply your expertise.
- **Headlines and opening lines matter more than article length.** "That article wasn't too long, your slope wasn't slippery enough" — you have to earn the reader's continued attention line by line; a 1% → 10% click-through improvement is achievable primarily by fixing headlines, not content.
- **Write short (<500 words) or long (2,000+ words); the middle length underperforms.** The Hustle's biggest hits were either ultra-distilled screenshots/tweets or 2,000+ word deep dives (Zach Crockett's Sunday stories); anything in between was "no man's land."
- **Hiring: look at the "bottom fourth of the resume."** Parr recruits by finding people already writing for fun (a great personal headline got Steph hired) and by probing the hobbies/interests section of a resume for evidence of an interesting, entertaining personality — a proxy for good writing.
- **Copy work / copy hour: learn to write by hand-copying great writing.** Modeled on Ben Franklin and Hunter S. Thompson (who retyped *The Great Gatsby*), Parr spent months copying the best sales letters by hand with pen and paper to internalize "texture," and still does 10 minutes of it before writing anything hard.
- **"Bold, Fast, Fun" (tattooed on his arm) is his operating philosophy.** Fast = force accountability by publishing immediately or putting money/stakes down (he voice-memos ideas the moment he has them so momentum doesn't die); Bold = write things that make people say "write that down"; editing, not the first draft, is where the real writing happens ("you got to get the crap out of the way").
- **Knowing what you stand against is as important as what you stand for.** Parr says he under-defined this at The Hustle (hired "artists" who were too slow) and corrected it at Hampton, which deliberately has a masculine, exclusive brand — not to exclude women, but to filter for people aggressive about growth.
- **Elements of Eloquence rhetorical devices he's collecting:** ploce (repeating a word with a different meaning — "forgive us our trespasses as we forgive them that trespass against us"), antithesis ("not because it's easy, but because it's hard"), and merism (naming parts to imply the whole — "ladies and gentlemen," "hook, line, and sinker").

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing process do I skip the "say it out loud and watch the reaction" test that Parr uses for phrasing?
- Am I writing the forgettable, generic version of touchpoints (thank-you pages, first emails, footers) that most people neglect — where's my equivalent "Forgotten Text"?
- Do my pieces fall into the "no man's land" middle length, and would they land harder split into a short distilled version or expanded into a long deep dive?
- What do I stand against, not just what do I stand for — is that missing from how I frame my own work or writing?
- Could researching the early/original version of something (via Wayback Machine or equivalent) give me sharper material than researching its current, polished state?
