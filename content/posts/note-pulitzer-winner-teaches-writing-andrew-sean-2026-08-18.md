## TL;DR
Andrew Sean Greer (Pulitzer Prize, *Less*) describes a writing process built on intuition first, technique second: write scenes out of order like a crossword puzzle, then edit by hunting for images and themes that "echo" each other. He argues great style comes from reading old, out-of-fashion books rather than contemporary bestsellers, that comedy works by being honest about tragedy rather than clever, and that revision is a craft skill closer to a painter's technical fussing than to inspiration. He closes with his central editing mantra — the editor is the patient, not the doctor — and the story of losing his British publisher only to win the Pulitzer ten months later.

## Key ideas

**Editing is pattern-finding, not planning.** Greer's first drafts are "absolutely terrible," written as out-of-order vignettes. His first editing pass just looks for images that repeat (a bird motif, say) and cuts what doesn't rhyme with anything else — "for a novel it all has to rhyme," giving readers the sense the story was for a reason, unlike a friend recounting a pointless dream.

**Writing like a crossword puzzle (his framing, borrowed from Nabokov).** He writes strong scenes he doesn't yet understand the purpose of, then later scenes give him "clues" that connect them, so the book assembles itself rather than being outlined. Planning too much causes either a mid-novel breakdown when the story diverges from the outline, or a book that "feels too calculated."

**Steal technique from old, unfashionable books.** He cites Colette's *Gigi* (ending on an implied proposal, "...", refusing sentimentality), Daphne du Maurier's *Rebecca* (narrator never named), and old French novels' "Madame M—" dash conventions — techniques mostly lost because modern writers, raised on TV, over-narrate every beat of a scene instead of trusting the reader to fill gaps.

**A narrator's voice is assembled from a "pile of books."** Before starting a book he gathers books whose combined voice matches what he's going for, and often rewrites the opening 100 pages 3-4 times (changing gender, POV, tense) until the prose starts coming easily — that ease is how he knows he's found the right narrator.

**Read like a detective, not a fan.** He no longer reads for pure pleasure; he interrogates technique — why Cormac McCarthy drops quotation marks (cold, distancing, wrong for Greer's warmer style), why José Saramago blends dialogue into prose (fairy-tale effect), why Proust builds long sentences that land on a single word after a colon. "I have to know how it's made... I'm going to have to make something in literature later that day."

**Something has to happen on every page.** Following Raymond Chandler (send two men with guns through the door when bored) and Dave Eggers ("a surprise on every page"), Greer treats plot/suspense as the mechanism that earns the reader's trust to reach the "blocks of poetry" — even literary fiction needs page-turning momentum.

**Write to your strength — the "Whitey Ford" lesson.** A grad-school mentor told him, after he stopped showing off with postmodern gimmicks and wrote an honest story, "Whitey Ford always pitched with his left hand" — meaning: stop hiding your real talent (for Greer, emotional vulnerability) behind cleverness.

**Comedy comes from being honest about tragedy, not from wit.** He wrote *Less* as a serious, "tedious self-pitying" novel about a depressed middle-aged gay man until he decided, out of despair, to make fun of him instead — which "opened every door." The funny material comes out fully formed in the first draft ("I'm not a witty person... I'm very much someone who thinks of the right thing to say" too late — *l'esprit de l'escalier*); what revision adds is the setup/payoff structure, e.g. moving a mushroom-trip scene so the character wakes up naked on a rickety "universe appreciation platform" instead of a plain teepee.

**The Pulitzer story.** He wrote *Less* assuming no one would read it, after his previous novel was barely reviewed and he felt "out of the club" as a writer — which freed him to be reckless. His British publisher, Faber, and 11 others rejected *Less* outright; no UK publisher took it until the day after he won the Pulitzer. Michael Chabon told him by phone: "There is no downside, and now you can write any book you want to." Michael Cunningham told him: "Do not waste any time worrying about whether you deserve it."

**"The editor is the patient, you are the doctor"** (via Daniel Handler). A reader/editor can accurately report where the pain is but not diagnose the cure — so take feedback about *what isn't working* seriously, but ignore their proposed fix. His editor Lee Boudreaux suggested cutting the ending of *Less*; instead of cutting it, he reworked the rest of the novel so the ending would land — "her solution was definitely the easiest one, but it was not the right one." Same logic applies to workshop advice: if a story has a mermaid that doesn't fit, the instinct to cut the mermaid usually makes the work more mediocre — the fix may be "more mermaid," not less.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "It should feel like it's... you know what you're doing. I don't know what the theme is yet." — on first drafts
- "They say that you should write on whiskey and edit on soda water."
- "The narrator is not Arthur Less. The narrator is much smarter and funnier... Arthur Less doesn't know it's a comedy... but we're having a good time because the narrator knows how the story ends."
- "What you want to sound is just like yourself. That's the only way to make any kind of art."
- "Comedy is when you decide it's a funny story. Life is not funny. You just decide."
- "There is no champagne room that you're finally invited into where all the VIPs are sitting around... There's no heaven. There's just writers."
- "The editor is the patient... A good editor will not tell you specifically what to do. They will just tell you where it hurts."
- "Or, more mermaid." — his answer to workshop feedback that wants the strange element cut

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own drafts have I cut the "mermaid" instead of committing to it — softened the strange, specific thing because feedback said it didn't fit?
- Am I editing by looking for what "echoes" across a piece, or just fixing sentences line by line without asking what the whole thing rhymes with?
- What's my equivalent of Greer's "pile of books" — the small stack of influences whose combined voice I'm actually trying to write in right now?
- Which of my competencies do I treat as "too easy to count," the way Greer almost didn't count his comic gift because it came without struggle?
- Do I read new work almost exclusively, and would deliberately reading older, out-of-print books surface techniques I've never considered?
