## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Oxford mathematician John Lennox, moving from a theology-of-language argument (the universe is "word-based," from mathematics to the genetic code) into writing craft and finally into AI. Lennox's central AI claim: the race to build superintelligence is literally an attempt to "make God" and be God, the mirror image of the Christian claim that God became human. He distinguishes narrow AI (useful, already here) from the "control problem" anxieties of thought leaders, argues machines simulate but cannot possess intelligence or consciousness because nobody knows what consciousness is, and reads the surveillance/totalitarian scenarios described by secular thinkers like Max Tegmark as eerily parallel to the Book of Revelation's "mark of the beast." He closes by urging scientifically literate Christians to get into AI so they have a seat at the ethics table, since technology is outrunning ethics.

## Key ideas
- **The universe is word-based, which undercuts materialism.** Mathematics ("the most precise language we've got") describes physical law, and the human genome is a 3.4-billion-letter code that functions as a word with meaning. Since in every other human experience words come from minds (even a 4-letter "EXIT" sign implies an author), it's incoherent to say a 3.4-billion-letter word arose by chance and necessity.
- **Structure and "thought flow" as reading method.** His mentor David Gooding, a Septuagint scholar, taught him that ancient texts (e.g., Matthew's gospel) use repeated phrases as structural markers rather than numbered chapters; the discipline is to ask "what does it say" (observe structure/repetition) before asking "what does it mean."
- **Explanation has levels, and scientific explanation doesn't exclude God.** A car engine can be explained via physics/engineering *and* via "Henry Ford" — different, complementary levels. Even Newton's law of gravity doesn't explain what gravity *is* ("hypotheses non fingo"). Citing neuroscientist Iain McGilchrist (*The Matter with Things*) and Jonathan Sacks: "science takes things apart to see how they work; religion puts them together to see what they mean" — 500 years of left-brain dominance has left us knowing how everything works but the meaning of nothing.
- **Information is non-material, which he says is fatal to materialism.** Information rides on a material substrate (ink, paper) but isn't itself material — so non-material entities exist, ending materialism "as far as I'm concerned."
- **Style matters as much as substance; metaphor is a stepping stone to reality, not a retreat from it.** Following C.S. Lewis, metaphors "stand for realities" (e.g., "my heart is broken," Jesus as "the door"). He criticizes people who read Revelation as either pure fantasy or flatly literal — the symbols (the sword from Christ's mouth, eyes of flame) consistently map onto real referents once cross-checked against surrounding scripture.
- **Writing/self-criticism practice:** ask not "how can this be understood" but "how could this be misunderstood" (his example: describing "God as father" to someone whose father was abusive). Pair with Feynman's line that "the easiest person to fool is yourself," and always submit drafts to a ruthless outside editor — his current autobiography editor cuts him for being "episodical" (travelogue with no depth).
- **On AI — narrow vs. general:** Most deployed AI (including ChatGPT) is narrow — one task done brilliantly (e.g., spotting disease in X-rays better than average doctors, or DeepMind solving protein folding in a day versus five PhD-years). The fear from serious people (Geoffrey Hinton, Elon Musk, Max Tegmark) centers on the "control problem" — systems we don't fully understand or control.
- **Tegmark's *Life 3.0* scenario vs. Revelation:** Tegmark describes AI-enabled totalitarian government forcing citizens to wear an Apple-Watch-like bracelet that surveils them and can inject a lethal toxin for stepping "out of line." Lennox says this "parallels almost exactly" Revelation's "mark of the beast" (no buying or selling without the mark, in hand or forehead) — and notes Peter Thiel agreed, in conversation, that tech trends toward surveillance-driven totalitarianism and that scripture anticipates it.
- **The AI race as "building God."** He separates trajectories: (1) enhancing humans genetically to become "gods with a small g" (Harari's framing), and (2) building a silicon-based entity that transcends its makers. Both read as a modern Tower of Babel; he quotes Psalm 115:8 ("those who make them become like them") and notes many drivers of superintelligence research are avowed atheists — his summary line: the biblical story is God became human, AI is the attempt to reverse that and make/become God.
- **What AI is not: conscious or truly intelligent.** He insists AI "simulates" intelligence — it's "sheer predictive computing power" — and cannot be conscious because "nobody knows what consciousness is." He uses ChatGPT for gathering ideas but distrusts it because it can hallucinate "to please me," so he always fact-checks, comparing it to Googling something you don't know.
- **The pastoral warning:** a sermon written by ChatGPT in 30 seconds "the night before" might be technically accurate (e.g., correctly stating the doctrine of the Trinity) but has "no spiritual power," because a machine has no spirit.
- **Call to action:** technology moves faster than ethics, driven partly by a "we shall be as gods" mentality ("if we can do it, we should do it"); scientifically literate Christians should enter AI so they can "sit at the ethics table."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "If we will attribute mind to words of four letters long, it's rather curious when we come to a word of 3.4 billion letters long that we say it happens by chance and necessity. That to my mind is nonsense."
- "Science no more conflicts with God as an explanation for the universe than Henry Ford conflicts with physics, chemistry, and automobile engineering as an explanation for the automobile. They're complementary."
- "We've ended up in a universe where we know how almost everything works, but we know the meaning of nothing." — Lennox summarizing Iain McGilchrist
- "This race for AI super[intelligence] is the race to make God and be God. The biblical message is God became human. It goes in the reverse direction."
- "It doesn't think... it's not real intelligence. And what God has done with human beings is to put intelligence together with consciousness. These machines are not conscious. Nor are they ever likely to be, for a very simple reason: nobody knows what consciousness is."
- "The technology moves much faster than the ethics... it's partly driven by that we shall be as gods. In other words, if we can do it, we should do it — and bother the consequences."
- "Behind every skyscraper there's an even greater ego."
- "How could this be misunderstood?" — his rule for self-editing, which he calls more useful than asking how something could be understood.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Lennox treats "information is non-material" as a knockout blow to materialism — is that actually airtight, or does it just relocate the mystery (what counts as "material" in physics has changed before)?
- His narrow/general AI split is a few years old in framing — how would he revise "most AI is narrow" now that agentic and more general-purpose systems are more common?
- Is the Tegmark/Revelation parallel evidence for anything, or just pattern-matching two authors independently worried about surveillance and control — worth separating the specific prediction (totalitarian control via technology) from the specific mechanism (a "mark")?
- His "how could this be misunderstood" editing rule seems transferable outside religious writing — where would applying it change how I draft technical or persuasive pieces?
- If consciousness is genuinely undefined, does that cut both ways — does it also mean we can't be fully certain AI *lacks* it, rather than only that we can't confirm it has it?
