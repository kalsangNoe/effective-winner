Groupthink isn't caused by team cohesion, as classic theory (Janis) claimed — the actual research shows cohesive teams often decide *better*. The real culprits are unchallenged overconfidence and cultures that punish genuine dissent. Polaroid and Bridgewater Associates are the contrasting case studies.

## Key Concepts
- **Cohesion doesn't cause groupthink — this is a debunked claim.** Re-analysis of the Bay of Pigs decision (Janis's original case study) shows it wasn't made by one stable, cohesive group. Studies comparing successful and failed strategic decisions across Fortune 500 teams found cohesive groups were *not* more likely to suppress dissent — and often performed better.
- **Polaroid: strong "commitment culture" curdled into blindness.** Edwin Land built a mission-driven, tightly-bonded culture that produced early breakthroughs (instant camera, film chemistry) — but the same culture became insular once digital photography emerged. Leaders were overconfident ("customers will always want prints") and marginalized internal dissenters until the company went bankrupt, despite having working digital camera prototypes years before competitors shipped.
- **Bridgewater Associates: dissent as a structural requirement, not a courtesy.** Hires for "cultural contribution" (does this person add a missing perspective?) instead of "cultural fit." Criticism of leadership — including the CEO — is expected to be public; employees can be penalized for *failing* to voice disagreement. An "issue log" asks people to bring problems, not pre-packaged solutions, to avoid solutions-only cultures that skip inquiry.
- **Assigned devil's advocates don't work; genuine dissenters do.** Experiments show a role-played devil's advocate barely moves group confidence (~4% shift); an authentic dissenter with a real minority opinion shifts it far more (~15%) and produces measurably better group decisions — even when the dissenting view itself turns out to be wrong, because it forces real reconsideration of assumptions.
- **"Ask for problems, not solutions."** A solutions-only culture produces advocacy without inquiry — teams converge on an answer before the full picture (often distributed unevenly across team members) has surfaced. This was implicated in NASA's Columbia disaster analysis.

## Details
The Polaroid/Bridgewater contrast is really about whether a strong shared culture is built around *conformity to values* (Polaroid) or *conformity to a process of surfacing disagreement* (Bridgewater). Both are "strong cultures" in the colloquial sense; only one survives market disruption, because only one has a built-in mechanism for detecting when its own founding assumptions have gone stale.
