## TL;DR
Rob Henderson (author of *Troubled*, a memoir about foster care, Yale, and the military) walks through how he actually wrote his book: he over-wrote everything, then cut ruthlessly using a "does this serve my three themes" filter (family, foster care, social class). His central technique is writing from the "voice of innocence" rather than the "voice of experience" — inhabiting the child's mind rather than narrating with adult hindsight — and using emotional intensity (especially shame or fear of looking bad) as the signal for which stories belong in the book. He also describes concrete excavation techniques (smell, music, food, waking at 3am), how he paced emotional highs and lows chapter to chapter, and advice from JD Vance and Tara Westover about resisting the pull toward academic/analytical writing in favor of pure narrative.

## Key ideas
- **The core question isn't "who am I" but "who am I in this story."** Memoir writing forced Henderson to realize a book isn't a loose heap of anecdotes — every story has to tie back to one or two specific themes (for him: family, foster care, social class/mobility).
- **Voice of innocence vs. voice of experience.** He initially planned to write in hindsight (adult Rob explaining why things happened), but realized he had to write each chapter from the perspective of himself at that age — blind to context, not understanding his own decisions — to pull the reader in with him.
- **The "block of ice" approach to drafting.** He wrote a "vomit draft" of every memory he could recall (~100,000 words, a "master doc of everything"), then cut ~20%. Easier to sculpt from a big block than stretch a small one.
- **Trepidation is the selection criterion.** "The more trepidation you have about sharing something, the more that you should put that in your memoir." He kept stories based on gut feeling — if recalling something made him feel shame or vulnerability, that was the signal to keep it, not cut it.
- **Deliberately not making himself look good.** He cut a story about pulling a drowning girl from a pool as a kid because, on reflection, he'd included it out of ego, not because it served the book's themes. He also kept the fights he *lost*, not the ones he won, because those were the ones tied to his life unraveling.
- **Darwinian editing via multiple readers.** He sent drafts to writer friends; if two or more independently flagged the same problem, he cut or fixed it — treating convergent feedback as signal against the inherent subjectivity of writing critique.
- **Pacing as emotional variance.** He opened chapter one with his Yale graduation so readers knew the story had a happy ending before enduring hardship. Within and across chapters he aimed for "high-low-high-low" swings rather than a "misery memoir."
- **Voice evolves with the narrator.** Prose starts plain/unsophisticated (young Rob) and gradually becomes more complex, ending in his current academic voice — mirrored even in citation density, which increases from zero early on to several per page by the end.
- **One sentence can carry a whole world.** "Here's how you make a bed with perfect corners" replaced nine pages on military basic training — a single concrete detail lets the reader infer the rest, rather than needing a full chapter.
- **Excavating buried memories physically.** He listened to a 90s Spotify playlist, ordered discontinued foods/drinks (Dr. Thunder), smelled cookies, bought the same scented candle from his time stationed in Germany, and would wake at 3am to type down resurfacing memories before they faded — all to jog memory associatively rather than just thinking harder.
- **Dialogue must be truthful, not factual.** He couldn't remember exact words from conversations decades ago, but tried to stay faithful to the "spirit" and status dynamics of the interaction (e.g., his own halting, comma-heavy speech as a scared 10-year-old vs. his therapist's clean, adult phrasing).
- **JD Vance and Tara Westover both warned against the academic pull.** Vance told him his vision for the book would change once he started (it did — both intended a sociological, data-driven book that became personal memoir instead). Westover's advice: bringing in statistics mid-story "breaks the spell" for the reader.
- **Westover's other rule: the innocuous details are what wound people, not the dramatic ones.** His sister was unbothered by dramatic family stories but upset over a disputed detail (her exact age in one scene); surprising, "meaningless" details can land harder than the big emotional beats.
- **The preface is an academic compromise, not the memoir's true voice.** Editors wanted stats and research woven throughout; Henderson resisted per Westover's advice, but conceded to front-load the research/credibility case in the preface (and the final two-three chapters) so readers would trust he understood the subject academically, not just personally — while the narrative core stayed unmediated.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "When you're writing memoir, the question isn't who am I, but who am I in this story."
- "The more trepidation you have about sharing something, the more that you should put that in your memoir."
- "It's much easier to build a sculpture from a giant block of ice than to take a little ice cube and turn it into something."
- "Here's how you make a bed with perfect corners" — [on compressing nine pages of basic-training minutiae into a single telling detail]
- "You're going to break the spell for the reader." — Tara Westover, on interrupting narrative with statistics
- "The things that you think will possibly upset people won't, and the things that you think are completely innocuous and meaningless — those are the things that are going to get under people's skin." — Tara Westover
- "When adults let children down, they learn to let themselves down."
- "Read to him — but not because it will expand his vocabulary. Read to him because it will remind him that you love him." — closing line of *Troubled*

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- In my own writing, am I narrating from hindsight (voice of experience) when I should be inhabiting the moment as I understood it then (voice of innocence)?
- Which of my own stories am I keeping because they serve the piece's actual theme, versus keeping because they make me look good?
- Where do I reach for a statistic or abstraction right when a concrete scene would land harder — am I "breaking the spell" for my own reader?
- What's one small, mundane detail (a "perfect corners" moment) that could stand in for a whole chapter of context I'm currently over-explaining?
- Do I have a version of Henderson's "trepidation test" — am I noticing which of my own drafts I'm avoiding writing precisely because they're the ones worth writing?
