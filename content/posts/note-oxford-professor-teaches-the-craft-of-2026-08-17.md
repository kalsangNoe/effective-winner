## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Oxford historian Diarmaid MacCulloch (biographer of Thomas Cranmer and Thomas Cromwell) about how historians actually work: the daily discipline behind decades of research, how to sequence secondary and primary sources, and what separates beautifully written history from "leaden" academic prose. MacCulloch's throughline is that history is a moral discipline, not just a factual one — the historian's job is to defend sanity against lies, especially the comfortable lie of the heroic, victors'-eye narrative. He argues for "yes, but" as the historian's motto, for openly disclosing your own biases, and for treating place, disappointment, and even lunacy as legitimate evidence about the past.

## Key ideas
- **The historian as guardian of sanity.** MacCulloch frames history (and the humanities generally, vs. STEM) as the discipline that makes people good and sane rather than merely capable. He points to examples like demoting Martin Luther King Day and sanitizing museum labels as "wicked" and "insane" acts that history exists to resist. This framing comes from his father, a Church of England rector protected by "Parson's Freehold" (effectively tenure), who taught him that freedom to speak truth was "the essential gift of sanity."
- **Core method: "Be skeptical, and then be sympathetic."** Skepticism keeps you from being credulous about anyone's agenda ("why is this person saying this lie to me?"); sympathy follows because everyone is interesting as a human being. He applies this to sources as much as people — even the American Constitution needs to be read alongside Adams, Jefferson, and Washington's life stories.
- **A strict, unglamorous writing routine.** Work starts no earlier than 8:30am, never continues past 7pm, and never happens in the evening — with a nap built in every day ("unashamed since my 20s"). He credits his doctoral supervisor Sir Geoffrey Elton's rule: divide the day into three and work only two of those thirds, which removes the guilt of not working constantly.
- **Read secondary sources first, then go back to primary ones.** For his biographies of Cranmer and Cromwell, MacCulloch first read the great 17th-century synthesizers of the English Reformation (Bishop Burnet, John Strype) to absorb the received clichés without being dominated by them, then returned to original sources with a skeptical eye toward the conventional story.
- **Good prose is neither purple nor leaden.** He rejects "purple prose" outright, but doesn't despise plain, cliché-driven academic writing either — it's "horses for courses," fine for a technical study of Victorian water supply but not what wins the Wolfson History Prize (which he judges). His personal test for great prose: a sentence that can make him smile or laugh two or three times and still leave him thinking something clever and illuminating.
- **Beautiful writing can still be wrong.** Winston Churchill's history of WWII is "patently distorted" because it inflates his own role — proof that eloquence and accuracy are separate axes, and the most-read history isn't always the most honest.
- **"Yes, but" is the historian's motto.** This is his answer to how you avoid both naive heroism and cheap cynicism: acknowledge the positive story, then add the complicating truth. It's less satisfying than a simple heroic narrative but "more true."
- **Historians and novelists are neighbors, not the same job.** Comparing notes with Hilary Mantel while she wrote the Wolf Hall trilogy, MacCulloch drew the line at "mistakes" vs. "fictions" — he'd correct her that it was the Prior, not the Abbot, of St. Bartholomew's, but left her invented Antwerp romance (explaining Cromwell's illegitimate child) alone, since novelists are licensed to fill gaps that historians must mark with "may have." He praised Mantel's Cromwell as psychologically "totally convincing," especially her insight that Cromwell's devotion to Cardinal Wolsey explains his hatred of Anne Boleyn — undercutting the standard "Protestant reformers as allies" narrative.
- **Sense of place changes what you write.** Citing Robert Caro living three years in Texas to understand LBJ, and his own upbringing in a 16th-century Suffolk church, MacCulloch describes standing alone for 45 minutes inside St. Basil's Cathedral in Moscow (built for Ivan the Terrible) and realizing its terrifying, claustrophobic verticality — invisible from the symmetrical floor plan — was itself evidence of "the mind of a mad czar." He rewrote his script and his book on the spot.
- **"History is written by the victors" is true but incomplete.** Using Bede's account of Christianity's arrival in England, MacCulloch shows how a true story can still hide things — in Bede's case, the outsized role of women (probable abbesses, revealed by richly furnished Christian graves) that his male-bishop-centered narrative erased. Archaeology now supplements and corrects the textual record.
- **Disclose your own bias instead of pretending to neutrality.** "Know thyself" applies to historians as much as subjects; MacCulloch opens his books by stating who he is so readers can calibrate ("he would say that, wouldn't he?"). Failing to disclose bias, he argues, is itself a form of dishonesty.
- **Take lunacy and physical pain seriously as historical causes.** He treats Rosslyn Chapel's builder and Henry VIII's erratic final years (chronic pain, narcissism) as literal explanations, not metaphors — arguing historians underrate "the role of lunacy in history."
- **Teaching historical thinking through a game.** His classroom exercise, "Elizabeth the First Was a Carrot," runs students through 20 statements of increasing ambiguity to test what counts as a checkable historical claim — ending with an invented, plausible-sounding but entirely fake Tudor idiom, to teach students to distrust their own pattern-matching.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I have freedom to speak, and that is the essential gift of sanity. And I wish to spread that sanity to my readers who are constantly assailed by lies, particularly by the powerful." — Diarmaid MacCulloch
- "Be skeptical, and then be sympathetic. And the skepticism stops you being too sympathetic." — Diarmaid MacCulloch
- "My old doctoral supervisor... did say to me, 'Divide your day into three and work for two of those thirds and do not work, work, work in the other one.'" — Diarmaid MacCulloch
- "The historian's motto really ought to be 'yes, but.' ... It's not as simple, and at first sight it may not be so satisfying, but the satisfying part of it is it is true. It is more true than the simplifying versions, the heroic versions of the past." — Diarmaid MacCulloch
- "If novelists never have to say — and it sounds odd if they do — they never have to say 'may have.' They can simply present the story and fill in the gaps. And it may well be that the filling in the gaps is technically wrong." — Diarmaid MacCulloch, on Hilary Mantel
- "It is the mind of a mad czar." — Diarmaid MacCulloch, describing the interior of St. Basil's Cathedral
- "None of us are neutral, and we can't be. We're human beings, and we have passionately held convictions which shape the story which we write." — Diarmaid MacCulloch
- "We underestimate the role of lunacy in history." — Diarmaid MacCulloch

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I giving a "heroic version" of a story instead of the harder, truer "yes, but" version?
- Do I disclose my own biases to readers up front, the way MacCulloch insists a historian must — or am I implicitly claiming a neutrality I don't have?
- MacCulloch reads the secondary literature first to "get the clichés in mind" before going to primary sources — am I skipping that step and either reinventing the wheel or unconsciously parroting received wisdom?
- What's my equivalent of standing inside St. Basil's Cathedral — a place I should physically go before writing about it, rather than researching from a distance?
- Am I too quick to explain a strange historical or present-day actor rationally, when "he was just mad" or "she was in constant pain" might be the more honest, if less satisfying, explanation?
