## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Nat Eliason about the transition from writing online how-to content to writing narrative non-fiction (*Crypto Confidential*) and now a sci-fi novel. The core argument: facts and frameworks don't move people, stories do — and storytelling is a learnable craft with specific, teachable mechanics (promise/progress/payoff, stimulus/response, intention/obstacle, braiding). Nat also argues that over-editing and over-polishing writing (the "Grammarly problem") destroys the voice that makes writing distinctive, and that AI tools are useful only once fed a very specific, well-defined personal style — otherwise they default to generic, over-eager prose.

## Key ideas

**On developing taste and craft**
- **Read bad books on purpose**: Reading books you think are "not good" forces you to articulate *why*, which sharpens your ability to spot what great writers do well. Nat used Pierce Brown's *Red Rising* — fast, commercial, unliterary — as a deliberate counterweight to his own overly restrained style, reading it each morning to "prime" himself before writing.
- **Writer-reading vs. reader-reading**: A writer will always spot flaws in their own work; 99.9% of readers never will. The skill is knowing when to read as a critic and when to read as a normal reader "chilling in bed."
- **The taste gap (Ira Glass)**: Every serious writer goes through a period of recognizing good work without being able to produce it yet. You never fully close the gap — closing it means you stop learning — but leaving it open too long can be corrosive (Nat points to David Foster Wallace's suicide as the dark endpoint of never feeling "good enough").

**The Grammarly problem**
- **Editing tools optimize for a generic "correctness," not your voice.** Grammarly-style advice (shorter sentences, simpler words, standard structure) strips out the very idiosyncrasies that make writing distinctive. Nat deliberately wrote sections of *Crypto Confidential* with no dialogue tags or scene-setting to make readers feel the manic, disoriented headspace of the story — technically "bad" writing by any tool's standard, but effective.
- **Too many editors/cooks = lost style.** Nat argues against hiring excessive editing help for books, comparing it to how private equity "formulaically architects" a company for margin instead of letting a founder's idiosyncrasies drive it.

**Promise, Progress, Payoff (from Brandon Sanderson's lectures)**
- Every story has a **promise** (what you'll get out of this), **progress** (movement toward it), and **payoff** (the delivery). You can get away with a mediocre payoff or a slow middle, but if the promise is weak, nobody reads on.
- *Dune* is the exception that proves the rule — its promise is unclear for 50–70 pages, which is why most publishers passed on it; only readers who pushed through loved it.
- Ayn Rand's dialogue is bad and every character sounds like her — but she can get away with it because the promise ("Who is John Galt?") is one of the strongest ever written.

**Creating stakes: the "death" principle (from the book *Conflict and Suspense*)**
- The number one reason readers drop a book is there's nothing keeping them turning the page. The fastest way to create that pull is to signal **risk of death** — physical, psychological/identity, or societal. Nat's own book opens with him about to lose $100 million of other people's money on page one, deliberately compressing multiple "deaths" (relationship, identity, others' finances) into the first half-page.

**Writing dialogue and scenes**
- Real conversation is 80% filler; fictional dialogue can't be a transcript — it must advance conflict.
- Every scene is about **intention and obstacle** (Aaron Sorkin: "I worship at the altar of intention and obstacle").
- Scenes should end in "**yes, but**" (character gets what they want, with an unforeseen cost) or "**no, and**" (they don't get it, and things get worse) — never a flat resolution. Characters should generally get *further* from their goal as a story progresses, not steadily closer (Christopher Nolan reportedly diagrams this on whiteboards).
- **Redirection and non-verbal response** are cheap, powerful dialogue tools — answering a question with an unexpected non-answer, or having a character simply walk away, creates more tension than a direct yes/no.

**The fire-round checklist for scenes**
- **All five senses**, especially smell — most writers default to sight and sound only; smell is tied to memory and to how humans read emotional cues in others.
- **Keep the inner enemy in mind** — every external conflict needs an internal one (the hero's "refusal of the call") so the character feels human, not just a plot device.
- **How are you changing** — characters must return from the story's events changed, usually by letting some part of their old identity "die."
- **Stimulus → internalization → response** (controversial part: how much internal narration is actually needed — sometimes showing a physical tell, like a bouncing leg, communicates more than narrating the emotion directly).
- **Is the antagonist's opposition clear** — cited example: a Nerd Writer video breaking down *Top Gun: Maverick*'s opening, which repeats the stakes ("get to Mach 10 or lose funding") seven times in a few minutes without feeling repetitive.
- **Emotion, thought, decision, action** — a more granular version of stimulus/response; useful as scaffolding early on, then can be dropped once internalized.
- **Braiding** (not on the original list): a great writer weaves description, action, dialogue, and internalization together in the same sentence-by-sentence flow rather than dumping each in its own block — this is reportedly a key marker editors use to separate good writers from great ones.

**Writing for multiple audiences**
- For *Crypto Confidential*, Nat prioritized feedback from non-crypto readers (his wife Cosette, his sister) over crypto-insider readers, because insiders couldn't tell him what was confusing — they already understood everything.
- The sales team, not just the editor, pushed back on titles for "pitchability" — *Crypto Confidential* replaced Nat's preferred *Is Everyone Getting Rich Without Me* because the original didn't signal the topic clearly enough to sell.

**Writing with AI (Novelcrafter)**
- AI tools are only useful with heavy, specific customization: a ~30,000-word writing sample, an explicit style checklist ("never use an adverb if you don't have to," "show don't tell," avoid clichés like characters running a hand through their hair), and full scene/character context fed in behind a short prompt.
- Claude Sonnet reportedly outperforms Opus for matching his style because Opus "tries too hard to look smart" — an analogy Nat extends to insecure human writers overwriting to prove competence.
- AI's real value is reactive, not generative: the brain is better at critiquing an existing (even bad) idea than inventing one from nothing, so a mediocre AI suggestion is often more useful than a blank page.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The number one reason people drop books is there's nothing keeping them turning the next page."
- "A lot of good writing is in the Grammarly advice you ignore." — Nat Eliason
- "Every scene is about intention and obstacle... What does the person want? What is the obstacle that stands in their way?" (paraphrasing Aaron Sorkin)
- "You can get away with kind of a mediocre payoff and you can get away with being a little bit boring at parts of your progress, but if your promise isn't good, nobody will read your book." (paraphrasing Brandon Sanderson)

- "If I let it write the whole book, the book would be ass. It would be terrible." — Nat Eliason, on AI writing tools
- "Steinbeck said that East of Eden was the book he was preparing for his whole life. So to him, Grapes of Wrath was a warm-up." — Nat Eliason

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I over-polishing toward generic "correctness" instead of protecting a distinctive voice — my own "Grammarly problem"?
- Do my openings establish a strong enough "promise" that a reader would forgive a slow or imperfect middle?
- Am I using all five senses, or defaulting to sight/sound the way Nat says most writers do?
- Could I apply "braiding" (mixing description, action, internalization, and dialogue within the same passage) to non-fiction or essay writing, not just fiction?
- If I fed an AI tool a large writing sample and an explicit style checklist the way Nat does, would it meaningfully improve my drafts — or am I currently using AI tools too generically to get value from them?
