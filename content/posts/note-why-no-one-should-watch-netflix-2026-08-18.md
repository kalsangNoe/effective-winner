## TL;DR
David Perell interviews writer Riva Tez in a wide-ranging conversation about why she avoids passive entertainment (Netflix, mainstream media) in favor of deep, effortful engagement with texts, art, and ideas — Rilke's poetry, Bible manuscripts, the Federalist Papers, handwriting, and painting. Her throughline is that modern culture has traded beauty, authenticity, and hierarchy of taste for flattened, algorithm-optimized, dopamine-driven mediocrity, and that reclaiming a rich inner life requires deliberate friction: reading repetitively, writing by hand, sitting alone with your thoughts instead of reaching for a screen. She's also skeptical of scientism ("the church of graphs") and argues that some truths — about beauty, faith, or consciousness — resist empirical proof and shouldn't be dismissed for that reason. The conversation closes with her case for a classical education (Latin, Greek, the Odyssey, Euripides) as a corrective to a culture that has lost its sense of hierarchy, craft, and long time horizons.

## Key ideas
- **Passive entertainment as a symptom, not a preference.** Riva says she "started watching Netflix" essentially never — she doesn't seek out passive entertainment because she has "so many things I want to learn," and is baffled that people come home and "just zone out" rather than read or research.
- **Against "the church of graphs."** She coined this phrase for the reflexive demand to scientifically validate everything (citing a viral essay about Nietzsche's walking habits needing a study to "prove" walking is good). She argues some wisdom "has been bounded in history" through generations of accumulated practice and doesn't need a p-value — citing medieval monks who intuited germ theory long before it was formally proven.
- **Occam's razor, misused.** She notes William of Ockham was "a hardcore theologian" whose principle ("the simplest explanation... is a miracle") has been "bastardized" by people who invoke it to justify rationalism while ignoring that its author's simplest explanation was often God.
- **Faith and beauty resist justification.** "You can't reason your way into some justification... you just know it's beautiful." She frames her own faith similarly — not derived from argument but from direct experience, comparing it to standing before the Duomo in Florence, built by craftsmen with no ROI horizon within their own lifetime.
- **Manuscript research as a hobby.** She's spent months investigating the Codex Sinaiticus (the so-called "oldest New Testament," discovered by Tischendorf at St. Catherine's Monastery), questioning whether it's really from the 4th century — not to refute Christianity but out of pure curiosity, "just gonna... see what gets uncovered."
- **Creativity comes in obsessive cycles, not on demand.** She describes "manic creative energy" that arrives unpredictably and can't be forced — echoing Rilke's idea of the poet as a vessel for something larger ("a genie... some special metaphysical inspiration"). She's learned, with age, to let it "come cyclically" rather than fighting the fallow periods.
- **Solitude and boredom as prerequisites for insight.** Her best ideas surface "in bed" or while walking, in a near-meditative state where scattered thoughts "solidify" — she contrasts this with a culture where people can't be alone without reaching for their phones.
- **Authenticity over polish.** On her viral essay about the West "repackaging failure as democratic progress" and being drowned in "mass packaged mediocrity porn" — she wrote it in one sitting, unedited, because she cared intensely; she argues most writing today is over-professionalized and drained of real feeling (contrasting Henry Miller and Bukowski with sanitized "be kind" contemporary writing).
- **Objective beauty and natural hierarchy.** She rejects egalitarian aesthetics ("there's no such thing as beauty... everything must be the same") as a denial of real hierarchies — some things (a flower's mathematical symmetry, a Monet) are simply more beautiful than others, and pretending otherwise is a Nietzschean "last man" flattening of value.
- **Handwriting and craft as resistance to disembodied technology.** Raised in a school system requiring calligraphy/fountain pens through age 18, she argues that writing by hand — and the "best mistakes" a typewriter preserves that a delete key erases — keeps you connected to the work in a way typing doesn't.
- **On consciousness and AI:** She's skeptical of confident, dogmatic claims about consciousness or intelligence in either direction — noting 95% of the universe is unexplained "dark matter"/"dark energy" — and worries that AI researchers anthropomorphize systems using loaded terms ("agency," "intelligence") nobody has actually defined.
- **The case for classical education.** Her ideal curriculum centers spelling/grammar, Latin and Greek, and texts like the Odyssey, Euripides, and the Federalist Papers — arguing that reading the American founders' actual debates ("grounded in values," argued in newspapers) is humbling next to the shallowness of political discourse in 2020.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own life am I substituting "the church of graphs" — demanding empirical proof for things I already know experientially?
- Riva ties craft and beauty to long time horizons (JP Morgan's library, cathedral builders). What am I building right now with a similarly long horizon, versus what's optimized for a next-week ROI?
- Her creative process is cyclical and unforced ("let it happen when it happens") — how much of my own resistance to writing comes from fighting a fallow period instead of waiting it out?
- She distinguishes passive consumption (Netflix, doomscrolling) from active curiosity-following (manuscript research, Federalist Papers). What's the honest ratio of active-to-passive time in my own week?
- Handwriting and physical friction (fountain pens, typewriters) are framed as a corrective to disembodied digital work — is there an equivalent friction worth reintroducing into my own workflow?
