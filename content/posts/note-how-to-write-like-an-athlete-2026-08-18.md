## TL;DR
Jimmy Soni (author of *The Founders*, a book about PayPal, and a biography of Claude Shannon) walks through the entire mechanical and psychological process behind writing dense, research-heavy nonfiction while working a full-time job as a speechwriter. The throughline is that he treats writing like professional athletic training — reps, recovery, discipline, and explicit systems — rather than as a mystical creative act. He details his research and note-taking infrastructure (Scrivener, Google Docs/Sheets, Otter AI), his tricks for beating writer's block (voice typing, writing on his phone), his editorial philosophy (cut for surprise and delight, write with warmth, apply "the Apple model"), and what he took from mentors like Robert Caro and Walter Isaacson. It closes on a personal note about the letter he wrote to his daughter in the book's acknowledgements.

## Key ideas

**Writing upward vs. downward** — Soni distinguishes two modes: experts "write downward," straining to make their existing mastery decipherable to others. He writes "upward" — as a self-described "better than average charlatan" who knows nothing about a subject going in (PayPal, Claude Shannon) and takes the reader along his own learning journey. This is deliberate: because he doesn't know the terminology going in, neither does his reader, so he's forced to make it accessible.

**Finding the idea: the empty space on the bookshelf** — He keeps a running Google Doc of "books I should write someday." A project becomes real when he can visualize the two books that would sit on either side of his on a shelf and no one has written the one in between. With PayPal, everyone had covered Musk, Thiel, and Levchin's *current* fame but no one had gone back to the 1998–2002 origin period — an "entrepreneurial" instinct of seeing something the world needs and deciding to be the one who builds it.

**Research: find "Ed," not just the famous names** — Soni's core research philosophy is that famous subjects give rehearsed, over-repeated answers, while peripheral figures who've never been interviewed give the real texture. His example: he found "Ed Bogas" (a musician and chess player) on an old PayPal cap table, cold-called him, and got the story of how Peter Thiel's ferocious intensity at chess convinced Bogas to write PayPal's first seed check — a story Thiel himself would never remember or volunteer.

**The information-management system** — Google Sheets tracks interviewee names, dates, and file links; every file is duplicated to the cloud and a thumb drive; Otter AI transcribes at a flat fee. After every interview he immediately free-writes impressions in a "post-interview" Google Doc and records a voice memo of his reactions, because memory of vividness decays fast — deliberately capturing details (like Thiel secretly checking PayPal's market cap mid-interview) that never make the recorder but shape the story.

**Writing like an athlete: the funnel from raw material to book** — He wrote ~470,000 words of raw material to produce a 170,000-word book (PayPal is ~60,000 words of content) — roughly a 3:1 cut. Process: dump everything into Scrivener folders organized chronologically (1998–2002); thin folders signal under-researched periods. Then copy-paste ~9,000 words per chapter into Google Docs and "sculpt" it down to ~5,500 — avoiding the blank page by always starting with too much material, not too little. Each chapter went through ~13 Google Docs drafts and ~5 PDF passes (17–18 revisions total).

**Beating writer's block and fear**
- *Google Docs voice typing*: he'd cover his screen with a manila folder and dictate scenes without seeing spelling/grammar errors, removing the anxiety of watching himself write badly in real time.
- *Writing on the phone*: forces shorter sentences (form factor), and switching apps takes deliberate effort (unlike opening a new browser tab), which reduces distraction. He did much of his best editing in bed on his phone.
- *Compression via voice*: after a first voice-typed draft, he'd retell the same story in 2 minutes, then 45 seconds — people compress much more naturally in speech than in text, and that forced compression sharpens the writing.

**Studying a model book obsessively** — Soni read Brad Stone's *The Everything Store* (about Amazon) 16–17 times cover to cover, including 10 minutes every morning for 3.5 years before writing PayPal. The point wasn't to copy answers but to copy the *questions*: Stone's deep dig into where the name "Amazon" came from taught Soni to obsessively chase where the name "PayPal" came from.

**The Apple model: surprise and delight** — Borrowing Steve Jobs's obsession with unboxing experience, Soni tries to put "a gem on every page or every other page." His example: transitioning out of a funding-round scene with the joke "funding secured" (a nod to Musk's infamous tweet) instead of a flat question — cutting anything stale that's "already been said by 14 people before."

**Warmth in prose** — Drawing on William Zinsser's *On Writing Well*, warmth comes from specific word choice (e.g., "soft" vs. "smooth") that makes the reader feel inside a character's head rather than lectured at. He deliberately wrote the 19-year-old Elon Musk — anxious, broke, too shy to talk to anyone in the Netscape lobby — with warmth, because that vulnerability is what makes a "titan" human. He notes warmth isn't universal: investigative writing about something like Theranos should stay dry and fact-driven instead.

**Fact-checking as an editorial partner, not just a safety net** — He paid a fact-checker (Ben Kallen) who spent 6 months verifying every footnote against a documented source, catching wrong quotes and wrong years, and later became a sounding board for editorial judgment calls (what belongs in the footnote vs. the main text).

**Writing is a team sport** — He texted paragraphs to his friend Justin every morning for five and a half years for a simple reaction (even just an emoji), used conversation as a low-stakes way to test which ideas land before committing them to the page, and credits editors, a fact-checker, and outside readers the way Michael Jordan needed Phil Jackson.

**Fighting research paralysis** — Coach Lauren Zander made him commit to sending her 1,000 words a day for ~100 days, with a real financial penalty ($200 to a cause he hated) for missing a day — "if there weren't any consequences for overdosing on research, I could just research until the cows come home." He also leaned on his book contract's deadline: "humans are best when they're in harness."

**Lessons from Isaacson and Caro** — Isaacson told him to "go for broke" with his endnotes — use them to show the reader the work, not just to hit a word count. From Caro he took "turning over every page" (exhaustively working every source) and interviewing peripheral figures to understand the core of a story — Soni physically walked the route between two PayPal-era office buildings to understand the geography the way Caro walked LBJ's route in Washington.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "I'm a better than average charlatan who ventures into a field, knows nothing, and the goal is to take the reader along the learning journey with me because I know nothing and I'm not an expert." — Jimmy Soni
- "You find those stories when you interview people who are not on the front pages... the actual substance of making something work happens at all levels." — Jimmy Soni
- "If you start to think of your writing process as an athlete, you treat it very, very differently. You do reps. You do sets. You think about recovery. You think about training." — Jimmy Soni
- "Your job as a sculptor is to chisel the stone and find the statue that's already within it... it takes writing from a process of creation to a process of subtraction and refinement and reduction." — Jimmy Soni (paraphrasing Michelangelo)
- "It's possible to get too obsessed with the tools and the techniques, but a lot of the tools and techniques actually solve an important problem." — Jimmy Soni, on writing on a phone

- "Humans are best when they're in harness." — quoted by Jimmy Soni, attributed to Harry Lodge
- "It's really easy to find friends who make you feel good. It's much harder to find friends who make you better." — Jimmy Soni, from his letter to his daughter

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's my equivalent of "the empty space on the bookshelf" — is there a piece I keep assuming someone else has already written that actually hasn't been written?
- Do I capture post-interview/post-conversation impressions immediately, or do I let vivid details decay before I write them down?
- Where in my own writing process am I disguising avoidance (endless research, endless outlining) as diligence — and what real consequence could I install to force output?
- Am I writing "downward" (from expertise, straining to be understood) or "upward" (learning alongside the reader) — and does my current project call for the other mode?
- Who is my "Justin" — the low-stakes daily reader who can tell me, in real time, what's landing and what isn't?
