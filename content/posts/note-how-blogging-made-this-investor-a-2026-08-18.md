## TL;DR
David Perell interviews VC and author Chris Dixon (Read, Write, Own) about how a disciplined weekly blogging habit in the late 2000s launched his career, and how that same instinct for compression and clarity shaped a three-year process of writing his book. Dixon argues that long-form writing remains a wildly underused career hack, that social media's recency bias has killed the "middle" of the internet (blogging, magazines), and that AI will accelerate that same death-of-the-middle pattern — rewarding only the highest-end, most human, most authentic writing. The back half turns to business models for writers: why he wrote a book instead of another blog post, the "core idea" / earned-secret framework for choosing what to write about, and why creators should lean into humanity and firsthand knowledge as AI commoditizes competent-but-generic writing.

## Key ideas

**Blogging origin story**
- Dixon started blogging around 2008, largely unread for almost a year, until Fred Wilson linked to him and traffic (and a comment-section community) followed.
- His personal discipline: roughly one post a week, each one a single concept distilled from that week's conversations — he found the "sweet spot" was about four paragraphs; longer posts got less attention, shorter ones couldn't fully state an idea.
- Over about four years he wrote ~500 posts; only ~10 went viral and are remembered, which he calls a "self-regulating machine" — boring posts just get ignored, so there's little downside to trying.
- His famous one-liners ("come for the tools, stay for the network," "big thing starts out looking like a toy," "don't be evil to can't be evil") came out of live conversations, not solitary phrase-hunting — "it kind of came through intellectual sparring."

**Writing as a career hack, still underused**
- Blogging pulled him from a minor-league tech scene (New York, an obscure security startup he'd sold) into the top of Silicon Valley and eventually a major VC firm.
- He expected everyone would figure out this hack and start blogging their way to a profile; instead the "art form" faded outside a few holdouts (Tyler Cowen, Fred Wilson).
- His pitch to early-career people, especially in AI: you can become a high-profile thinker in ~2 months by writing a series of genuinely interesting blog posts — something short-form tweets and TikToks can't replicate because complex ideas "simply cannot be stated in short form in the right way."

**Where the simplicity/clarity habit comes from**
- Partly his philosophy training at Columbia (analytic philosophy tradition — Frege, Russell, Wittgenstein): "the best philosophy is just a new idea," expressed with almost no jargon or footnotes.
- Partly engineering: economy and modularity. His book editor noted the book is eccentrically modular — readable cover to cover but also in 3–4 page chunks, with internal cross-references like hyperlinks.
- A formative influence: his English-professor parents editing his college writing with a red-pen shorthand for "pretentious diction" — always choose the simpler word.

**Editing ear vs. writing ability**
- Dixon says his "ear" for good/bad writing was long ahead of his ability to diagnose *why* something was off — writing the book closed that gap, sharpening his ability to instantly name the problem (wrong topic sentence, misplaced clause, bad word choice).
- His default rhetorical structure per section: concrete anecdote → main argument → supporting arguments → steelman the counterarguments → answer them → tie back to the opening.

**The book's editing process (multi-year, iterative)**
- First two-thirds drafted in 1–2 months; it was too abstract, too passive-voice, too reliant on "tell not show," and leaned on "crutch words" (genuine, strong, very) doing work that concrete explanation should do.
- ~20 readers (including a professional writer/editor, technologists, lawyers) did paragraph-level Google Doc comments; comment volume was so high Google Docs would start bugging out and he'd have to fork a fresh copy every two weeks.
- A major rewrite stripped adjectives/adverbs and converted passive to active voice by actually researching who-did-what (e.g., "the internet was built to be decentralized" → naming Vint Cerf, Tim Berners-Lee).
- A later rewrite reworked how he handled counterarguments — from "here's his argument, he's wrong" to genuinely steelmanning critics like Moxie Marlinspike and showing what should be conceded.
- He hired an independent fact-checker (Laura Fox) who caught wrong dates/attributions and, as a byproduct, surfaced a great quote from the old "Read/Write Web" blog manifesto that ended up in the book.
- Two weeks alone in a rented beach house to process all reader comments — followed immediately by "despair" on a fresh read, discovering the comment-by-comment fixes had wrecked the overall structure, requiring another ~4-month restructuring pass.
- Because of repetition/omission risk across 200+ pages, he could only do a truly fresh read of the manuscript about once every two weeks.

**Why write a book instead of more blog posts**
- Business books are mostly compromised: authors are either constrained in what they can honestly say about their own projects, or the books aren't really written by them. "Every project that involves a lot of people over a long time is a giant series of [expletive] shows" — an honest account of that is rarely publishable.
- Two real advantages of the book format: (1) some ideas are already maximally compressed and can't be chopped into blog posts — the book is 213 pages of dense, chronological argument; (2) books cross into a different cultural register — they're "meme delivery vehicles" and can produce what he (borrowing from Spider-Verse) calls "canon events" in a reader's life, the way certain books redirected his own career into philosophy and tech.
- Motivation for the title *Read, Write, Own*: mirrors his three-era framing of internet history (read era → write era → own era) and was chosen for accessibility over insider terms like "web3."

**Business models / "core idea" framework**
- Peter Thiel's "secret" concept, reframed as an "earned secret": good things to write about are counterintuitive and won by lived experience, not idle observation.
- Dixon's advice to people unsure what to write: "their ignorance is your opportunity" (riffing on Bezos's "your margin is my opportunity").
- Perell's parallel framework, "your core idea": find the message a 20-year domain expert already knows is true but hasn't written down; the bottleneck isn't ideas, it's writing being hard plus a "cold start distribution problem" for people with no existing audience.

**Death of the middle, and what AI will do to writing**
- Pattern from retail (internet killed JCPenney/Sears, rewarded Amazon/Walmart at one end and Gucci/LVMH at the other) applies to media: blogging and magazines (Sports Illustrated shutting down) died in the middle, while long books and short tweets/TikToks survived at the barbell's ends.
- Dixon's first-pass model: AI will do the same to writing — "death of the middle" for competent-but-generic content, while top authors/publications and short-form both hold up, because human connection, community, and status persist (his analogy: chess got worse than computers 20 years ago, yet human chess is more popular than ever to watch).
- His practical formula for AI-resistant writing, given directly to an entrepreneur: (1) a genuine insight, ideally one where AI's "consensus" output disagrees with you; (2) deep firsthand knowledge; (3) a layered personal story — because "humans like humans."
- He tried ChatGPT while drafting the book; it wasn't his voice and wasn't the quality he wanted, though a friend ran the finished text through a plagiarism/AI-detection tool and it scored as clearly human-written.

**Recency bias and the internet's unrealized promise**
- Revisits Kevin Kelly's "1,000 True Fans" as the internet's original, mostly unrealized promise — that removing intermediaries would let creators serve niches directly instead of chasing mass audiences.
- Instead, platforms reinserted themselves as intermediaries and, in Dixon's view, made things worse than pre-internet: authors get ~15% from a book, YouTube shares ~45%, Spotify ~30%, but "the main social networks take 100%."
- He calls recency-bias algorithms his single biggest source of anger about the current internet — engagement-optimized feeds favor what people want "in the same way people want sugar and processed foods," not what serves them over a longer time horizon.
- His personal counter-habit: a self-imposed rule to read 50 pages of a book every day, treating books as "the protein of the mind" and social media as "sugar."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's my equivalent "earned secret" or "core idea" — the counterintuitive thing I know from lived experience that isn't obvious to most people?
- Am I writing in the death-of-the-middle danger zone (competent but generic), or am I pushing toward one edge of the barbell — either maximal compression/authority or maximal personal/human specificity?
- Do I have a real feedback loop (readers, an editor, a "close the gap between ear and ability" practice) or am I only ever reading my own first-instinct reaction to my drafts?
- Which of my posts/ideas are already too compressed to chop into shorter pieces, and might actually deserve book-length treatment instead of another post?
- Am I building any structural defense against recency bias in my own reading and writing habits (e.g. a page-count rule, revisiting old work) the way Dixon does with his 50-pages-a-day rule?
