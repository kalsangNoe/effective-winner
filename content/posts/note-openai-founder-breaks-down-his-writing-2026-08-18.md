## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Sam Altman about how he actually writes and thinks, not about AI hype. Altman's core claim is that writing's real value isn't the finished prose — he says outright "writing is not my gift" — but that it's the best tool he's found for externalizing and stress-testing vague thinking: you can't hide messy logic once you have to stare at it on a page. He traces this belief through OpenAI's planning documents (which have shrunk from 25 pages to a half page over time), his failed attempt at blogging and novel-writing, and lessons from Paul Graham and Peter Thiel. He's optimistic about ChatGPT as a tool that lowers the "activation energy" of writing and helps people find and sharpen an idea's "seed," but skeptical it will produce good storytelling or replace the human origin story readers want from a book.

## Key ideas
- **Writing as externalized thinking, not communication.** Altman: "I think of writing is sort of externalized thinking... I still if I have like a very hard problem... have not found anything better to do than to like sit down and make myself write it out." He writes mostly for himself, then for small internal groups, and "public at this point very rarely."
- **Clear communication is downstream of clear thinking.** He argues unclear writing is "a symptom of unfocused thinking for the most part" — the hard part is getting the idea clear, not phrasing it well.
- **OpenAI's planning docs got shorter over years, not longer.** Every ~2 years he's written a document called "our plan": the first was ~25 pages, then 15, then 4, and now he thinks "we could do like a half page version" — framed as "a great side of progress."
- **He's not a naturally gifted writer, and is at peace with that.** He started his blog and once wanted to be a novelist (mostly for "the romantic life of it," smoking in a Paris café) but concluded "it's not my calling in life... I'm not a very good writer and I'm not going to be a blogger and that's okay" — because the exercise still taught him he could write to clarify his own thinking.
- **What Peter Thiel and Paul Graham taught him.** Thiel: rare combination of "very interesting things to say and very interesting ways to say them" — most people only manage one. Thiel's answer to "biggest investing mistake" was not investing in Facebook's Series A/B, illustrating a "quadruple down on rare good ideas" philosophy. Paul Graham taught him mostly by example — "clarity, precision, density," "no posturing," writing that "doesn't waste your time."
- **How he uses ChatGPT for writing.** Not to generate ideas, but as "a super thesaurus" when stuck on a word or phrase, and to amplify a "seed" idea once he already has a distinct point of view: "if chat GPT disagrees with me... then chatgbt can help me find examples and stories things that amplify and help grow the initial seed." He explicitly separates two student cases he watched: one who had ChatGPT write the whole essay ("a real like what have we done moment"), versus one using it to get unstuck and generate more ideas — "far better than I think anybody would have done on their own."
- **Anxiety blocks original thought.** Citing an unattributed quote — "most people can't even let themselves think the interesting thoughts much less say the interesting ideas" — he says background anxiety and constant self-criticism make it hard to explore an idea before worrying what others will think.
- **His note-taking system, in detail.** A spiral notebook (so pages rip out easily), lies flat, hard front/back cover, pocket-sized; he burns through one every two to three weeks and never has a "completed" notebook because he rips pages out and discards them. Preferred pens: Uniball Micro5, or a Muji 36/37 in dark blue ink.
- **On teaching writing in the ChatGPT era.** Recounts a creative-writing teacher's exercise: students overwrite with metaphors and flowery language; cutting a 10-page draft to one page reveals "there was like no story at all." His point: technical prettiness and story are separate skills, and tools like ChatGPT should be aimed at "can we make it easier to get both" — pointing to Twilight as "quite interesting story, horrible writing."
- **He doesn't think AI will kill human writing.** "I don't see any evidence whatsoever that AI seems to be killing writing." He predicts the most popular novel of "20207" [2027, mis-transcribed] will still carry a human writer's name, because part of the pleasure of a great book is the felt connection to the person who wrote it — a feeling he doesn't expect AI writing to replicate short of full superintelligence.
- **What he wants from future ChatGPT: lower activation energy for writing.** He wants to write a "10 page thing" in the fifteen minutes of an Uber ride instead of needing hours of uninterrupted focus in the right mood — volume up, stakes down.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I think of writing is sort of externalized thinking... I still if I have like a very hard problem... have not found anything better to do than to like sit down and make myself write it out."
- "It's harder to hide really messy thinking when you have to actually write it down and look at — like stare at it."
- "Writing is not my gift and I'm okay with that — like writing is super valuable to me as a tool for thinking, for communicating internally with the org."
- "The really good ideas are rare, and when you find one you should quadruple down on it and it should be the only thing you push on." (on the Peter Thiel philosophy)
- "I think unclear communication is a symptom of unfocused thinking for the most part."
- "I don't see any evidence whatsoever that AI seems to be killing writing... I don't think Paul Graham is sitting around being like AI is gonna kill my writing."
- "When I finish a great book the first thing I go do is I want to know about the writer, I want to know their life story — and I don't think I'll ever have that feeling toward AI writing."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I writing to communicate, or am I writing to find out what I actually think — and does my process (drafting alone, editing, revising) actually match whichever goal I claim?
- Altman's planning docs shrank from 25 pages to half a page over years — is my own writing trending toward density over time, or am I still padding drafts the way his early team did?
- Where in my own work am I using AI to "expand a seed" I already have versus letting it generate the seed itself — and can I tell the difference in the output quality, the way Altman could watching the two students?
- Do I have a physical/ritual system (like his spiral notebook) for capturing raw, disposable thinking before it has to be "good," or am I self-editing too early because everything I write is already in a polished document?
