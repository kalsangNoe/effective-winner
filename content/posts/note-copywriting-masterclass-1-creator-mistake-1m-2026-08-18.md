## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Alex Hormozi about his writing system for the "$100M" book series (1M+ copies sold). Hormozi describes a highly disciplined process: outline (table of contents) first, write fast and messy, then "crunch" through ~10-19 drafts, cutting anything that isn't "operationalizable." He frames persuasion around two levers — promise and pain — and argues pain, delivered with acute specificity, is what actually moves people to buy. He also names the single biggest mistake he sees creators make: building new products for their existing audience instead of building more audience for one product, which fragments effort across too many businesses.

## Key ideas

**Process**
- **Table of contents is the real outline, done first.** He calls it "the hardest thing I spend my time on" — once it exists, "that's basically the game plan." He shows Perell the actual messy draft ToCs for $100M Leads, which went through many complete rewrites (the book was originally organized around "leverage" before that theme got cut almost entirely).
- **Words first, doodles last.** He writes prose first, marks spots for illustrations with placeholder notes, then does rough doodles, and only finalizes artwork at the very end because paragraph order still shifts.
- **~10-19 drafts, "coats of paint."** $100M Leads took 19 drafts and was restarted from scratch around draft 12 after socializing it with ~10 readers. Editing is mostly "crunching things down" — fewer words, simpler words — until removing anything more would hurt the substance.
- **Two-thirds discovery, one-third retrieval.** Writing isn't just transcribing what he already knows; most of the value comes from resolving apparent contradictions between two ideas he believes are both true ("that's the most exciting part").

**Filtering ideas: validity and utility**
- **Validity = is it true, and in how many situations. Utility = is it useful.** Example: "sometimes things happen and sometimes things don't" is valid but useless; sales/marketing "lore" is often useful but not valid (works in one scenario, not others). He aims for frameworks that are both — the Value Equation (fast, easy, risk-free) is his example of one that's survived years of people trying to "break" it.
- **MECE as the core structuring tool** (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) — borrowed from consulting. He uses "more, better, different" as a go-to MECE frame and constantly tests frameworks by trying to find an example that doesn't fit; if he can't break it, it's done.
- **The "operationalizable" filter.** His top cutting criterion: "what can I tell someone to do — if I can't change their behavior, they cannot learn." Learning is defined as behavior change; if content doesn't change what someone does, it doesn't belong in the book. This is why his books read as low on theory, high on action.

**Persuasion / copywriting**
- **Promise and pain are the two levers of persuasion**, but pain is underused. "Pain is what motivates a lot of people to take action," while most people writing sales copy overemphasize promise.
- **Sell at the point of greatest deprivation, not greatest satisfaction** — his steak-house analogy: offering a second steak right after someone's eaten one fails; offering two steaks to someone walking in starving works. He extends this to timing offers around a *newly created* problem (e.g., a marketing agency that solves leads should sell the next problem — handling those leads — not push more of the same).
- **"The pain is the pitch" in practice: describe pain with extreme specificity, in the prospect's own language and experience.** His example: writing his wife's weight-loss sales page by imagining exact sensory moments (chafing between her thighs in a coverall, avoiding photos, standing in the back row) rather than writing his own less-compelling story. He argues specificity of pain description is what signals expertise and earns trust — "you can't know that pain this well and not be able to deliver."
- **Book cover/title/subtitle were extensively A/B tested.** "Leads" beat "promotion," "advertising," and "marketing" as the key word; "strangers" narrowly beat "more people" (71% to 29%); concision wasn't what won.

**On creators and business**
- **The #1 creator mistake: building new products for your audience instead of building more audience for your product.** Success from one product gets misread as "audience is proven, now sell more things" — but the thing that made money was the audience-building activity, not the product. Result: creators end up running "six businesses" that are each individually huge in potential but starved of attention and audience, when they should have kept expanding reach behind one consumable/recurring product.
- **10x the effort → 100x-1000x the word of mouth.** He compares it to gold medal vs. fourth place: "the real difference is small, the practical difference is enormous," and best returns come at the very end of the work (the "16th coat of paint"). He ties this to Naval's idea that technology "democratizes consumption and consolidates production" — the internet raises the bar to stand out but also the size of the reward for the person who does.
- **He "back-tests" for evergreen writing** rather than trying to predict the future — deliberately avoiding naming specific platforms (Facebook, Instagram) in a book about advertising so the underlying principles (people want fast, easy, risk-free) hold decades later, the way they would have 2,000 years ago.
- **Books are the "gateway drug," but content comes first.** He deliberately prices/sells the books cheap because he wants them to function as durable trust assets for the ~99% of readers who'll never buy anything else — "the ultimate lead magnet should be a timeless asset," not a disposable funnel piece.

**On teaching writing (final riff)**
- If asked to design a UNLV writing class: start with definitions of terms, then the objective ("why are we writing"), then rules of writing (as few words as possible, use the simpler word, vary sentence rhythm — short short long), then heavy constrained practice with fast feedback via X/Twitter and the Hemingway app. He optimizes for maximum comprehension, not maximum word concision, and deliberately uses "lower grade language."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I write the way I would like to read." — Alex Hormozi, on why his chapters follow the same structure (story → short description → examples → notes).
- "If you can't remember the lesson you might as well not have lived it and learned it." — Hormozi, on why he keeps a spreadsheet of ~600 life stories.
- "I have very little loyalty to my ideas... I'm absolutely married to the truth, zero about how we get there." — Hormozi.
- "You make an offer at the point of greatest deprivation, not at the point of greatest satisfaction." — Hormozi.
- "I think that if you can accurately describe a prospect's pain in their own language, in their own experiences, you can persuade them to buy whatever your product is." — Hormozi.
- "If you put in 10 times more work into a book, because the quality is better, you end up with 100x or 1000x the word of mouth... it's kind of the difference between gold medal and fourth place. The real difference is small, the practical difference is enormous." — Hormozi.
- "The number one creator mistake is building new products for their audience rather than building more audience for their product." — Hormozi.
- "I want to build assets, not magazines with hard covers." — Hormozi, on why he obsesses over making his books evergreen.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I optimizing for promise when pain (described with real specificity) would move the reader more?
- Do I have an equivalent to Hormozi's "operationalizable" filter — a rule that forces me to cut ideas that don't change what the reader does?
- Am I tempted to launch a new product/piece for an audience I've already built, when the better move is deepening reach behind what already works?
- Could I test titles/headlines/subheads the way he tested "leads" vs. "advertising" vs. "marketing" — with real A/B data instead of gut feel?
- His validity/utility split (true vs. actually useful) — which of my own frameworks or claims are valid but not useful, or useful but not fully valid?
