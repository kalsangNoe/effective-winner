Voicing a novel idea to a skeptical audience is a distinct skill from having the idea in the first place. The research points to counterintuitive tactics: lead with weaknesses, repeat yourself, and pick your audience by track record rather than by friendliness.

## Key Concepts
- **Lead with your idea's weaknesses ("Sarick effect").** Entrepreneur Rufus Griscom pitched investors and Disney with slides titled "Here's why you shouldn't invest/buy." It disarmed skeptical audiences, made him look smarter (a study found critical reviewers rated as more intelligent than positive ones for identical prose), and built trust — since the audience was going to find the flaws anyway.
- **Mere exposure beats a single big pitch.** Liking an unfamiliar idea increases with repeated, spaced-out exposure (10–20 short exposures, mixed with other content, works better than one long pitch). Change agents typically undercommunicate their vision by roughly 10x relative to what audiences need to internalize it.
- **Pick disagreeable audiences with a track record of originality, not agreeable ones.** Agreeable people avoid conflict and are less likely to back you when it counts; people with a documented history of challenging the status quo are more likely to seriously engage with — and back — your idea.
- **Status must be earned before power can be exercised safely.** Carmen Medina's first attempt to push CIA information-sharing reform failed because she lacked status; her second attempt succeeded because she built credibility first, then used it (Steve Jobs likewise consistently promoted the Apple employees who successfully challenged him).
- **Framing and audience-tailoring matter as much as content.** Kawasaki's 10/20/30 pitch rule (10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point font) and his insistence on customizing the pitch per audience is the practitioner-level version of the same principle: match the message to who's receiving it, not just what's true.

## Details
The common thread is that persuasion under skepticism runs on different rules than persuasion under goodwill. When your audience is inclined to look for reasons to say no (investors, gatekeepers, managers protecting the status quo), overconfidence reads as a red flag and triggers defensiveness. Acknowledging weaknesses first reframes the interaction from "convince me" to "help me solve this," which is a more honest and more effective register.
