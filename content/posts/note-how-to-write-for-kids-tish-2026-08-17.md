## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Tish Rabe, a 40-year children's book veteran (200+ books, 53 Cat in the Hat titles, Dr. Seuss's official rhyme-writer after his death) about the mechanics of writing for kids. Her core method is structural, not inspirational: write the ending first, work in strict beginning-middle-end arcs, and treat every page turn as a unit of suspense. She's equally concrete about craft mechanics — pure vs. slant rhyme, four lines per single page vs. eight per spread, characters facing into the page turn — and about the unglamorous business side: flat fees vs. royalties, self-publishing pitfalls, and why translating rhyming books rarely works. The throughline is that "fun and light" writing is the product of a lot of invisible engineering.

## Key ideas
- **Write the ending first, then back into the story.** Rabe picked this up from Sesame Street writers' meetings, where sketches were built by writing the punchline first. She writes the final page of a book (e.g., *Kindness Is Caring, Friendship Is Sharing*) before the opening page, because "I need to know" what the story has to arrive at.
- **Structure is always three parts: beginning, middle, end.** Beginning = setting and character (she names characters via research — "Amani" means peace in Swahili, found by Googling African girls' names). Middle = a threat or complication (a hungry lion). End = resolution that reinforces the theme (zebras' stripes blending together to protect one another).
- **Know your age band and write characters older than your reader.** Zero-to-four books (like *Sweet Dreams Ahead*) use very few words per page and a slow rhythm meant to lull a child to sleep. Four-to-seven books use kid protagonists who are two or three years older than the target reader, "because kids love to look up to older kids."
- **The page turn is a unit of suspense unique to the format.** Perell frames it well: "the difference between page 393 and 394 in a novel is not like page seven and eight in a children's book." Rabe writes toward page turns deliberately — ending a spread on a cliffhanger line ("I'll know I am ___") and revealing the payoff ("home") only after the turn.
- **Animals solve a representation problem.** Real kids in a 24-42 page book inevitably exclude some reader ("where's the little girl that looks just like me?"). Animals are also easier to draw expressively and give illustrators more creative latitude.
- **Words come first, always — and authors rarely meet illustrators.** Rabe writes the rhyming text, then adds an "ART" note describing the scene; the illustrator interprets and often changes it (e.g., Daddy Bear "eating breakfast" became "packing a backpack"). She has never spoken to the illustrator of her own Dr. Seuss books — the editor is the intermediary who protects visual consistency.
- **Editing is brutal and outsourced.** She writes many drafts before anyone sees them, has her own kids read drafts aloud ("if they stumbled at all, you change it"), and insists on a professional editor because "you're way too close to it." Her top tip for any writer: have someone who has never seen the piece read it out loud to you — you'll hear the mistakes you're blind to.
- **Rhyme craft, concretely.** Pure rhymes (cool/pool) vs. slant rhymes (barn/farm, go/home) — Dr. Seuss stuck to pure rhyme; Rabe allows slant rhyme when the exact word matters more than the rhyme ("nothing rhymes with home," but *home* was the word the page needed). When truly stuck, invent a word or town name to unlock a rhyme family (Seuss's "Vanvlex" for "necks"; her own town of "Gerplets" to rhyme with "pets").
- **Borrow existing melodies as containers.** She repeatedly writes new lyrics to *Twinkle Twinkle Little Star* or *The Twelve Days of Christmas* because the rhythm is already universally internalized — confirmed live by three Ugandan teachers on a Zoom call who all knew the tune instantly.
- **Fact-based books demand real research; story books carry a softer message.** For the Cat in the Hat Learning Library she went to the library, gathered already-simplified children's science, then versified it for accuracy (and had to redraw a spread when Pluto was demoted from planet status). Story books like *Bert and the Broken Teapot* teach differently — "people and friendship are more important than things" — with no facts to fact-check.
- **On writer's block:** she deliberately sets her internal deadline earlier than the real one ("some buffer"), and when truly stuck: "you cannot force it. Go do something else... clear your head." She endorses the "bed, bath, bus" trick — sleep, relax, or change scenery.
- **The business is fragmented and non-obvious.** Flat fee vs. royalty (Dr. Seuss books pay royalties, disbursed twice a year — "dinner on August 1st is very risky"). Self-published authors should NOT get their manuscript illustrated before submitting to publishers, because editors who love the words but hate the pictures (or vice versa) will pass rather than untangle the two. Translating rhyming books into other languages rarely works — she recommends dropping the rhyme for translations rather than forcing it.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- If I wrote the ending of my next piece first and worked backward, what would change about the middle?
- Where's the equivalent of a "page turn" in the writing I do — the exact spot where I control suspense by withholding the next beat?
- Am I choosing form (structure, meter, format) deliberately, the way Rabe chooses spreads vs. single pages by hand, or defaulting to whatever's easiest?
- What's my version of "bed, bath, bus" for getting unstuck, and do I actually use it or just push through?
- Do I build slack into my own deadlines the way Rabe privately moves hers a month earlier than the real one?
