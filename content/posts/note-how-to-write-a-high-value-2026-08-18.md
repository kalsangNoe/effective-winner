## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Byrne Hobart, writer of The Diff newsletter (50,000+ subscribers, many of them VCs, founders, and hedge fund managers), about how he consistently produces high-density, high-insight writing. Hobart's core argument is that value in writing comes from compression and understanding, not raw information: he keeps a running notes file that slowly crystallizes into essays, prefers success stories to scandals because scandals collapse into predictable narrative tropes, and treats "the huh reaction" — telling readers something true and surprising that they didn't know — as his actual product. He's also skeptical that talent explains outcomes; he attributes his output mostly to sustained effort, reading volume, and a disciplined daily writing schedule protected from real-time news and social media.

## Key ideas

**On idea generation**
- **Ideas start as a loose notes file, not an outline.** He keeps a running file of half-formed thoughts, links to read later, and quotes; some entries "slowly evolve into actual paragraphs" and eventually a coherent piece, while others just sit there.
- **His links email is a leading indicator of what he'll write about.** Long-form reads he lingers over on weekends often seed later newsletter topics; if he's about to cover a company, he'll first read a book about it or read more broadly in that industry.

**On narrative and story selection**
- **Success stories are harder to write well than scandals, which is exactly why they're more valuable.** A scandal "has an actual plot" — a rise, a downfall, an aftermath — and everyone involved has an incentive to blame someone else, which hands the writer a ready-made narrative (he cites *The Smartest Guys in the Room* and how every ex-Enron employee "was trying to" pin the blame elsewhere). A success story lacks that built-in drama, so writing about one well is a harder, more differentiated task.
- **Question who's telling the story and why.** When reconstructing a boardroom scene or a leak, he asks who was actually in the room, who benefits from a given version reaching the media, and adjusts his confidence accordingly — quoting an unnamed source's rule of thumb that if you're an expert in an area, "every single sentence, every single paragraph" of a typical article on it will contain something you'd push back on.

**On effort, timing, and being first**
- **Being first to cover breaking news is largely a coin flip he doesn't try to win.** He contrasts this with Joe Weisenthal (then at Business Insider), who reportedly has two tweets pre-written for jobs day — one for a stronger-than-expected report, one for weaker — ready to fire instantly. Hobart instead competes on depth and the "huh" reaction: telling people something true they didn't already know, like how gas stations actually make most of their money from lottery tickets and convenience-store margin, not gas.

**On growth and audience**
- **A tight, high-quality reader base beats a large, undifferentiated one.** He describes near "diminishing returns" to growth once a newsletter has a smart median reader, because new subscribers who wouldn't otherwise seek out this kind of content mostly add noise rather than value.
- **The newsletter functions as a social/professional filter, not just a publication.** Writing about a niche topic reliably surfaces the people already embedded in that world, which is a lower-effort way of "crafting and refining my social network" for someone who isn't naturally extroverted.

**On the writing process itself**
- **Mornings are protected writing time, deliberately insulated from the news cycle.** He writes most of the newsletter early — partly for focus, partly to reduce the risk of publishing something that ages badly (he describes having to quickly correct a COVID-era line about vaccine timelines after new information broke overnight).
- **Retention and memory improve when new information gets hung on existing knowledge.** He describes forgetting isolated facts (like why some IPOs briefly show a "negative price") for years until he stumbled on it again — and argues that the more parallels and analogies you can draw between domains, the more a given piece of reading sticks and compounds.
- **Ending a piece is often about waiting for the unconscious to finish, not forcing it.** He gets his best concluding sentences while walking, showering, or doing chores — not by staring at the draft — and is skeptical that deliberately engineering "distraction" (like checking email) replicates that effect, since it's just swapping one text-based task for another.

**On corporate and business writing**
- **Companies split into "references culture" and "working memory culture."** GitLab-style organizations maintain durable, wiki-like documentation that survives someone leaving; Amazon/Microsoft-style organizations instead invest in getting a small group to think a decision through deeply and hold the reasoning in their heads — writing there is more about the thinking process than the artifact left behind.
- **Amazon's six-page memo culture exists to force clarity and equalize scrutiny.** Because everyone reads silently before the meeting starts, a poorly reasoned paragraph can't hide, and (per Hobart) Bezos's own slow, careful reading style reinforces the norm that nothing in the memo is skippable.
- **Effort is close to impossible to compare across people.** He argues you can never know whether your own struggle with a task is more or less severe than someone else's — citing ultramarathon runners who may simply be wired to find distance running easier, not more disciplined — so he avoids treating visible effort as evidence of virtue.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I try to make the newsletter value added... it's a point about the content itself... why did this get written?"
- "A scandal has an actual plot... there's this downfall and there's this aftermath... a success story is like... they're a very large corporation with lots of vice presidents who are happy."
- "Honestly, a lot of it is the huh reaction — because it's hard, and it's also filling gaps in my own... people don't know but will find interesting."
- "I don't think I have a natural ability to [remember things]... a lot of it is reading a lot in the same domains... [drawing] analogies between domains."
- "It's very hard to be the first person to cover that story... but there's a competition of can you tweet it first, and you don't want to enter."
- "You will never know if your internal struggles... are more or less severe than somebody else's. You just... don't know."
- "I always think of writing as a technology... better than other mediums because it's so [compressed]... they need highly compressed information."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I choosing topics because they have a ready-made narrative arc (a "scandal shape"), or because they're genuinely worth explaining — and am I willing to do the harder work success stories require?
- Do I have a real "notes file" that lets half-formed ideas sit and mature, or am I forcing pieces into existence from a blank page?
- Where in my own writing routine could I protect a fixed block of time from the news cycle and social media the way Hobart protects his mornings?
- Am I actually optimizing for a "huh reaction" — a specific, verifiable thing the reader didn't know — or am I restating consensus more elegantly?
- How much of what I consider "talent" in my own output is really just accumulated reading and pattern-matching across domains, which is a trainable input rather than a fixed trait?
