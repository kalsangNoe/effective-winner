## TL;DR
In this short clip (from a David Perell interview), Steven Pinker argues that most academic writing is a "sheer waste": brilliant, taxpayer-funded work gets locked inside a closed circle of specialists instead of reaching the public that paid for it. He extends the critique inward too — bad prose creates real costs even among academics themselves, forcing peers to reread the same paragraph five or six times and risking outright misunderstanding of the work. He also frames clarity as an aesthetic obligation, not just a practical one: well-crafted writing is a pleasure, stilted and turgid writing is an annoyance, and academics who neglect this after doing serious scholarship are shirking an "essential task."

## Key ideas
Pinker's answer moves through three distinct costs of bad academic writing, then lands on a moral framing:

- **Waste of public investment.** Taxpayers fund a huge amount of academic research, but the results stay inside "a closed little circle" of specialists talking to each other rather than being made accessible to the people who paid for it.
- **An obligation to translate, not just publish.** It's not enough for the work to exist — Pinker says researchers "should give it away" and "state it in a form that is accessible," implying accessibility is a responsibility that follows the research, not an optional add-on.
- **Waste within the profession itself.** Even other academics pay a cost: Pinker describes having to "read the same paragraph like five or six times" just to figure out what a piece of writing is saying.
- **The risk of misunderstanding.** Rereading isn't just inefficient — it's unreliable. Even after the effort, "I might get it wrong," which undermines the entire point of writing something down to be understood.
- **Foregone pleasure and beauty.** Beyond function, Pinker treats prose as an aesthetic experience: reading something well-crafted is enjoyable, while reading something "stilted and turgid and bloated" is actively annoying.
- **The core indictment.** He ties it together by describing the imbalance directly: academics "devote so much brainpower into the scholarship and then just blow off the essential task of letting the world know what you've done" — treating communication as beneath the "real" work rather than as part of it.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Just a sheer waste... there's an awful lot of really brilliant work, a lot of really smart people... in academia, and why are they doing it? Just to entertain each other in a closed little circle. I mean, taxpayers pay for it." — Steven Pinker
- "It should be accessible. They should give it away. They should state it in a form that is accessible." — Steven Pinker
- "There's just an enormous amount of wasted effort and potential for misunderstanding in bad prose. If I have to read the same paragraph like five or six times in order to know what they're talking about... I might get it wrong. Then what — what's the point?" — Steven Pinker

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "entertaining a closed little circle" — writing for people who already agree with or understand me — instead of writing for a genuinely broader reader?
- Have I ever been the reader forced to reread a paragraph five or six times, and did I actually end up understanding it correctly, or just moving on with a wrong impression?
- Do I treat clarity as part of the work itself, or as a cleanup step I do (or skip) after the "real" thinking is finished?
- Pinker ties accessibility to the fact that taxpayers funded the research — is there an equivalent obligation in my own writing, tied to whoever's time, attention, or trust I'm spending?
- What's the difference, in my own drafts, between prose that's merely correct and prose that's actually a pleasure to read — and how much am I optimizing for the latter?
