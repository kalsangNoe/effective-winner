# The Mundanity of Excellence by Daniel F. Chambliss

**Source:** Chambliss, D. "The Mundanity of Excellence: An Ethnographic Report on Stratification and Olympic Swimmers." *Sociological Theory* 7:1 (Spring 1989), pp. 70–87. Includes DeNora's 1992 comment and Chambliss's reply, *Sociological Theory* 10:1, pp. 99–105.

## TL;DR
Based on years of ethnographic fieldwork with Olympic swimmers, Chambliss argues that "talent" is a useless, mystifying concept: elite performance isn't produced by innate gifts or by doing more of the same training, but by qualitative changes in technique, discipline, and attitude — a large number of small, individually unremarkable actions, each performed correctly and consistently, compounded into habit over time. Excellence is mundane, not magical, and the same logic extends well beyond sport.

## Key takeaways
1. **Excellence = consistent superiority of performance**, not an absolute standard — it just means one athlete (or level) reliably outperforms another.
2. **Excellence is not produced by quantitative increases** — more hours, more yardage, more "psyching up" doesn't move someone up a level. Small quantitative gaps (e.g. the 2.2% separating Rowdy Gaines's 1984 gold from 8th place) can reflect enormous *qualitative* differences.
3. **Levels of the sport are qualitatively distinct "worlds," not rungs on a ladder.** A "C" swimmer and a top-ranked "AAAA" swimmer look dramatically different physically — different strokes, turns, attitudes, friend groups, diets — not just faster versions of each other. Athletes don't "work their way up" by simple accumulation; movement between levels requires a qualitative jump, usually triggered by a change of setting (new team, new coach).
4. **"Talent" fails as an explanation for three reasons:** (a) other concrete factors — geography, family income, coaching access, body type, luck — explain success more precisely; (b) talent is empirically indistinguishable from its own effects (it's *inferred* backward from success, with no independent evidence — Kalinowski's research found swimmers are only labeled "talented" *after* they succeed); (c) the physical minimum required for elite performance seems strikingly low — most Olympic-caliber physical potential in the population is simply never pursued, because most people don't actually want to reach that level.
5. **Excellence is mundane**: superlative performance is a confluence of dozens of small skills — a proper flip turn, sculling the arms correctly, a streamlined push-off — each ordinary on its own, drilled into habit, and synthesized into a whole. Mary T. Meagher broke a world record within a year of making just two "mundane" changes: always arriving to practice on time, and always executing turns legally.
6. **Motivation is mundane too.** Big, dramatic goals (an Olympic medal) are largely ineffective on their own; what works is Karl Weick's "small wins" — concrete, achievable near-term tasks (win this set, fix this week's start) that compound into larger achievement.
7. **Maintaining a sense of "mundanity" under pressure is the key psychological skill.** Top performers treat high-stakes competition as routine ("it's just another swim meet") via rituals (warmups, visualization) that import daily habits into the novel situation. Chambliss contrasts this with "Michael," a graduate student who never treated his dissertation as an ordinary piece of work to just finish — and it stalled him for years.
8. **The argument generalizes beyond swimming** — Chambliss applies it to business (Drucker's "practices"), academia, speechmaking, acting, and other fields: excellence everywhere is the accumulation of correct, consistent, unglamorous choices, not a special inner quality.
9. **DeNora's 1992 comment pushes back**: she argues *excellence itself*, not just talent, is a meaningless-outside-its-social-construction concept, and that Chambliss doesn't fully reckon with this. She distinguishes "game" fields like swimming, where rules/criteria are fixed before competition, from "nongame" fields like art or academia, where criteria and performance are *coproduced* (e.g., Picasso's greatness is partly constituted by what the art world says about him) — so Chambliss's swimming-derived model doesn't map cleanly onto fields like the arts.
10. **Chambliss's reply**: he never intended a general theory of stratification, only to show success requires no mystery. He pushes back on DeNora's game/nongame split — sport's rules are negotiated too (the butterfly stroke began as an "illegal" breaststroke variant; a rule was changed to legalize Tracy Caulkins's technique) — and notes her examples (Beethoven, Picasso) are atypical rule-breaking innovators, not representative artists. He agrees excellence and "levels" are socially constructed, but maintains that *given* a standard, it's still meaningful to ask what practices produce consistent superiority — which was his actual project.
