## TL;DR
Mitch Albom (Tuesdays with Morrie, The Five People You Meet in Heaven, 14 books, 40M+ copies sold) walks through his entire craft in a long-form conversation with David Perell. His core method: pick a theme before a plot, know the ending before you start, and always stay "tethered" to that one big idea. He builds his prose through relentless self-editing toward brevity, treats writing as a disciplined daily job rather than something that requires inspiration, and thinks about pacing and structure almost musically — rhythm, not rules, is what makes writing work. Throughout, he returns to journalism-honed instincts: find the story at the margins (the gravedigger, not the pundits), write for the reader rather than himself, and write about ordinary human transformation (loss, love, mattering) rather than his own personal interests.

## Key ideas

**Serve the story, always.** Every piece needs one big idea you never sail too far from — Albom compares it to an astronaut's tether to the spaceship: deviations are fine as long as you stay connected to the cord. In columns, the first and last paragraphs should work together; in novels, he wants to know the ending before he starts, even if the middle is unknown.

**Slow parts fast, fast parts slow.** Early in his career editors criticized him for over-explaining; he overcorrected into extreme brevity — his books read short (~220–230 "real" pages) because he cuts anything that isn't essential. Tuesdays with Morrie was contracted at 320 pages but came in near 160 once typeset, because he refused to pad it: "I said what I want to say."

**Theme first, then story — never the other way.** He starts every book with a theme (not characters or plot) and builds a story as the delivery mechanism. The Five People You Meet in Heaven wasn't really about heaven — it was about "people who don't think they matter and that everybody matters." Twice was built around the theme that the grass is always greener, applied specifically to love, with a premise (a man who can redo any moment in his life, but loses the ability to be loved the same way if he abandons a true love) engineered to prove that theme.

**Write for the reader, not yourself — about 98% of the time.** He loves 1950s doo-wop music but won't write a book about it because it's hard to make interesting to a broad audience; a theme only "makes the list" when his own curiosity overlaps with what readers will care about.

**Building a writing routine.** Roughly three disciplined hours each morning, no music/news/email/internet, starting around 7am and stopping by 10 — deliberately stopping mid-sentence or mid-paragraph so the next day starts with momentum rather than dread. He treats writing like a job (learned from journalism and from fellow writers in his band, the Rock Bottom Remainders), never misses deadlines, and never suffers writer's block because he's kept a running file of emailed-to-himself book ideas for decades.

**Find the story at the margins.** His model is Jimmy Breslin covering JFK's funeral by interviewing the gravedigger instead of writing grand think-pieces — that oblique angle captured the nation's grief better than direct commentary. Albom's own example: at the 1992 Olympics he skipped writing about Carl Lewis's headline race to instead chase down Derek Redmond's father, who ran onto the track to carry his injured son across the finish line ("I taught him how to run... I put his feet on my feet") — it became one of his best-known columns.

**Characters must transform.** The main character needs a clear before/after; ancillary characters don't. In The Five People You Meet in Heaven he spent the first chapter stacking small, lonely details (an empty fishing line, falling asleep to seagulls) to establish where the character starts, then worked backward from where he wanted him to end.

**Pacing is rhythm, and rhythm is physical, not intellectual.** He edits by ear — reading a paragraph and feeling whether it "flows" or "stops." His wife noticed he physically rocks while writing and stops rocking when a passage isn't working. He compares structure to a color wheel (alternating tone like alternating colors) and cites Bill Goldman's observation that most movies cram disproportionate action into their final minutes — readers, like viewers, expect and accept a late crescendo.

**Humility produces the most beautiful writing.** Discussing his faith-themed books (Have a Little Faith, and his admiration for Gilead by Marilynne Robinson and the Psalms), he argues that writing from a place of humility before something larger than yourself — God, mystery, awe — opens up language you can't reach when you're trying to write something beautiful for its own sake.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "The minute they let go of that cord, they're never getting back... I always try to remember what I'm serving um in whatever piece I write."
- "I think she was talking about the Tom Clancy — this is how a submarine works kind of thing... that I think is the slow parts fast that you want to get through or avoid altogether."
- "I don't start with characters. I don't start with plots. I start with a theme that I want to cover."
- "What I want people to say is I couldn't stop thinking about that after I finished it. It made me think about my own life."
- "If they actually — it wasn't even 10 pages. It was like the first two pages. And I said, well, if I don't get them on the first page they're not going to read past it." (his editor Bill Thomas told him: "you're thinking like a sports writer writing a sports column... they're not going to read the first paragraph and say, this is not for me.")
- "One out of every three times he comes to the plate, something good happens" — his own example of finding the "spice of life" phrasing versus a dry stat like "batting .333."
- "You should recognize what how big your gas tank is and pretty much don't try to outrun your gas tank... for me it's about three hours."
- "When you write, you rock back and forth, but sometimes you stop. What's going on when you stop?" — his wife's observation, which he confirmed meant "it's not working when I stop."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I know the "ending" — the north star — of a piece before I start writing it, or am I discovering it as I go, and does that cost me coherence?
- Where in my own writing am I still "whittling" out of fear of the old criticism (too long, too slow), and where have I overcorrected into being too terse?
- Is there a theme I'm curious about that also overlaps with what a reader would care about — or am I writing about my "doo-wop music," something that only interests me?
- Where's the equivalent of the gravedigger in a story I'm trying to tell — the oblique, marginal angle that would capture the whole thing better than a direct take?
- Can I train myself to notice rhythm physically (the "rocking," the sense of a sentence going "dut" instead of flowing) rather than only through intellectual line-editing?
