## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Clint Bentley and Greg Kwedar, co-writers/directors of the Oscar-nominated *Train Dreams* (adapted from Denis Johnson's novella), about how they actually make films. The throughline is that craft comes from listening — to a place, to a text, to a film in the edit room — rather than imposing a plan on it. A mentor once told them to use dialogue "only as a last resort," a lesson they learned by literally cutting a whole dialogue scene in *Trans Pacos* down to one line and some looks and finding it worked better. They also talk about overwriting on purpose (so you know what you're cutting from), building sense of place through on-the-ground research, and how all four of their films ended up with different endings than what they originally shot.

## Key ideas
- **Beware of too much dialogue.** A mentor told them to use dialogue "only as a last resort." It didn't make sense to them until they cut a long, well-written truck scene between two characters in *Trans Pacos* down to one line and some looks between them during the shoot — and it was "so much better than all this we had written." Lesson: find the least you can do that gives the most to the audience. (This doesn't apply to every film — they cite *His Girl Friday* and *Before Sunrise* as dialogue-driven films that are supposed to be that way.)
- **Overwrite on purpose, then cut.** They write scenes — and even full voiceover — in excess first, because "we won't know what we're reaching for unless you put it there first." A great, deeply-researched voiceover about glaciers and the history of the land got cut from *Train Dreams* after their sound designer told them: "it's some very good writing, but it's not a good scene... do you want the good writing or do you want a good scene?"
- **Build stories from the dirt up.** Rather than starting with an idea and finding a place to set it, they immerse in a real location first and let story emerge from it — staying in a cabin near where Denis Johnson lived, hiring a local naturalist, going to logging-town bars at night to talk to strangers. A chance conversation with a Kootenai tribe member working at a fish hatchery directly shaped the character of Ignatius Jack and the image of a 10-foot sturgeon hanging from a pole.
- **Listen to the work, not your plan for it.** They quote a rule: "the work just comes to first it whispers, then it talks, then it screams and it has its own sense of what it wants." A scene transcribed almost verbatim from the novella (a man says he was shot by his dog) worked at first, then stopped fitting as the narrative developed and had to be cut — "it's just about listening to the work and what it wants to be."
- **Let the ending find itself.** They shot the novella's famous, almost Gravity's Rainbow-level ending (the "wolf boy" sequence) as planned, but in the edit it became clear the plane sequence — originally a smaller beat — was actually the film's true ending. All four of their released films have ended up with a different ending than what they started shooting.
- **Improv is structured, not loose.** They set up documentary-style situations on set (e.g., "Joel and Felicity are now gonna feed the chickens, let's see what happens") and shape the results in the edit. The goal: viewers should never be able to tell what was written years earlier versus improvised on the day. The pilot's line "you better hold on to something," which audiences latched onto thematically, was improvised by the real (non-actor) pilot on set.
- **Treat real life as cinematically as a heist.** A central premise of the film was giving ordinary, resonant moments — falling in love, feeling peaceful with family — the same weight movies usually reserve for spectacle, without becoming "schmaltzy." Calibration matters: early cuts had either too many barbs between the leads (felt like they hated each other) or too much sweetness (didn't feel real).
- **Mission statement as a filter, not a checklist.** Fifteen-plus years ago they wrote a career mission statement — "to tell stories of human connection in impossible places" — that isn't consciously applied scene-by-scene but functions as a filter for which projects and opportunities to say yes to as success brings more options.
- **Feedback: get notes from people who know what you're going for, and from people who have no idea.** Their inner circle for script notes is small (one friend, Linda Halbert, has read almost every draft for over a decade). They value both informed feedback and naive feedback ("what did you make your movie about?") — the latter is "the closest to an audience you're going to get." Sometimes watching someone's body language while they watch is more informative than what they say.
- **Music: find the rhythm, don't manufacture it.** Bryce Desner scored all their films. They build mood reels and writing-stage playlists, edit with temp music, then strip it all out before Desner starts so he isn't anchored to someone else's temp choices. A song can work "because it doesn't fit" — but only for a limited stretch before it overstays.
- **Closing mantras:** Clint's father's refrain, "pay attention, pay attention, pay attention," and Greg's borrowed line from the recovery program depicted in *Sing Sing*, "trust the process" — the idea that focusing on granular details lets the bigger picture take care of itself.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The work just comes to — first it whispers, then it talks, then it screams, and it has its own sense of what it wants." — Greg Kwedar
- "That scene became one line and some looks between them, and it was so much better than all this we had written." — Clint Bentley, on cutting the *Trans Pacos* truck scene
- "It's some very good writing, but it's not a good scene. So you pick — which one you want. Do you want the good writing or do you want a good scene?" — their sound designer, Lee Alivan
- "We build stories from the dirt up — we come into a place and try to listen to the people and the place itself first and allow the story to kind of come from those experiences." — Greg Kwedar
- "Sometimes there's a version of something being great — 'oh, I had this grand vision, woke up in a dream.' And then there's another version of: I just took that scene and I found 74 ways to improve it." — Greg Kwedar
- "By focusing on the details, the bigger picture will take care of itself." — Greg Kwedar
- "This world is intricately stitched together. Every thread we pull, we know not how it affects the design of things. We're but children on this earth pulling bolts out of a Ferris wheel, thinking ourselves to be gods." — line written by Clint Bentley for *Train Dreams*

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I using dialogue (or explicit explanation) as a first resort instead of a last one — what could a look, an action, or silence carry instead?
- Am I overwriting on purpose so I actually know what I'm cutting, or am I just writing the minimum and hoping it's enough?
- Do I have a small, trusted "think tank" for feedback, and am I also seeking out naive readers who have no context — both seem to serve different, necessary functions?
- Have I ever let a piece's real ending reveal itself in revision rather than forcing the ending I planned from the start?
- What's my equivalent of "going out to the bars in the logging town" — am I doing the on-the-ground, texture-gathering work before I write, or working entirely from research and imagination?
