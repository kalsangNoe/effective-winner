## TL;DR
David Perell interviews YouTuber Johnny Harris about his four-month, 25-person production process for videos on topics ranging from Mormon history to ice cream machines. The core argument: writing for YouTube is not text writing but "visual directing" — words and visuals are coded together in a two-column script, guided by Steven Pinker's "classic style" (active, concrete, who-did-what-to-whom language) and a promise-driven structure where the thumbnail/title sets an expectation the video must pay off. Harris insists craftsmanship and personal obsession, not data-driven optimization, are what make a story land, even as his team has built out fact-checking, art-direction, and packaging systems to scale that obsession. He closes by naming his growth edge — bringing in other people's human stories instead of funneling everything through his own perspective.

## Key ideas

**The four-month pipeline.** Story day/greenlight → a reporting brief he writes himself → 3–4 weeks of researcher work producing a 60–80 page "info doc" → he curates/outlines → 3–4 days of intense "scripting week" (writing and visually directing simultaneously) → production kickoff → shame cut → rough cut → fine cut → fine two → audio lock → picture lock, with ~150 notes per stage in Google Docs/Frame.io. They make ~30 videos a year (down from 48).

**Writing is visual directing, not text writing.** He writes prose in a left column and immediately "codes" the right column with color-coded visual directions (a macro turns red text into a checkbox for assets to collect). He calls it "coding" because he's speaking to a future animator, not writing sentences — "every word must pair with a motion or an action in the animation."

**Classic style (from Steven Pinker's *Sense of Style*).** His writing bible: active language with a clear agent doing something to something — "they fled" instead of "the migration of 6,000 Mormons." Concepts like "globalization" are vague; "America sending Ford to Japan" is concrete and visual to the brain.

**Every story is a promise.** The thumbnail/title is decided early and functions as "why should you click" — separate from the deeper story. He writes 7+ title options, mostly starting with "how" or "why." The video must reinforce that promise within the first minute even if it doesn't fully answer it until the end.

**"Loading up the icon with motives."** In map-based storytelling, he gives abstract entities (e.g., "the Mormons," "the federal government") motives, intentions, and even reductionist dialogue so viewers can track their conflict spatially and emotionally.

**Two registers: explainer voice and poetic/contemplative voice.** Technical, active, play-by-play prose alternates with a slower, more human, less-precise "contemplative" register used to surface paradox and complexity (e.g., pairing "undetonated bombs" with "abandoned bones"). Switching tones, he says, makes content more memorable.

**Field work has shifted from over-engineered to experiential.** After over-scripting a 2018 Hong Kong shoot (recording full VO on camera in the field), he now deliberately goes into the field without a fixed narrative — reacting and experiencing — and writes the connective prose only after he returns and has "let it settle."

**Surprise through language, not content.** His Antarctica video experiment: describe a "wonky" mapping/exploration story using unorthodox, awe-struck language instead of predictable jargon. His metaphor: most writers stay "at the bottom of the canal" using well-worn phrasing; fresh language requires climbing the canal wall.

**The Economist as a model.** He cites their profile of Syria's new leader — noting his palace "is five times larger than the White House" and "echoes with emptiness" with no staff to serve coffee — as the kind of concrete human detail that makes geopolitics visceral instead of abstract.

**Craftsmanship over optimization.** After scaling to 4 videos/month and 17 videos in flight at once, he felt the "magic" drain out and dialed back, accepting himself as a bottleneck: "if I'm obsessed with it, it will have some magic to it that people will like." The company's growth strategy now is launching other creator-led channels rather than optimizing his own further.

**Personal writing routine.** A strict 9am–2pm writing block, 3–4 days a week, with no meetings/Slack/email — "very disciplined and very urgent." He's dyslexic, doesn't read on the page, dictates notes to his phone, and writes prose "hearing it" rather than as text on a page.

**Growth edge: inviting other people's stories in.** He names his current limitation as everything funneling through his own eyes/process, and says he wants to shift toward facilitating other people's human stories rather than always being the narrator.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "Storytelling is the curation of chaotic information about the world into a very firm linear presentation."
- "I am writing a piece of prose in the left [column] and then I am pressing Tab and I'm going to the right and I am coding the visuals... it's not writing, I'm not writing the visuals."
- "Every story is effectively a promise — what makes someone pay attention to a story is anticipation that some promise is going to be fulfilled."
- "You can be an LLM... and just fill in the next word, and it's going to sound like everyone else who's talking about that topic. Or you can be a human and say: I am going to say this thing in a completely different way, but it's still the same thing."
- "If I'm obsessed with it then it will have some magic to it that the people will like — and that does put a ceiling on how much we can make, and we've sort of accepted that."
- "There is a way more complex deeper story at the heart of every story I tell... but I believe that surfacing it and simplifying it and storytelling it lets millions of people who otherwise would have never engaged with that complex story have access to it."
- "Finding the things that you want to understand deeply, really being able to identify them, and then cultivate that, lean into that, and try to communicate them to somebody else... there's really no skipping that process."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I staying "at the bottom of the canal" — reaching for the first phrasing instead of climbing for a fresher one?
- Do I have an equivalent of the two-column script — a way of tracking not just what I say but how it should land (structure, pacing, tone shifts) as I draft?
- Am I alternating between a "technical/explainer" register and a "human/contemplative" one, or defaulting to one voice throughout a piece?
- What's my version of "the promise" — am I clear, before I start writing, on what a reader is actually going to get and by when?
- Where am I over-engineering a plan in advance versus letting the material (interviews, research, experience) tell me what the piece actually is?
