Both great speeches and great decks are built around exactly one memorable idea, stated verbatim near the open and restated at the close — everything else is either supporting evidence or noise to cut. Slides fail for the same structural reason meetings fail: they let the presenter avoid deciding what actually matters most.

## Key Concepts
- **The verbatim Big Thought.** Write your one-sentence takeaway word-for-word (Harvard Business Review's cap: ≤15 words), state it explicitly near the top ("The one thing I want you to remember today is..."), and restate it verbatim at the close. If you don't know your one-sentence takeaway, no audience member will either.
- **Duarte's "secret structure" for speeches.** Describe the status quo → contrast it with your idea → oscillate between what-is and what-could-be → make a call to action → end with a vivid picture of the idea realized. Derived from analyzing MLK's "I Have a Dream" and Steve Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch.
- **The neighbor-encounter rule for openings.** A speech's opening anecdote should run exactly as long as a chance hallway encounter with a neighbor naturally would — social instinct already knows the right length; trust it instead of over-preparing.
- **TED's 18-minute cap.** "Short enough to hold people's attention... long enough to say something that matters" (Chris Anderson). A hard external constraint that forces exactly the same distillation discipline as a page limit does for writing.
- **One message per slide, image over text.** Molecular biologist John Medina found an arresting image paired with words lifts recall to ~65%, versus ~10% for words alone — the brain reliably processes only about two simultaneous stimuli (e.g., spoken word + image) well, so bulleted text on top of both overloads it. Test: could someone glancing at a slide going 65 mph get the point (the "3-second billboard" test)?
- **McKinsey's 20-to-2 rule.** New hires are told: for every 20 slides you want in a deck, use 2. A concrete ratio for how aggressively to cut after a first draft.
- **Always close with a specific ask.** Every deck should end by filling in the blank — "I called this meeting/built this deck so I can get ___ or teach you ___" — distilled to as few words as possible, not a generic "Questions?" slide.

## Details
The failure mode in both formats is the same: reaching for more content (slides, jokes, caveats, stats) as a substitute for deciding what the one point actually is. Edward Tufte's line on PowerPoint — that it functions like a drug with real side effects, "making us stupid, degrading the quality and credibility of our communication" — is really an argument that the *format itself* makes it easy to avoid that decision, which is why Bezos's memo-based alternative (see [[Meetings & Facilitation]]) forces it back.

The contrast case is instructive: a flat, forgettable 2021 Biden commencement address (28 minutes, no clear Big Thought, ended in "But all kidding aside...") versus Pamela Meyer's TED opener — "I don't want to alarm anybody in this room, but it's just come to my attention that the person to your right is a liar" — which does the whole job (hook, permission to laugh, thesis) in three sentences.

Deck-building sequence: (1) write the precise outcome + 3–5 supporting points, ordered like a legal case, *before* opening slide software; (2) simplify every slide to one message, one font/style; (3) pair points with images, not bulleted text; (4) cap total structure to one big idea + 3–5 points, roughly a dozen slides; (5) close with the explicit ask.
