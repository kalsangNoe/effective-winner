## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Tim Mackie (biblical scholar) and Jon Collins (his curious, untrained collaborator), founders of The Bible Project, first about their video-making process, then about how the Bible actually works as literature. Their core argument: the Bible is not a reference book or rulebook but a deliberately dense, riddle-filled piece of literary art that uses repetition, parallelism, and metaphor to make readers do interpretive work — and that work is the point, not an obstacle. Certainty isn't the goal of reading scripture; trust is, and the "illusion of knowing" has to be consciously turned off to actually learn something. They trace one extended example — the tree of "knowing good and bad" in Genesis 3 echoed by Solomon's request in 1 Kings 3 — to show how reading at both a close, word-level altitude and a wide, pattern-level altitude reveals meaning neither level shows alone.

## Key ideas
- **Videos start from scholarship, get compressed through dialogue.** Tim spends years researching (languages, cross-cultural scholarship in Greek, Hebrew, German, French, Latin) and prepares notes like "a professor prepping to teach a class." Jon, who "won't do any homework," drills him with questions as an untrained-but-curious stand-in for the viewer; the pipeline runs notes → discussion → script → more research → more notes → animation studio.
- **The "illusion of knowing" must be deliberately switched off.** Tim: brains trick us into feeling we understand things we don't, because "if we realized how little we actually understand about the world, we would go crazy." Genuine inquiry (and genuine conversation) requires re-entering a topic pretending not to know, even after 11 years of talking about the same ideas.
- **Certainty isn't the goal — trust is.** Tim: "the hope of certainty has gone out the window," not from skepticism but because scripture is training the reader in trust, not control. He calls the Bible's story "a botched quest for wisdom" — people trying to seize knowledge that isn't theirs to seize.
- **The Adam/Eve ↔ Solomon parallel as the model case.** The tree of "knowing good and bad" in Eden is forbidden; in 1 Kings 3, Solomon explicitly asks God for "the knowledge of good and bad" (a phrase often hidden in translation) and receives it gladly. Tim's realization: God wasn't withholding the knowledge, he was training humans to ask for it rather than take it — a riddle the Bible never explains outright but expects readers to discover by comparing stories.
- **Reading altitude: zoom between word-level detail and whole-pattern view.** Tim argues the ideal biblical reader — per the biblical authors' own design — reads both up close (why does this text repeat this detail three times?) and from a high altitude (how does this story rhyme with five others?), and that the close detail is what "sticks out" later at altitude.
- **Poetry and riddles are a feature, not a bug.** Jesus taught in riddles and Paul writes dense, run-on, unfinished Greek sentences on purpose (or at least without needing to be systematic) because indirect communication sparks a longer "learning journey" rather than closing a question in one pass. Tim: metaphor is how humans understand anything at all — "how do we change how we think? It's by adopting new metaphors."
- **The word/Word riddle (John 1:1).** "The word was with God, and the word was God" only makes sense as a hyperlink back to Genesis 1, where God's spoken word orders creation — an invisible thought given form outside oneself, both distinct from and identical to its source.
- **Meditation (Hebrew *haga*) means muttering the text daily until "the Bible starts to read you."** Psalm 1's ideal reader speaks scripture quietly, day and night, for life, then discusses it with friends — the goal isn't mastering the text but letting it reshape imagination and desire over time.
- **The four gospel writers as distinct literary styles.** Luke: orderly, journalistic, edits/simplifies his source (Mark) for Greco-Roman readers. Matthew and Luke: heavy word-level hyperlinking and structural patterning. John: "like a Rembrandt painting" — mostly dark, with a few illuminated details that carry outsized weight — poetic, relational, the product of decades of prayerful meditation by the last living apostle.
- **The Bible shaped English and Western moral intuition.** The King James (1611) built on Tyndale, Wycliffe, and especially the Geneva Bible (Shakespeare's Bible, carried to America by the Pilgrims). Idioms like "skin of our teeth" (from Job) entered English directly. More broadly, the idea that "the person without power has the moral high ground" is Hebrew Bible/Jesus-shaped, not a human default.
- **Writing the scripts as dialogue was an accident that became the method.** Jon's on-page questions are literally reverse-engineered from what actually confused him during the real research conversations, giving viewers permission to be beginners rather than experts — and encoding their belief (via Cleo Abram's line) that people "overestimate how much context others have and underestimate their intelligence."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I collapsing "the illusion of knowing" onto a topic I haven't actually re-examined in years — and what would it look like to re-enter it pretending not to know?
- Do I write to resolve tension for the reader immediately, or do I ever deliberately withhold and let ambiguity do interpretive work, the way Tim describes Jesus's parables and Paul's unfinished sentences doing?
- What's my equivalent of "reading altitude" — am I ever zooming between close word-level detail and wide pattern-level structure in the same piece, or do I default to one altitude?
- The Adam/Eve–Solomon parallel only became visible by holding two stories side by side without being told to. What other pairs of ideas (in my own notes, or across different domains) am I not yet placing next to each other?
- Jon's stand-in-for-the-viewer method (writing down his real confusion, not a hypothetical one) is a concrete technique — could I use a real collaborator's genuine questions as scaffolding for an explainer instead of imagining an audience?
