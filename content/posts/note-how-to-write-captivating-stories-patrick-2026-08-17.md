## TL;DR
David Perell interviews New Yorker staff writer Patrick Radden Keefe (Empire of Pain, Say Nothing, London Falling) on the craft of narrative nonfiction. Keefe's core argument: good nonfiction is architecture, not just reporting — structure gets decided early, characters are rationed to manage "context load," and readers are seduced with small mysteries, textural variety, and a "donkey" character who pulls them through complicated material. He also covers write-around reporting on uncooperative subjects and what editor Daniel Zalewski taught him about thinking like a reader while writing.

## Key ideas

**Write-around reporting: refusal isn't the end of the story.** When a powerful subject won't talk, Keefe writes about them anyway — "the train is leaving the station" whether they board or not. His Sackler book was written despite three generations refusing to talk and threatening to sue; he interviewed roommates, associates, and doormen instead, producing "a fuller, truer picture" than sit-downs would have.

**The goal is a painting, not a photograph.** Keefe tells subjects upfront his portrait will be filtered through his own sensibility, not their self-image. He wants people who know the subject to say "you really captured their essence" — even if the subject finds it uncomfortable to read.

**Specific beats generic.** A "green sofa" tells the reader nothing; "an old Victrola" and "a vintage TWA poster" are concrete and evocative. In London Falling he traces three families' immigration histories (India, Uganda, WWII-era Eastern Europe) because understanding people means understanding the milieu they came from.

**Readers are jaded — open a small mystery to earn attention.** His Chapo Guzmán piece opens in an Amsterdam airport, not the Sinaloa mountains, subverting assumptions and planting a question — "how do we get back to Mexico?" He calls this "withholding some little piece of information," distinct from whodunit-style mystery.

**Structure gets decided before most of the reporting happens.** Keefe sketches "eight or nine beats" on the back of an envelope early on — beginning, end, big reversals — which lets him be "ruthless" about cutting people who don't fit. Writing itself is "90% research," then a fast, obsessive sprint at the end.

**Texture and variety fight reader fatigue.** He mixes statistics, exposition, quotes, and scenes deliberately, since unbroken exposition "feels like quicksand." Statistics land best as a picturable unit — "two football fields" beats "200 yards," "overdose deaths exceeding all wars since WWII combined" beats a raw death toll.

**Screenwriting taught him scene discipline.** A screenplay page equals roughly a minute of screen time, so a scene should enter "at the last possible minute" and exit at the first — a discipline he carries into nonfiction, condensing a 300-page court transcript into five pages. Books and articles also pace revelations differently: a shocking scene lands as one unrepeatable "bell" in a magazine piece, but 200 pages into a book it can sit without coloring everything around it, since readers have more room to metabolize it.

**Cull the cast to manage "context load."** Editor Daniel Zalewski taught him too many early characters force costly memory work on readers; Keefe now limits named people (a crowd of FBI agents cut to a handful), favoring a few over a comprehensive cast.

**Find your "donkey."** Borrowing the term from Lawrence Wright, Keefe looks for a character compelling enough to "pull the reader through" complicated material — a story can be as complex as needed as long as readers feel they're following someone.

**Voice comes from quotable specificity.** He builds a subject's voice from found quotes even when they won't talk to him, and repeats a subject's verbal tic without flagging it ("At the risk of being immodest," from Carl Icahn) to let the pattern do the characterizing.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "It's not going to look like a photograph of you. It's going to look like a painting of you." — Patrick Radden Keefe
- "The train is leaving the station... it doesn't mean the train's not going to leave if you don't [get on]." — Keefe, on write-around reporting
- "I want to seduce you with this narrative... I'm sort of fighting for the ones who don't [click away]." — Keefe
- "You have a deck of cards. The universe of things that can happen is limited by objective reality and what I was able to bring home." — Keefe, on structure
- "You have to cull the herd here." — Daniel Zalewski, quoted by Keefe
- "Take it apart like a magic trick or a Swiss watch... and just steal, steal, steal." — Keefe, on learning to write

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where am I giving readers a "green sofa" instead of a "Victrola and a TWA poster" — generic detail instead of specific, evocative detail?
- Am I deciding structure before most of my research, or only backfilling it at the end?
- How many characters am I introducing early on, and can the reader actually hold onto all of them?
- Do I have a "donkey" — a character strong enough to pull a reader through denser material — in what I'm writing now?
