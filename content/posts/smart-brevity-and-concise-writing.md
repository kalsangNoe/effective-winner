A four-part structure — tease, lede, "Why it matters," optional "Go deeper" — for writing that survives a distracted, scanning reader. Built by the Axios founders after data (not intuition) showed even their most engaged, highest-paying readers wanted fewer words, not more. Brevity here means ruthless selection and word-level precision, not omission of nuance: "short, not shallow."

## Key Concepts
- **The Core 4 structure.** A muscular tease (≤6 words) → one strong first sentence/lede (the single most memorable fact) → a bolded "Why it matters" Axiom (1–2 sentences of context) → an optional "Go deeper" link for readers who want more. Designed to fit on one phone screen. Guardrail, not a rigid rule ("Smart Jazz") — the gold standard is always whatever's clearest for the reader.
- **Audience-first, one person at a time.** Picture one specific real person (name, job, face) rather than a vague audience — "if you try to speak to everyone, usually you reach no one." Figure out the ONE thing you want that person to remember before writing anything else, then write that sentence first.
- **Time on text is short — design for it, not against it.** Eye-tracking research (Prof. Ronald Yaros) puts average reading time at ~26 seconds, with readers in a state of "continuous partial attention" even when they care about the topic. The top four attention-killers ("kick-outs"): too much text, too much jargon, too many choices, long video.
- **Word-level mechanics are learnable.** Strong words are concrete and picturable (fire, chop, crush); weak words are abstract (process, discourse). Purge "10-dollar"/journalese words, replace foggy hedges ("could," "may," "might") with what's actually happening, and default to active verbs and shorter syllable counts — a one-syllable word beats a two-syllable word beats a three-syllable word.
- **Axioms are skimming infrastructure, not decoration.** Bolded signposts ("Why it matters," "The big picture," "By the numbers," "The bottom line") cue the reader's brain into context-processing mode; bullets are mandatory for 3+ related facts (the "Golden Rule of Bullets"); bold text works as an "eye-trap" for skimmers.
- **Say it out loud before you type it.** The reliable trick for finding your real lede: tell a real person (editor, spouse, roommate) what happened, out loud, before drafting — the spoken version is almost always sharper than the first written attempt. "No one gives a shit if no one sees or reads it."
- **Selection is the actual craft.** "You are the chef" — the writer's job is choosing what makes the cut, not exhaustively including everything; readers can't do their own curation. Editing test: cut hundreds of words from a draft and challenge the author to identify what's missing — usually they can't.

## Details
The method was reverse-engineered from data, not theory: Axios's founders watched ~80% of readers abandon their "toast of Washington" 1,600-word columns after ~490 words, and found that even Politico Pro subscribers paying $100k+/year for expert political intelligence said, when surveyed, that they wanted *shorter* stories (only ~5% valued length). That "even our most demanding readers want less" moment is the founding insight behind the whole system — it's presented as evidence against the common assumption that expertise or high stakes justify more words.

The Core 4 generalizes across formats. Newsletters get a hard structural version of it: name the newsletter, state word count/read time up top (Axios uses 265 wpm), open with "1 big thing," cap at 5–10 items and ~1,000 words total, close with something personal. Emojis are the zero-word extreme of the same idea — used sparingly as consistent topic-branding (e.g., one emoji always meaning "data"), they function like a visual Axiom.

Two lighter applications extend the same logic outward: **social media** (give the reader something — a fact, a laugh, a stat — rather than asking them for a click; tone shifts by platform: Twitter urgent/data-driven, Instagram image-led, Facebook provocatively framed) and **visual design** (establish one clear hierarchy via scale/color/contrast, then cut every element that isn't doing that work — Axios's own case study cropped a dominant elephant illustration down to make a small flag, the actual point, legible).
