Before drafting, successful writers spend substantial time on research, structure, and planning. This separates a vague idea from one with enough material to sustain a full work. Multiple concrete systems have emerged: the index-card method (organizing by theme), the envelope method (organizing by story scene), the whiteboard method (external visualization), and several interview-based synthesis methods.

## Key Concepts

**The index-card system.** Susan Orlean transfers research onto 5x8 index cards (~700 cards for *Rin Tin Tin*), then physically moves them around a large floor space to find structure and thematic groupings before connecting them into narrative. This forced re-reading (transcription to cards) and card-by-card sorting reveals patterns that laptop browsing can't produce. The method scales: Orlean's research for *The Library Book* took 6-8 weeks on the arson investigation alone, all on cards before a single sentence of prose was written.

**The envelope method.** Yann Martel (*Life of Pi*) spent two to two-and-a-half years researching (zoo biology, survival at sea, religious studies) until he had ~400 pages of notes. He physically cut them into snippets — sometimes a single word, sometimes whole scenes — and sorted them into labeled envelopes laid out in story order. Writing then means opening an envelope, not facing a blank page. This is closer to screenplay structure (scene-by-scene) than thematic organization, and mirrors how Patrick Radden Keefe structures narrative reporting: he sketches "beginning, end, big reversals" on the back of an envelope early on, which tells him exactly which people belong and which perfectly good subjects must be cut because they don't fit the architecture.

**The whiteboard method.** Daniel Pink visualizes structure externally before writing — literally whiteboards or large post-its behind his desk. This isn't fixed; Pink stress-tests it against actual drafting. Writing a planned short section on "breaks," he discovered so much material existed it became its own chapter. The external structure forces dialogue with the research itself and exposes when the plan doesn't hold.

**Interview synthesis at scale.** Lenny Rachitsky conducts 15–20 founder interviews with identical questions, then pastes all answers into one document and stares at it until frameworks emerge. This mechanical approach — identical prompts, side-by-side responses — surfaces patterns individual interviews miss. Johann Hari uses a "bee and beehive" method: catch one lead, follow as far as it goes, catch another; 200–300 interviews total, with ~80% eventually cut. Systematic exploration beats a predetermined thesis.

**Diary as research protocol.** Stefan Sagmeister keeps a 10-year diary, typed and reread, looking for recurring patterns. Those patterns become seeds for books and exhibitions. The reread is the key: forgotten entries resurface connections you can't force. Michael Pollan maintains daily journals per project, capturing quotes and highlights (not transcripts) and writing extensive entries after immersive experiences—vividness fades fast, and "forgetting is a feature, not a bug" because it surfaces the residual impression.

**Research that inhabits time and place.** Jack Carr visits at least one location per book (Mozambique, Kamchatka, Ukraine, Morocco) to inhabit the setting. For *Cry Havoc* (1968 Vietnam), he surrounded his office with era-appropriate sensory details: period maps, National Geographics, watches and knives available in 1968 so his characters could only draw on knowledge available to them then. David Grann sailed to remote Wager Island in Patagonia in wintertime to match castaways' conditions; he wrote almost nothing about the trip but standing in 25–30°F winds (vs. his logged 32°F) made him realize the crew must have had hypothermia, reshaping how he wrote their decision-making. Experiential research—standing in the place, feeling the weather—corrects assumptions that research alone can't catch.

**Material from listening, not books.** Andrew Hunter Murray runs a 12-year weekly podcast because "everyone knows really interesting things they don't realize are interesting." The best anecdotes come from chance conversations, not archives. This applies to research too: go where your audience congregates (Sam Parr found early traction by seeding Reddit in communities of self-publishing and Soylent users) or let field research be unscripted. Johnny Harris deliberately writes without a fixed narrative after going into the field—he reacts, experiences, then writes connective prose only after returning, resisting the temptation to pre-script discovery.

**Research scales and filters.** Stop researching when you notice repetition: "I've heard this before, I've heard this before" signals saturation. David Baldacci spends weeks researching a 1958 Los Angeles diner but includes only 3–4 well-chosen details (like a mechanical cowboy sign) because more than that and "the page gets flipped." Research to saturation, then cut almost all of it. The exception is detail that serves suspension of disbelief, not comprehensiveness. Daniel Pink triangulates evidence across disciplines: one obscure paper isn't evidence, but findings showing up independently in economics, neuroscience, and sports psychology are trustworthy. Cut when you stop learning; include only what the reader needs.

**The 30–40 page proposal as structure test.** Before writing a book, Pink writes a proposal as a test of the idea: "if this idea can't withstand a 30-page proposal, it's not going to withstand a 300-page book." It's also a test of himself—if he hates writing the proposal, he'll hate writing the book. A good pitch needs to be fresh and familiar simultaneously, plus a clear answer to why you are the only person who could write it.

**Research via frameworks.** Nick Bilton approached the Silk Road story like Tetris: cross-reference logs, interviews, geotags, timestamps, then visit locations to verify. He reconstructed an untagged campground location by calculating drive times from geotagged photos and searching Google Maps for wooded areas within that radius. Once structural frameworks are set (like Keefe's beginning-end-reversals envelope), they tell you what to research and who to interview. Alex Hormozi tests frameworks with real cases: "validity vs. utility" means a thing can be true but useless, or useful but invalid. MECE structuring (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive) forces you to look for counterexamples. If you can't break it with a real case, it's ready.

**Reading practices shape research.** Barbell reading splits between ultra-current (Twitter, group chats) and timeless (books older than 50 years); skip the middle. The Cultural Tutor avoids what "everyone else reads" because consensus thinking converges; a book surviving centuries (Boethius, 1,200-year bestseller) guarantees influence. Read secondary sources first (like Diarmaid MacCulloch reading Bishop Burnet before original manuscripts) to absorb clichés without being dominated by them, then return to primary sources with skepticism toward the conventional story. Jayne Anne Phillips does "embodied research"—visited an asylum, went to West Virginia, read four Civil War letter volumes and let period speech "wash over" her, rather than just mining facts. Amor Towles reads four novels written within ~18 months of each other, around the same period (1954: Flannery O'Connor, James Baldwin, *The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit*, Raymond Chandler), to recover what people actually felt in that era rather than relying on Wikipedia facts. Period fiction teaches sensibility better than textbooks.

## Details

**Orlean's three-phase separation.** (1) Research/reporting — task-based days with zero writing; (2) thinking/processing — transcribing notes to cards, moving them, still no prose; (3) writing — strict daily word quota (roughly 1,000 words, five days a week) that is liberating because it replaces "staring into the void" with a concrete task. Rushing these phases together produces thin work.

**The promise to the reader.** Pink asks: what promise am I making? This single question (stated as "what is this book **for**?") forces clarity before research spirals. A book without a clear promise won't cohere no matter how good the research is.

**Triangulation and the "cute finding" trap.** One study is not evidence. Findings showing up independently across economics, neuroscience, and sports psychology are. This separates strong nonfiction from papers stuffed with interesting-but-unsupported claims.

**Periphrasis and the "Ed" approach.** Michael Lewis's insight (via Jimmy Soni): Renaissance painters hid detail in the toenails. Best anecdotes come from people on cap-table edges, not central figures. Soni cold-called Ed Bogas (an obscure musician on an early PayPal cap table) and learned how Peter Thiel's chess intensity convinced Bogas to write the first seed check—a story Thiel himself would never volunteer. The periphery often has richer, less-rehearsed material than the obvious central character.

**Immediate post-interview capture.** Soni's infrastructure: free-write + voice memo of post-interview reactions (details like "Thiel checking market cap mid-interview") immediately after. Memory of vividness decays fast; what you felt in the room doesn't survive to the next day without capture.

**Theme precedes structure.** Nick Bilton: "I won't start a book until I know what the theme is." His Theranos piece had the theme "fake it till you make it taken too far"; his Tim Cook/Apple piece: "the future is coming whether we like it or not." Theme guides research—it tells you what questions to ask and which details matter. It's a filter, not a cage.

**Archives and discovery.** Maria Popova: the internet is surface-level; the Library of Congress holds undigitized reels of Neruda's first US appearance, material algorithms missed. Institutions still house what search erased. Relying only on digitized sources misrepresents a life. Steven Johnson's "spark file" (120,000 words of hunches since 1987, revisited every six months) surfaces connections: old ideas that didn't make sense click into place years later when a "missing piece" arrives. Capture without judgment at the time; frictionless capture keeps ideas alive.

**Vocabulary as toolkit.** Amor Towles learned French because 1900s Moscow aristocracy actually spoke French, studied baseball slang, finance jargon, street vernaculars. Treating different communities as their own "languages" trains the ear and expands the palette. Description must come from character consciousness, not the writer—ask what the character (not you) would notice. Count Rostov, an aristocrat, sees a waiter must have run up because the coffee wasn't a degree cooler. That's observation from inside a character's knowledge.

**Fieldwork and narrative emergence.** Clint Bentley and Greg Kwedar build stories from the dirt up: immerse first, story emerges later. Go to location, stay in cabin, hire local guides, talk to strangers; chance conversation shaped character development. Dan Wang advocates texture over tectonic plates: instead of writing about systems directly, focus on concrete entry points (bowl of soup in Kunming, cliffside architecture) that reveal systems through texture. The texture makes the abstract visible.

**Framework-first interview discipline.** Johann Hari pinned five questions to the door for years ("Is attention getting worse? Why? Who's doing it? How do we fix it? Where is fixing it?") and edited by killing anything not serving those questions. This anchor held a multi-year project. Ryan Holiday uses a three-step note-card system: flag with stickies while reading, wait weeks, transfer only what survives to cards. The gap forces better curation—what seemed essential days ago now feels extraneous.

**Writing as engineering, not art.** Pink frames nonfiction as engineering—"you're building something that has to work," stress-testing whether structural walls hold. Books need sturdy overall structure even if one room is slightly misplaced; plays are like watches—if gears don't mesh, it won't tell time. This discipline separates clear nonfiction from shapeless essay collections.
