## TL;DR
Jack and Ryan Met of AJR describe songwriting as a search for personal truth: the more embarrassing or specific the material, the more universally it lands, because "people feel the same things as you." They trace their evolution from generic dorm-room party songs to character-driven, vulnerable writing (a song from their dog's point of view, a song about intimacy issues), and explain their creative partnership as subconscious-writes/conscious-edits. They're relatively unworried about AI replacing songwriting because AI can only blend existing artists into a "nebulous middle" with no point of view, whereas believability comes from an artist's specific, irreproducible truth. The conversation also covers how TikTok-era virality, live shows built like Broadway productions, and "write a thousand bad songs" craft development have reshaped how they work.

## Key ideas

**On truth and embarrassment as the engine of connection**
- The core creative rule: write the thing that would most embarrass you to say to a stranger or your best friend — "that has to be the next song concept." Early on they wrote crowd-pleasing party songs; when nobody showed up anymore, they realized audiences don't connect to generic material, they connect to specific vulnerability.
- Their first steps into this were songs like "I'm Weak" (giving into temptation) and "Joe" (about idolizing a high school classmate), each a rung deeper into admitting something uncool.
- Ryan: distinguish vulnerable specificity from "trauma dumping." Trauma dumping is broad and self-centered ("my dad messed me up this much"); good writing zooms in on one tiny, concrete detail — "you don't write about the brutality of war, you write about a child's burnt sock laying on the ground."

**Writing from character, not autobiography**
- Their dog song began as a gimmick but found its emotional core when they flipped the frame: their dog thinks he's protecting them, when really they're protecting him. A verse imagines the dog processing their father's death: "I hope we don't move again. They lost half my [stuff]. Those dumb moving men. And where did your dad go? Did they also lose him? Well, I brought you this stick. I hope that it helps."
- "Inertia" works the same way but as a list of observed vignettes (a friend who keeps saying he'll quit his job, a couple who keeps threatening to break up) rather than one autobiographical story.
- A song becomes "three-dimensional" (vs. two-dimensional/derivative) at an unpredictable moment when the character starts telling the writers what happens next instead of the reverse — similar to novelists describing characters who "start surprising them."

**The subconscious/conscious writing partnership**
- Quoting Steve Martin: "your subconscious writes and your conscious edits." Ryan is usually the subconscious — writing from raw feeling, not caring what's cool — and Jack is the conscious editor who reshapes it for an audience ("Add in a trap snare," pull back the Broadway-lecture tone).
- Jack studied crowd reactions obsessively in college — literally staring at people at parties to see which songs made them stand on tables versus talk through. That habit trained his ear for what connects publicly, complementing Ryan's emotional-lyric instinct.
- Because they have each other, neither has to fully play both roles — one can stay purely subconscious/personal while the other manages audience-facing craft.

**Musical theater and "no limitations"**
- Broadway was their first love (they staged living-room shows before forming a band) and shapes their overture-style album openers and constant-mid-song swerves — "change up times, talk about a random thing, and then there's a new character."
- Early demos often sound "literally like a Broadway song," and it's usually Jack's job to pull them back toward pop (add a trap snare) before the theatricality tips into feeling "lectured at."

**Live shows as the real endpoint**
- "The tour is the end goal piece of art... the album is the soundtrack to that." They write with the live show already in mind and reverse-engineer songs around stage moments (a musical pause built in for a bow, a skydiving illusion using two 3D video walls).
- They deliberately show the audience how the trick is built — a recurring "making of" segment during shows — modeled on Penn & Teller's approach of deconstructing a trick while still performing it, because audiences enjoy being fooled and love being wrong about what's coming next.
- They're moving away from standard verse-chorus-verse-chorus-bridge structure, partly because live audiences get bored hearing a chorus a third time and respond better to a surprising outro.

**Craft development: "a thousand bad songs"**
- Their advice to newer artists: write a lot, tour a lot, play to small crowds for years. The first ~500 songs will be visible copies of favorite artists (Beach Boys, Imagine Dragons) — that's normal and useful, because it builds a toolkit ("I know how to implement a hint of Frankie Valli") they can later deploy intentionally.
- Not being classically trained is framed as a feature: a Dunning-Kruger-style naivety about "correct" chord progressions let them take risks (mixing yodeling, classical piano, and beatboxing) that more trained musicians might self-censor.
- "Off notes" and imperfections function as proof of authenticity, comparable to typos signaling a human actually wrote something — but faking imperfection deliberately (post-AI "look how human this is") would be a dystopian betrayal of that signal.

**On AI in music**
- Ryan isn't worried about AI replacing songwriting because AI can only interpolate existing artists into "some nebulous middle... a basic conglomerate," with no singular point of view — it can't produce Randy Newman's specific, unrepeatable vocal-cord-and-delivery combination that makes a lyric believable.
- They avoided AI-generated art for their new EP's album cover because it looked "too AI" — the artist they chose captured how a family reunion *feels* (mixed, hard-to-name emotion) rather than how it *looks*.
- One legitimate use case: Ryan used an AI voice tool (Kits AI) trained on Jack's voice to preview how a demo would sound in Jack's register before Jack re-recorded it live — speeding workflow without replacing creative authorship. Using AI to impersonate an artist and release music as them (e.g., a fake Drake song) is "a mess."
- Prediction: AI won't be able to forecast cultural shifts (like the folk/country resurgence, or Hozier's "Take Me to Church") because that requires reading what humans are about to feel next, not just what was previously popular.

**Dealing with hate and staying unapologetic**
- Hate stung early on, but they came to see that "the things they hate about us are the things that we love about ourselves" — and that audiences aren't hating them as people, just disliking a specific piece of music, the same way Jack dislikes certain songs without hating their creators.
- They've watched other artists release a unique, polarizing debut, get hate for it, then sand down the follow-up to sound more conventional — and lose popularity as a result. AJR's stance: lean further into the unique thing rather than apologizing for it.
- Their personal bar for regret: "write something you can defend in front of a jury." A silly novelty song like "Thirsty" (yodeling hook, widely mocked on TikTok) is harder to defend than a song like the dog song, because the former wasn't really their truth.

**Industry economics and virality**
- Streaming pays little unless you're at Ed Sheeran-scale volume; real money comes from touring, merch, and licensing (they've been in Microsoft and Apple commercials). They lost money on early tours deliberately, betting on long-term fan growth — a bet they say paid off over ~15 years.
- Virality is unpredictable and can lag by years: "World's Smallest Violin" took ~2 years after release to catch on via a TikTok anime-art trend; another song took 5 years. They compare picking a single today to "a game of roulette," where a bigger fanbase just buys more attempts, not certainty.

## Quotes worth keeping

- Ryan: "If I write about something embarrassing, odds are that the audience is going to feel the exact same way... and that becomes really addicting."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's the equivalent of "write about the burnt sock, not the war" in my own writing — am I reaching for the abstract claim when a single concrete image would land harder?
- Where in my work am I still writing "photocopies of photocopies" of my own past output instead of finding a genuinely new angle, the way AJR consciously avoids repeating an overture?
- Do I have a "subconscious writer / conscious editor" partner or process, or am I trying to do both roles alone — and does that cost me either rawness or clarity?
- What's my personal "jury defense" bar — the thing I could stand behind as true even if it gets mocked, versus content I made just to chase attention?
- Am I mistaking trauma-dumping (broad, self-centered) for vulnerability (narrow, specific, other-oriented) in my own writing?
