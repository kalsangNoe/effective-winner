## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Ryan Holiday, author of 16 books, about how he sustains creative output without burning out or being derailed by launches, criticism, or fear. Holiday's core argument is that momentum — not motivation, talent, or inspiration — is the real engine of a creative career: you protect yourself from both good and bad news by already being deep into the next project, you trust a process you've been through before rather than second-guessing it each time, and you get reps constantly (the Daily Stoic email, note cards, self-contained thousand-word chapter drafts) so that when the "trough of despair" hits, you recognize it instead of panicking. He also covers concrete craft mechanics: writing in individual Google Docs per chapter for pacing, a three-stage note-card system for research, why introductions define a book's shape, and how to solicit feedback without becoming a rubber stamp or self-indulgent.

## Key ideas
- **Insulate yourself from outcomes by already working on the next thing.** When *The Obstacle Is the Way* underperformed on launch, Holiday was already deep into *Ego Is the Enemy* and "didn't care" either way — good news is just as distracting as bad news, costing "like a day for this weird ephemeral mood that's not like doesn't last."
- **Flip the satisfaction ratio over a career.** Early on, 90% of his satisfaction came from external reception and 10% from the work itself; he's tried to invert that, defining success by things "up to you" — was it the best thing you wrote, was it less disruptive to your life than past projects — because "so much of it is outside of your control."
- **Quantity is a path to quality, not opposed to it.** He rejects the Robert Caro model (few books, years each) partly because that economic model barely exists anymore, and because "the pauses between books are really hard" — like staying in shape versus getting back into shape.
- **Trust the process, but only because you've survived it before.** Every project has a "trough of despair" (via Paul Graham); the first time it's confusing, the second time you wonder if it's different, by the third time you recognize it as just what happens. On *Discipline Is Destiny*, he found an old note card he'd written to himself in red Sharpie: "when you go through these cards in June it's going to feel like there's not a book here but there is. just keep going through the cards."
- **Forward momentum beats chronology.** When he lacked material for the Queen Elizabeth chapter, he paused it and kept writing other chapters rather than stalling — "the forward momentum was more important than chronology."
- **The introduction is "the creator of the shape."** You have to have cracked the whole book's structure before you start, and the intro should codify that — citing Kanye's *Yeezus* opening, Nabokov's *Lolita*, and the *Open* biography as examples of front-loaded signal. He deliberately avoids being too precious about intros, preferring to draft roughly and revise later.
- **Certainty ages badly, not style.** Rereading his own old books for anniversary editions, what makes him cringe isn't the prose but stated certainties that a decade more experience has complicated — "part of the reason it's just tighter and leaner is that there's nuance that was not there."
- **Editing is mostly cutting and combining, not adding.** On *Right Thing, Right Now* he took an extra year, cut the manuscript from ~85,000 words down to 65-70k, then rebuilt: "you can't just cut and then just leave blank spaces. You got to mend all those incision points back."
- **Know what feedback to accept.** Distinguishes coaching (useful, sought) from reviews/random opinions (mostly irrelevant). On *Conspiracy* he had to explicitly tell his editor what book he was writing so notes could be evaluated against that, not against a generic version of the genre. Contrasts *Seinfeld* (disciplined, no bad episodes) with *Curb Your Enthusiasm* (haphazard, higher highs and lower lows) as a model for balancing self-indulgence early in a project against discipline later.
- **The three-step note-card system:** read the book and flag with stickies, wait a few weeks, then transfer only what's still worth keeping onto cards — sorted later into chapters already written (pickups), chapters not yet written, or a general box "to hopefully find a home."
- **Write each chapter as a self-contained ~1,000-1,500-word piece in its own Google Doc.** This both sustains day-to-day momentum and forces each section to have its own pacing — "the story has to be interesting and self-justifying... it has to work by itself" before it gets interwoven with the rest.
- **Get reps any way you can.** Books are slow and expensive compared to a comedian's stage time; the Daily Stoic email — 366 stories, sent daily for 8 years to a real audience — functioned as unplanned deliberate practice: "I think if anything made me a better storyteller it's that I do that every day."
- **Read to lead, not just to read.** Citing a "secretive movie producer" mentor and General Mattis's line that failing to read is "unconscionable" for a leader because trial-and-error learning costs "body bags," Holiday argues you have to read deeply enough on a topic to avoid being "functionally illiterate" on it — including tracing modern ideas back to their oldest sources (Thucydides underlies modern China/US framing, for example).

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own work do I let a launch's outcome (good or bad) steal a day I should have spent already on the next thing?
- Do I have a version of the note-card "cooling off" step — writing something, walking away, and only keeping what survives — or do I keep everything on first pass?
- What's my equivalent of the Daily Stoic: a low-stakes, high-frequency, real-audience practice that quietly builds the skill I actually care about?
- When I reread old writing and cringe, is it the style or a stated certainty I no longer hold — and does that distinction change how I revise?
- Do I know precisely enough what I'm trying to make that I could reject feedback the way Holiday rejected notes on *Conspiracy* — or am I still taking all notes as equally valid?
