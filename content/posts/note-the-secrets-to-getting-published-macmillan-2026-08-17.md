## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Jon Yaged, CEO of Macmillan, to demystify how a top-five publisher actually works: who gets in the door, how deals are priced, where the money goes, and why the business resists data-driven prediction. Yaged's throughline is that publishing is unusually creator-friendly and unusually feel-driven — advances are set by comps and BookScan data, but the biggest hits (Harry Potter, first-time authors) are outliers no algorithm catches. He's candid about AI's real near-term uses (print forecasting, tip sheets, backlist metadata) versus its legal problem (uncopyrightable output), and about the structural reasons Macmillan can take a longer view than public competitors: private, family ownership.

## Key ideas

- **Consolidation buys scale, not more output.** The goal is profitability under pressure from retailer leverage (Amazon, Barnes & Noble), rising advances, and freight/printing/salary costs — but "1 + 1 doesn't equal two anymore. 1 + 1 equals something less than two" in terms of the books that actually get made.

- **Getting published runs almost entirely through agents.** Cold-emailing the "official" submissions address doesn't work. Agents or existing author/editor relationships account for 99% of Macmillan's list. Industry-wide, maybe 1 in 20–30 submitted manuscripts gets published; the industry puts out roughly 20–25k books a year, and Macmillan does about 1,200 of them (~5% market share, behind Penguin Random House).

- **The economics of a $30 book:** the retailer takes more than $15; the publisher nets under 50 cents per cover-price dollar; of that, the author's royalty (10–15% of cover price, ~$3–4.50/copy, on top of the advance) is the single biggest line item, then printing, then freight and marketing roughly tied. On foreign-rights licensing, authors commonly get 50–75%+ once the advance earns out. Yaged, who's also worked in music and film, calls publishing the most creator-friendly of the traditional media businesses — no "Hollywood accounting."

- **Data sets the price, not the bet.** BookScan (~85% of physical sales) plus agent-supplied numbers build the "comps" used to set advances. But the outliers — the books that define a career or a genre — aren't predictable: Harry Potter was rejected by nearly every New York publisher.

- **Formula creep is risk-aversion, not strategy.** Convergent cover design (the "Crazy Rich Asians look" copied everywhere) and the story-point-anecdote nonfiction template happen because "when things work, people want more of it." Genuine shifts — a new cover style, a resurgent genre like paranormal romance — come from someone willing to bet against consensus first.

- **Kids' publishing is its own, harder business.** A 32-page picture book costs as much to print as a 400-page novel, margins are thin (made up in volume), author/illustrator splits are almost always 50/50, and the format is unusually durable — ebooks never displaced physical picture books, and backlist titles like *Where the Wild Things Are* keep selling for decades because parents want to share them with their kids.

- **Backlist is the real profit engine.** Roughly half of Macmillan's revenue is backlist (anything over a year old) — lower marketing spend, word-of-mouth-driven. Publisher M&A is largely a bet on acquiring that backlist as an "annuity."

- **AI's near-term value is operational, not authorial.** Macmillan already uses it for print/demand forecasting — it estimates 1 million fewer copies printed last year as a result — and sees it drafting tip sheets and refreshing backlist metadata/keywords for discoverability. But U.S. copyright doesn't protect AI-generated work, so a book substantially written by AI is legally unownable — a business risk, not just a creative one.

- **Human narration still wins for memoir; AI is edging into production.** Jeremy Renner's and Matthew Perry's memoirs, and the *Careless People* audiobook, worked because the author told their own story. Macmillan has used AI voice-cloning once, disclosed openly, to patch a re-recorded backlist audiobook when the original narrator's voice had changed.

- **Marketing is one repeatable move:** "identify the community you're trying to reach... identify the connectors, the evangelists within that community, and then you empower them to spread the gospel." The channel changes (bloggers → Instagram → TikTok) but the mechanism is always word of mouth from a trusted source.

- **Private, family ownership buys patience.** Two shareholders in Germany (Stefan von Holtzbrinck and his niece Christiane Schuler) let Macmillan avoid the quarter-by-quarter pressure Yaged says is incompatible with creative work. The mandate he was given on day one: "we got to make some money, but we never manage to the last nickel... and most importantly, we got to publish books that make a difference."

- **Declining literacy is existential for the industry**, especially among kids — third- and fourth-graders reading less, historically worse for boys but now rising among girls too — because kids who don't become readers young rarely become adult readers.

- "I want all the information so I can make a gut decision... I haven't seen the algorithm yet that can identify the outlier." — Jon Yaged

- "The concept of creating an atmosphere where creativity can thrive is, I think, my biggest job." — Jon Yaged
- "There's no more creator-friendly business than the publishing industry... there's nothing that's fairer, or where the creator gets a bigger cut, than in publishing." — Jon Yaged

## Open questions / reflection prompts

- If backlist (>1 year old) is roughly half of Macmillan's revenue, how much of my own writing is built to have a long tail versus a launch-week spike?
- Yaged says the best marketing move is still "find the community, find its evangelists, empower them" — who are the actual evangelists for my writing, by name, and am I giving them anything to spread?
- The formula-creep critique (identical covers, identical nonfiction structure) applies just as easily to blog posts and newsletters — where am I copying "what worked" instead of taking the kind of risk that becomes the next trend?
- If AI-generated text isn't copyrightable, what does that imply for how I should disclose or bound my own use of AI in anything I intend to publish or claim ownership of?
- Yaged frames his job as "creating an atmosphere where creativity can thrive" rather than picking winners himself — what would that look like applied to how I run my own writing practice or collaborations?
