> [ ] Definition of an argument 
> 	An argument is a connected series of statements intended to present a reason for a proposition. 

The statements that present reasoning are called premises. 
The proposition that they reason for are called conclusions. 

So to rephrase the definition, an argument is a connected series of premises intended to present a reason for a conclusion. 

Arguments can be done for a lot of reasons. 
People give reasons that justify their belief in their conclusions. 

Arguments could be made to justify actions instead of beliefs. 
Arguments could also be done to explains why something happens. 

A lot of arguments are done to persuade people. 

How to spot an argument? 
You now know what an argument is. But how do you spot an argument? You can easily spot arguments by looking for special argument markers. These include words like so and because. They are more precisely called reason or premise markers since they are used when providing the reasoning for a conclusion. 


As set of conclusion markers: therefore, thus, hence, accordingly, which shows/establishes/provies/is evidence that and so on. 

There are also a lot more reason markers: since, for, which can be shown/established/proven by the fact that, and so on. 

You must be able to differentiate when a speaker is offering an argument vs when they are not. If I say 'It is raining, and I am carrying and umbrealla' I am not giving an argument. But if I say 'It is raining, and that is why I am carrying an umbrella', then I am giving an argument. Although it is a bad argument it demonstrates the difference. 

Also you should remember the presence of argument markers doesn't always mean an argument. And you should remember that an argument can be given without containing any argument markers.

How to defend against regress? 
- Guarding 
- Assuring 
- Evaluating 
- Discounting 

The following technique are used to turn bad arguments into better arguments and to stop the regress of reasons. 

Guarding 
- In this technique we weaken the premise. You admit uncertainty. 
	- Change the premise from 'all' to 'many' or 'some', or from 'definitely' to 'possibly'. 

Assuring 
- These terms prevent the audience from asking justification of the claim. 
- "I am sure", "Surely", " I am certain" , "Certainly", "I have no doubt", "Undoubtedly"

- Only use this when the premises are obvious where it is pointless or distracting.
- Be on the lookout for abusive assuring. 

- Assurances work only in a context of trust. 
- I tell you that I am certain but you will only take my word of it if you trust me. 

Evaluating 
Jargon term --normative language. These include words like good and bad as well as words like right and wrong. 

we call something good when its meets the relevant standards. 

You call something good but you do not specify the standards. 

Discounting 
- In this method you anticipate and defuse the methods beforehand. 
- You get to formulate the objection in a way that you want instead of it in the way that you would prefer.
