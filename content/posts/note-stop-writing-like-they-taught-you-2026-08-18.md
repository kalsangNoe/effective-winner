## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Mark Forsyth (author of *Elements of Eloquence*) about figures of rhetoric — old, mostly Greek-named tools for rephrasing a sentence so it sticks in the mind. Forsyth's core argument: school teaches writing for efficiency and plainness, but the most memorable lines in history (Shakespeare, Churchill, the Bible, Taylor Swift) all work because they're crafted with deliberate rhetorical patterning, not because they say something new. The content of a sentence and its memorability are separate problems, and rhetoric solves the second one. He walks through roughly a dozen named techniques with examples spanning Shakespeare, Churchill, JFK, Obama, the Bible, Oscar Wilde, and pop songs, showing the same handful of moves recur across 3,000 years of language.

## Key ideas
- **Polyptoton** — reusing the same word root in different grammatical forms ("Love is not love that alters when it alteration finds"; Taylor Swift's "players gonna play play play play play"; "give us this day our daily bread" — day/daily, trespasses/trespass). Cheap to deploy, very effective for titles ("Dream the dream," "Walk the walk").
- **Isocolon and chiasmus** — isocolon weighs two phrases of equal length against each other ("not because it's easy, but because it's hard"); chiasmus mirrors structure exactly, A-B-B-A ("ask not what your country can do for you, but what you can do for your country"). Both create symmetry that "sounds reasonable" and sticks.
- **Tricolon** (rule of three) — three items, often with a twist on the third: "the good, the bad, and the ugly"; "liberty, equality, fraternity" vs. the Nazis' "Ein Reich, ein Volk, ein Führer." Churchill's real 1940 quote was "blood, toil, tears, and sweat" — cultural memory trimmed it to the punchier "blood, sweat, and tears." Obama used 21 tricolons in his victory speech.
- **Anaphora vs. epistrophe** — anaphora repeats words at the *start* of successive clauses (Churchill's "we shall fight them..."; MLK's "I have a dream"); epistrophe repeats at the *end* (Obama's "yes we can"; Lincoln's "of the people, by the people, for the people"). Both produce a hypnotic, trance-like rhythm that lowers a listener's resistance to what follows.
- **Synesthesia** — deliberately crossing senses ("she smelled the way the Taj Mahal looks by moonlight," Raymond Chandler). Forsyth notes plain sensory description (wine-label language: "notes of raspberry") is often accurate but fails to paint a picture; jumping across senses does.
- **Personification** — giving abstractions (jealousy as "the green-eyed monster," death, time) a body and behavior; Shakespeare used this constantly and it can grow into full allegory.
- **Schesis onomaton** — a sentence or title with no main verb: Dickens's *Bleak House* opens with just "London." Star Trek's "Space, the final frontier." Rule-breaking that punches harder than a grammatically complete sentence.
- **Paradox** — a statement that appears self-contradictory but is true on reflection (Oscar Wilde: "the only thing worse than being talked about is not being talked about"). A *veridical paradox* is one that's counterintuitive but provably true (the Monty Hall problem). Paradox often overlaps with chiasmus ("the first shall be last, and the last shall be first").
- **Hyperbole and adynaton** — deliberate overstatement, pushed to the impossible ("when pigs fly"; Auden's "I'll love you till China and Africa meet"). A satire writer's rule Forsyth cites: the mistake is not going far enough — hyperbole has to be *obviously* false or it just reads as a wrong claim.
- **The central argument against "plain writing"** — writing for pure efficiency is like "dressing for efficiency": technically functional, but strips out what makes something human. Forsyth's analogy: Soylent gets the nutrition right and misses the entire point of food. He argues rhetoric isn't decoration bolted onto meaning — it's what makes the same idea *enchant* rather than just inform, and that trade-off is worth it except in genuinely utilitarian writing (a quarterly report).
- **Poetry's problem is context, not content** — Forsyth argues poems get "ruined" by being taught as essay-prompts stripped of the setting they were meant for. His example: Twist and Shout is best understood not by musicological analysis but by knowing it's the song played loud at parties — meaning lives in use, not just in text.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Why you would take writing of all things and say this must be completely plain. This must never be beautiful. That's crazy." — Mark Forsyth
- "Write to enchant. To make things better than they were." — Mark Forsyth
- "I think writing for efficiency is like dressing for efficiency. You know, if we all dressed for efficiency, we'd just be there in high-visibility waterproof trousers." — Mark Forsyth
- "You don't need to have anything new to say... the figures of rhetoric are just ways of rephrasing a single sentence and making it stick in the mind much more." — Mark Forsyth
- "If you can find a way to make a sensible observation sound like it doesn't make sense, then it will really, really stick in the memory." — Mark Forsyth, on paradox
- "You can analyze something till hell and back, but if you don't know where it belongs in a culture... you don't get a greater appreciation of it by chopping it up." — Mark Forsyth, on Twist and Shout and poetry taken out of context

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I optimizing for "efficient and correct" when the actual goal is to be remembered — and would one deliberate rhetorical move (polyptoton, tricolon, a dropped verb) do more work than another paragraph of explanation?
- Titles seem to be the highest-leverage place for these tools (schesis onomaton, polyptoton) — am I treating titles as an afterthought instead of a place to compress an idea into something that sticks?
- Forsyth warns rhetoric can tip into "baroque and rococo" self-parody — what's my equivalent of "too many chilies," and how would I actually notice I'd crossed it rather than just trusting instinct?
- Is there content I'm writing that should be closer to Soylent (a report, documentation) versus content that's being wrongly treated as Soylent when it should aim to enchant?
