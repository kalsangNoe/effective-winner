## TL;DR
Elif Shafak (21 books, president of the Royal Society of Literature) argues that beautiful writing comes from refusing to separate categories — magic from reality, mind from heart, autobiography from invention, melancholy from humor — and instead writing from intuition rather than control. She resists the "magical realism" label because it assumes a duality she doesn't believe in: in her native Istanbul, wonder and sorrow, humor and absurdity are always fused in the same moment. Her method is intuitive and immersive ("a little bit drunk," inside the story rather than above it), fueled by omnivorous reading, deep listening (including to silences), activated senses, and heavy metal played on loop. She closes by tying craft to freedom: writers need political and psychological freedom to write honestly, and she has personally faced prosecution in Turkey for what her fiction touched.

## Key ideas

- **Reject the magic/reality duality.** Shafak pushes back on "magical realism" as a label because it implies two separate domains. In Istanbul, "you might come across a very sad moment, but underneath there's a layer of absurd... or the opposite." She wants a word for work that doesn't split magic from reality at all.

- **Write from intuition, not audience-anticipation.** The moment a writer starts asking "what will people say, are the readers going to like this," that's "a very dangerous road." She stayed inside her imaginary world until the book was finished — panic about reception comes only after, once "the book is born and it's free."

- **No fixed schedule — just daily continuity.** She doesn't believe in precise writing routines ("we're mothers... we have many other responsibilities"). What's constant is reading, note-taking, and writing every day, with heavy deleting along the way. If she can't write in the day, she writes at night; if not at night, the next morning.

- **Writes at night, with heavy metal on repeat.** She's nocturnal, can't stand silence, and listens to one song on loop 70–80 times ("melodic death metal," industrial, Gothic metalcore, Nordic bands) to "zoom in and zoom out" into a different mental place. She finds metal "so honest... so raw," not aggressive but calming.

- **Two ways to write a novel — engineer vs. drunk.** One approach: plot everything out, author in full control. Her approach: write intuitively, "a little bit drunk," not knowing what a character will do five chapters ahead, letting side characters take over. To earn the confidence for that risk, she over-prepares beforehand through heavy reading, research, and listening.

- **Read everything, listen even more.** Writers should be "intellectual nomads" with no comfort zones — graphic novels and cookbooks alongside Dostoevsky and neuroscience. Listening matters as much as reading: "what they don't say is as important as what they do say," and oral storytelling (ballads, folktales, riddles) carries knowledge written culture misses.

- **No heroes — characters live on a spectrum.** She's drawn to the moment "a timid person suddenly shows courage" or a brave person is suddenly scared — transformation, not fixed virtue. She's more interested in "the periphery... more than the center," the unseen and unheard.

- **Doubt is not a phase you graduate out of.** "We always and always go through valleys of anxiety, mountains of depression... one week you might feel like I got this... and then the next week you're crawling on the floor." Writing "There Are Rivers in the Sky" required holding three storylines simultaneously, "like weaving a braid."

- **Metaphor should surface the invisible and add emotional hue.** Discussing her line about clouds "the color of a neglected fish tank," she explains she tries to activate all the senses — citing "10 Minutes 38 Seconds in This Strange World," where a dead character's brain stays briefly alive, forcing the question of how memory works through smell and taste (echoing Proust).

- **Literature beyond the autobiographical: the "transcendental."** Creative-writing workshops teach "write what you know," but for Shafak fiction's deeper power is becoming the other — "you become the other and then you become another... the person I regarded as my other is actually my brother."

- **Soft vs. hard writing is a tradeoff with clarity.** Poetic, "soft bed sheets" prose trades "singularity of meaning" for writing that "can mean more things." She refuses to preach: "when you're writing, you cannot preach... I have to leave the answers to the readers because every reader is going to come up with their own answer."

- **English vs. Turkish: different tools for different registers.** She wrote her first four novels in Turkish before switching to English over 20 years ago for the "cognitive distance" — like stepping back from a painting. Turkish carries her sorrow and melancholy more easily; English, with its absorbed loanwords like "hutzpah" and "kismet," is where she finds compassionate humor, irony, and satire more naturally.

- **Kronos vs. Chyros — two kinds of time.** Clock time (Kronos) is measurable; deep, cyclical time (Chyros) is "the time of storytelling." She illustrates with the Thames: declared biologically dead ~150 years ago from sewage, now home to nearly 300 species — proof history isn't linear progress, and companies are polluting it again "as we're speaking right now."

- **Freedom is the precondition for craft.** "What a writer needs first and foremost is freedom — the freedom to read, freedom to write, freedom to be." She's personally been "prosecuted, investigated, targeted" in Turkey for what her fiction touched, and won't take freedom for granted.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "I do not separate these categories like here's the domain of magic and here's the domain of reality... I think in life there is magic in every moment, every breath."
- "For me as a novelist, the best way forward is to remain inside that imaginary world that I am constructing and to believe in it with all my heart."
- "You're a little bit drunk. You don't quite know what you're doing. You take risks... you're more lost inside the text as an author. You're not above the text."
- "It doesn't matter whether it's your first book, fifth book... we always and always go through valleys of anxiety, mountains of depression. This is not a linear steady progress."
- "When we sit down and when we start writing, we become wiser... because we tap into something that is bigger than us, that is older than us, and that is definitely wiser than us."
- "When you're writing, you cannot preach. That is so off-putting... I really don't know the answers myself. The only thing I know is that there are questions that I care about."
- "My connection with the Turkish language is very emotional... My connection with the English language is more intellectual, more cerebral, and I feel like I need that too."
- "A part of me does not take the freedom to write or the freedom to read for granted."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I imposing a false duality (serious/funny, personal/universal, fact/story) that reality doesn't actually respect?
- Am I writing toward an anticipated reader reaction instead of staying "inside the imaginary world" until the piece is done?
- Do I have a version of Shafak's heavy-metal-on-loop ritual — some repeatable sensory trigger that reliably gets me into a generative state?
- Which of my characters (or arguments, in nonfiction) are too heroic or too flat, and where's the moment they could show the opposite of their nature?
- Am I over-explaining meaning in my own prose out of fear of ambiguity, when leaving something unresolved might make it resonate more?
