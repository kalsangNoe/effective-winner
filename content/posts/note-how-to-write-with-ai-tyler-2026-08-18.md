## TL;DR
David Perell interviews economist Tyler Cowen about how he actually uses AI day to day and what it means for writers. Cowen's core stance: use the best available models constantly for research, context-building, and critique, but never let AI write your actual prose — keep your writing "weird" and human because that's the only thing that will differentiate it from AI output. He predicts fewer books (especially "predictive" books about the near future), a shift toward high-frequency writing (blogs, Substack, Twitter), and a world where truly personal/human writing (memoir, mentorship, fieldwork-based work) is the main category that survives. He also argues secrets and human networks become more valuable precisely because generic information becomes cheap and abundant.

## Key ideas
- **AI as secondary literature, not a writing tool.** Cowen uses AI mainly when reading — interrogating LLMs about historical topics (e.g., prepping a podcast on Richard II and Henry V by querying instead of buying 20-30 books) and rereading Shakespeare or Wuthering Heights while asking the AI what puzzles exist in a chapter. He calls it "the new secondary literature."
- **Hallucinations are a solved-enough problem for his use case.** He says hallucinations have dropped "by more than 10x in the last year" for top reasoning models, and since he's usually the questioner (not publishing the AI's raw answers), errors don't trip him up — he still fact-checks everything the way he'd fact-check any source.
- **Don't ask the AI for meta-questions.** Asking "what question should I ask person X" produces bland, homogenized output. Better: ask specific, detailed historical/practical questions ("what was special about Wycliffe's Bible translation and how did his patrons feel about it") — out-of-left-field questions emerge from you, not from the AI.
- **Never let AI write your prose.** For a column on declassifying documents, he asked o1 Pro for legal background but didn't use its actual language — "I don't let it write for me... it's like my little baby." He wants to stay "this weirdo" rather than get smoothed into generic clarity, because that idiosyncrasy is what makes his writing his.
- **AI is excellent at critique, especially tone.** Following advice from Agnes Callard, he ran his own writing through AI asking what readers would find "obnoxious" — it correctly flagged a passage as condescending. He notes many high-tempered managers now run sharp critiques through AI to "make it warm" before sending.
- **Fewer books, more high-frequency writing.** Because AI changes the world fast, "predictive books" (2-4 year lead time) about near-future topics become obsolete before publication — so writers should shift to blogs/Substack/daily writing for time-sensitive topics, saving books for subjects "frozen in history."
- **The "question box" replaces most books.** Cowen thinks AI won't so much write books as make books unnecessary — readers will just ask the AI directly. What will persist is writing that's "more interesting than the question box": memoir, biography, mentorship — things requiring fieldwork, interviews, and lived experience the AI can't fabricate authentically.
- **Writing for the AI as a reader.** Since AIs are now "your most sympathetic reader" and already know the background, Cowen says he's "less inclined to fill in the blanks" for human readers at the margin — a deliberate trade-off, not advice for everyone.
- **Secrets and networks appreciate in value.** As public information becomes free and instantly reproducible, Peter Thiel-style "secrets" (private information others don't have) and access to strong human networks become disproportionately more valuable — he floats a productive person's network being "50x, 5000x" more impactful because of the "free army of highly intelligent servants" they can direct.
- **Model-specific workflow:** o1 Pro for deep individual queries (2-4 min wait, worth it); Deep Research for occasional 10-page reports (built on O3, very powerful but he underuses it); Claude for the best actual prose ("thoughtful, philosophical, dreamy, flexible... the best writer"); DeepSeek deliberately for weirdness/creativity/emotion ("China boss") despite worse hallucination rates; Gemini for huge documents and its YouTube-transcript integration; Perplexity daily as a Google replacement with reliable citations.
- **Prompting advice:** Give a very long, dictated first prompt (he uses voice dictation for 1.5-3 minutes) to set context, then keep follow-ups short — asking 10 things in one long prompt degrades quality ("it almost gets tired... needs to take a nap"). Mentally address the AI like "an alien or a non-human animal," being maximally literal.
- **Teaching stance:** In his PhD and law classes, students must use AI and report how — "how much of it is yours? It's all yours... just like when you write with pen and paper." He calls the academic silence on AI training "a scandal."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I don't directly use AI for writing typically... I don't let it write for me. I want the writing to be my own — it's like my little baby, so to speak."
- "I want to be like Tyler Cowen this weirdo... I don't want the AI messing with that, and it's not going to, because I won't let it."
- "The truly human books will stand out all the more, and a lot of the rest will be this — will be AI slop... human slop."
- "Put humans out of your mind. Imagine yourself either speaking to an alien or maybe a non-human animal — this feels a need to be more literal."
- "It's a lot better to prompt one thing 10 times than 10 things one time... if you ask a really long [prompt], it almost gets tired by the end of it, like it needs to take a nap."
- "Humans know secrets. Maybe AIs can be fed secrets, but they don't in general know secrets."
- "The most innovative people are the AI companies themselves — they use AI to improve AI, and they hold their secrets close to their chest."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I letting AI smooth away the "weirdness" that actually makes it mine, versus using it purely for background research?
- What's my equivalent of Cowen's "question box" test — which pieces I'm writing would readers rather just ask an AI directly, and which require lived experience or fieldwork an AI can't fake?
- Am I writing anything explicitly "for the AI reader" (i.e., assuming it already has full context and skipping the on-ramp for humans) — and is that a trade-off I actually want to make?
- Do I have a "China boss" equivalent — a deliberately weird/wrong-shaped model or source I consult specifically to break out of homogenized, blandly-competent AI output?
- Given that public information is becoming cheap and instant, what are my actual "secrets" (private knowledge, relationships, lived experience) worth doubling down on instead of publishing generically?
