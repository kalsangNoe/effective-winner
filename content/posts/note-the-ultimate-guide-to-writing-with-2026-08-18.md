## TL;DR
David Perell (founder of the former writing school Write of Passage) argues that AI won't kill non-fiction writing but will raise the bar for it: the old SEO/content-mill playbook of publishing consistently regardless of quality is dead, and only writing with genuine personal experience, expertise, taste, and a spiky point of view will survive. He distinguishes writing *for* you (bad) from writing *with* you (good) — AI as a thinking partner, interviewer, and editor rather than a ghostwriter. He predicts writing will follow music's path (sampling went from taboo to normal) rather than chess's (humans still watched despite computers being better), meaning in 10-15 years only output quality will matter, not how it was produced. The back half is a practical, model-by-model breakdown of his actual workflow (ChatGPT 4.5, o1 Pro, Deep Research, Claude 3.5/3.7, Grok, Perplexity, ElevenLabs, Granola, Whisper Flow) for creating, thinking, researching, and remembering.

## Key ideas

**Backstory / thesis**
- **Write of Passage's curriculum flipped in under 6 months** — in November 2024 AI was under 10% of the curriculum; by March 2025 he "can't even imagine it not being the lynch pin of it."
- **Two big thoughts bookend the episode**: writers who dismiss AI outright are "out of your mind," but genuinely great writers "are going to be all right" — it's not doom and gloom.

**AI is actually funny**
- AI-generated niche humor (his example: a "be me" meme about economist Tyler Cowen) can already beat what a human could produce in a week, because AI covers esoteric interests no comedian would bother with.
- Citing Ethan Mollick: as of Sept 2024, 18% of financial consumer complaints, 24% of press releases, 15% of job postings, and 14% of UN press releases showed signs of LLM writing — "the most rapid change in human written communication ever."

**Why personal writing is AI-proof**
- Writing's core value is human connection — "writing is a kind of antidote to loneliness" (paraphrasing David Foster Wallace: we read to countenance loneliness). A personal narrative from a computer is "a completely hollow thing," so memoir/biography survive.
- Beyond personal story, look for **experience and expertise** AI can't have: his examples are knowing the current vibe of Austin, knowing how to run a 200-person Zoom session, and hearing pre-publication gossip about the YouTube algorithm from friends — information travels from private circles to conferences to books, and LLMs only catch up last.

**Two kinds of quality**
- **Objective quality** (craft, prose, research depth — e.g., Gibbon's *Decline and Fall*, Caro's *The Power Broker*) vs. **tailored quality** (how precisely a piece matches your exact interest, right now). AI is weak on the first, unbeatable on the second — his example: a Deep Research report on the flora/fauna along his specific walking route in Austin beat any generic book on the topic because it was "10 out of 10 personalized."
- The two skills that matter regardless of who's typing: **taste** (discernment — most of what's generated, human or AI, gets cut, like Michelangelo removing marble) and a **spiky point of view** (Peter Thiel's "what do you believe that few people agree with" — AI, trained on consensus, can't fake this).

**Writing will be like chess or music**
- Chess: computers surpassed humans, but people still watch humans play for the drama. Music: sampling was once "theft," now normal (he cites the Beastie Boys, Dr. Dre, Kanye). He bets writing goes the music route — in 10-15 years "the only thing that'll matter" is the objective quality of the output, not whether AI wrote it for you, with you, or you wrote it alone.

**AI is the end of slop**
- Defines slop as "when simply publishing or getting something done is more important than the quality of what you publish" — and argues the SEO era (bloated recipe blogs) and the "publish every week no matter what" newsletter strategy he taught for years both fit that definition. AI raises the bar so distribution-over-quality no longer works; going forward, "content's going to be king."

**How he actually writes with LLMs**
- Hard rule: writing *for* you (bad, no serious writer he respects believes in it) vs. writing *with* you (good — everyone he admires does this). Example: he wouldn't ask ChatGPT to write about San Francisco's decline — the personal stories (his mom mugged, shattered car windows, finding a syringe as a kid) are what AI can't supply; AI can supply historical background via Deep Research.
- Workflow: speaks ideas aloud on walks (dictation, since typing hurts his fingers) → asks AI to turn it into an outline or prose → asks AI what was interesting, unclear, or needs more detail → iterates to a V2 → uses AI as an "interviewer" for character development in his 11,000-word piece about converting to Christianity.
- Building a **custom project folder** for his writing voice: section 1 = explicit bullet points of what he does/doesn't want his writing to sound like (derived by having AI analyze his best past writing); section 2 = "training data" — annotated examples of paragraphs he likes and why.
- His note-taking is shifting from many small notes (for himself to search) to a few large documents (for AI's expanding context window to ingest) — expects to feed millions of words of training data within a couple years.

**Thinking with LLMs**
- Cites Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella: "The new workflow for me is that I think with AI and work with my colleagues." A friend running a 2,000-person company told his execs that an hour with an LLM beats "about 70% of the conversations I have with you."
- Uses Grok's "argumentative mode" to debate ideas he holds strongly, then asks for a summary of key points and pushback at the end — treats disagreement with AI as consequence-free compared to arguing with a friend.
- New killer app: **memory**. Humans forget (his own embarrassing story: taking a date to a steakhouse after she'd told him twice she doesn't eat red meat); AI won't. He predicts this could consolidate power upward in organizations — quoting a friend's essay on Google's Sundar Pichai: "AI Sundar's bandwidth is capped only by the number of TPUs you give him."

**Models diverging, and hallucination caution**
- Predicts models will keep differentiating rather than converging: Claude 3.7 leaned into coding, GPT-4.5 into qualitative/writing, Grok into up-to-date info and free speech.
- Cautionary tale: he spent two days building a video around a "perfect" John Steinbeck quote about food that ChatGPT hallucinated — it wasn't in *East of Eden*, and 30,000 people saw the error before it was caught. Cites Benedict Evans: LLMs are good at things without wrong answers, bad at precise information retrieval.

**His model-by-model toolkit**
- **ChatGPT 4.5** — main tool for *creating*: cleans up dictated rambling into polished prose fast; colleagues couldn't tell AI did the final pass on a Slack writeup.
- **o1 Pro / Deep Research** — for *consuming*: he's fine waiting 12+ minutes for something 10/10 tailored to his exact question (e.g., why I-35 traffic in Austin is so bad).
- **Claude 3.5/3.7** — sounds "the most human" for voice; also better at generating charts than ChatGPT's tables.
- **Grok** — "your crazy friend," most personality, best for funny analogies and voice-mode arguing; Patrick Collison (Stripe CEO) reportedly uses it as a live reading companion.
- **Perplexity** — best for quick, sourced factual answers (its own deep research is weak; ChatGPT's is best-in-class).
- **ElevenLabs** — near-instant, high-quality transcription (replacing $150/24hr services) and a cloned version of his own voice for fixing audio without re-recording.
- **Granola** — meeting-note AI that transcribes and lets him query "what did we decide about X" after the fact.
- **Whisper Flow / Super Whisper** — dictation far more accurate than Siri, learns his capitalization habits over time.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "I define slop as when simply publishing or getting something done is more important than the quality of what you publish."

- "The new workflow for me is that I think with AI and work with my colleagues." (quoting Satya Nadella)

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I relying on distribution/consistency instead of quality — i.e., where am I producing "slop" by Perell's definition?
- What experience or expertise do I have that an LLM genuinely can't replicate — and am I actually writing from it, or defaulting to generic takes?
- Am I using AI to write *for* me anywhere, versus as a genuine thinking partner that surfaces ideas I'd have found myself?
- Could I build a "voice folder" of my own best writing plus annotations, the way Perell describes, to make AI feedback on my drafts more useful?
- Where have I taken an AI-generated fact/quote at face value without verifying it, the way Perell's Steinbeck quote slipped through?
