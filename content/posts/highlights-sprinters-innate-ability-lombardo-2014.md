# You Can't Teach Speed—Sprinters Falsify the Deliberate Practice Model of Expertise by Lombardo & Deaner

**Source:** PeerJ, 2014

## TL;DR
This paper challenges the deliberate practice model by examining elite sprint performance. The authors argue that elite sprinters exhibit measurable speed advantages before formal training begins, achieve world-class status rapidly (often within 3-7.5 years rather than 10,000 hours), and consistently report being recognized as exceptional sprinters in their youth. These findings contradict predictions of the deliberate practice model, suggesting that innate physiological capacity (notably early-expression speed) is a non-negligible factor in sprint expertise.

## Key takeaways

1. **Pre-Training Exceptionality**: Every documented elite male and female sprinter showed evidence of exceptional speed prior to beginning formal sprint training. Sprinters recalled being unusually fast as children and adolescents, compared to control groups (throwers) who did not have this pre-training advantage.

2. **Rapid Ascent to Elite Status**: Rather than requiring 10,000 hours or approximately 10 years of training as the deliberate practice model predicts, elite sprinters achieved world-class status rapidly: Study 1 median = 3 years; Study 2 = 7.5 years. This violates a core prediction of Ericsson's model.

3. **Sprinters vs. Throwers Comparison**: The study compared sprinters with throwers, reasoning that both require structured training and coaching but only sprinters are speed-dependent. Throwers did not recall exceptional throwing ability in youth, while sprinters uniformly recalled early speed advantage. This design strengthens the causal inference that pre-training traits predict specialization.

4. **Physiological Constraints on Training Effects**: The paper suggests that speed (the ability to generate high velocity over short distances) has a significant innate/heritable component in early development. Training improves technique and efficiency but may have limits on how much it can increase baseline speed capacity beyond genetic predisposition.

5. **Refutes Talent-Doesn't-Exist Narrative**: Ericsson's model explicitly states "we deny that these differences are due to innate talent, physical predispositions, or genetically determined limits." The sprinter evidence shows this absolutism is inconsistent with data—innate differences in speed capacity exist and predict who becomes an elite sprinter.

6. **Domain Limitations of Deliberate Practice**: The findings suggest that the deliberate practice model may apply more strongly to domains where technique or knowledge (chess, music, surgery) can largely overcome initial ability differences. In speed-based athletic domains, physiological capacity sets firmer bounds.

7. **Early Identification Implications**: If pre-training speed predicts sprinting success, talent identification in track and field can begin early (childhood) and should leverage natural ability rather than assuming all children have equivalent potential. This contrasts with Ericsson's retrospective-talent-labeling view.

8. **Innate Differences in Muscle Fiber Composition**: While not detailed in the abstract, the paper implies that differences in muscle fiber type distribution (fast-twitch vs. slow-twitch), which are largely genetically determined, may constrain the trainability of sprint speed independent of practice volume.

9. **Falsification via Single Domain**: The authors argue that finding one domain (sprinting) where the deliberate practice model fails—where exceptionality precedes training, rapid expertise emerges, and pre-training traits predict outcomes—is sufficient to falsify universal claims of the model.

10. **Implication for Expertise Science**: The findings suggest a more nuanced model: expertise results from interaction between trainable factors (technique, strategy, work ethic) and non-trainable or slowly-trainable factors (physiological capacity, sensorimotor ability). Deliberate practice matters most when the latter are relatively equal across individuals.
