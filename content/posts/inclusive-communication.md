Plain, direct, jargon-free writing wasn't designed as an accessibility practice, but it functions as one: it strips out much of the cultural bias and complexity that creeps into writing by default, and it directly helps readers with learning disabilities and non-native English speakers. Deliberate practice — checking relevance, checking blind spots — is still required on top of the plain-language baseline.

## Key Concepts
- **The relevance test.** Per the Asian American Journalists Association: "Is race/ethnicity/religion/national origin relevant?" If a descriptor isn't doing real explanatory work, cut it — using it anyway, without explaining its relevance, perpetuates stereotypes even when unintentional.
- **The swap test.** Mentally substitute one identity, race, or nationality for another in a sentence and check whether the language and intent still read as nonjudgmental — a fast, repeatable check for blind spots you can't otherwise see from inside your own perspective.
- **Accessibility is a real fraction of any audience, not an edge case.** 1 in 5 children report learning disabilities (National Center for Learning Disabilities); extrapolated to adults, that's roughly 65 million Americans — meaning ~20% of a general audience may be reading through some form of disability-related friction.
- **Plain language as a side effect, not the original goal.** Short sentences, concrete words, and minimal jargon (the same rules that make writing scannable for busy readers) independently help readers with dyslexia and readers processing in a second language — the authors are explicit that Smart Brevity wasn't built for this purpose, but noticed the effect in practice.
- **Ask, don't assume.** Best practice for writing about someone specific: ask how they identify (e.g., Asian American vs. Chinese American; specific tribal affiliation for Indigenous Americans; preferred gender pronouns) rather than defaulting to a broad or assumed label.

## Details
Co-founder Roy Schwartz's own school experience anchors the chapter: undiagnosed dyslexia got him marked as a "problem child" ("Short and not very sweet. Do you not own a dictionary?" — a teacher's comment on his paper), which he later reframed as a forcing function — "Dyslexia caused great pain in my life, but it gave me a gift. I had to problem-solve, work harder, create systems to thrive." His resulting checklist collapses to three moves: write in plain, clear language; use bullet points to force separation of distinct ideas (mixing several points together is a common way writers lose an audience); and keep it simple and short, since complexity confuses and abstraction alienates independent of any disability.

The photo/imagery version of the relevance test matters too: illustrating a story about anti-Asian hate with a photo of an Asian takeout restaurant reinforces a trope even though no text is doing the stereotyping — the same "is this relevant, or just a reflex association" question applies to visual choices, not only word choices.
