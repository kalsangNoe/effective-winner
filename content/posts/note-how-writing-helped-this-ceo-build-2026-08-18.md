## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Sam Corcos, founder of Levels (a metabolic health company reportedly worth $300M+), about how he built a remote-first, writing-centric operating culture. Corcos argues writing isn't just documentation — it's the mechanism for clear thinking, alignment across a distributed team, and defense against people rationalizing bad decisions after the fact. He walks through concrete systems: hypothesis-driven memos, retros with clinical pass/fail definitions, Loom videos paired with written memos, "Think Weeks," no-meeting days, and hiring practices (the "keeper test," treating hiring as a sales problem of talking people out of joining). He closes with a tangent on quitting news for a month and how it improved his focus and reading.

## Key ideas
- **Writing is thought, not communication.** Corcos: most people think they understand an idea until they try to write it down and discover they don't. He cites James Clear: "If you want to have a better understanding of the world than most people, spend a few extra hours each week processing your experiences... If you know you're not going to remember something from reading a book, try writing one."
- **Writing takes less time than people assume, but feels heavier.** He tracks his time rigorously and found he spends closer to 5% of his time writing, versus the 20% people (including himself) would have guessed — because deep-focus writing feels more cognitively expensive per hour than meetings or casual conversation.
- **Effort should scale with the stakes of the decision, not be applied uniformly.** He explicitly rejects "everyone write everything down" — a small app change might warrant a two-paragraph memo, while a major strategic bet warrants a 30-50 page document.
- **Writing "freezes thought" and prevents drift.** Verbal alignment decays like a game of telephone; a written memo is a stable reference everyone can be pointed back to when interpretations diverge, which he says is easier than trying to keep everyone aligned purely through conversation.
- **Open silos vs. closed silos.** The real distinction isn't sync vs. async, it's whether information is *findable* later. Email/DMs are closed silos — only looped-in people can ever find the discussion. Long-form docs and open comment threads are open silos anyone can search in the future. Levels built its own internal tool to make open silos the default and kill "the formation of information silos" (a phrase he attributes to a conversation with someone from Stripe).
- **Chat tools are "pseudo-async" and are actually worse than meetings for deep work.** He cites a RescueTime stat: the median tech worker never goes more than ~30 minutes without checking a communications tool. His fix: explicit blocks of true sync (e.g., "Meeting Monday, no-meeting Wednesday") and explicit blocks of true async, rather than a constant hybrid drip of interruptions — what he calls staying stuck in a "hyperactive hive mind."
- **Hiring is treated as a sales problem, in reverse.** Levels spends much of the interview trying to convince candidates *not* to join, describing the demanding, writing-heavy, low-acronym, high-transparency culture bluntly so only aligned people opt in — "95% of people would not be happy working in the culture that we've built."
- **The "keeper test" (borrowed from Netflix):** imagine an employee tells you they're quitting for another job — if your gut reaction isn't relief, you should be fighting to keep them; if it is relief, you already know you need to have the hard conversation now, not later.
- **Hypotheses, ROI framing, and retros form a scientific-method loop.** Before starting work, write a hypothesis with a specific numeric prediction (e.g., "this will deliver $90,000/month in business value") so it can be compared against competing projects and checked later — this closes the loop that lets people notice when they were "two orders of magnitude off" instead of quietly rationalizing the miss. Retros use clinical, non-subjective pass/fail definitions of the metric moving (rather than "did it feel like a win") specifically to make rationalization harder.
- **Communication is the last, most-skipped step of the scientific method.** Corcos: analysis nobody reads or acts on "hasn't cured cancer" — you've only solved it privately. Every project retro gets written up and also gets a short verbal summary in the team all-hands.
- **One-way doors vs. two-way doors determines how much writing a decision deserves.** Irreversible ("tattoo") decisions merit a full memo; reversible ("hat") decisions should just ship, and hypotheses for cheap two-way-door bets can be one line.
- **Think Weeks (borrowed from Bill Gates)** are full weeks blocked off from email/Slack for deep strategic writing. Corcos says it takes 2-3 days of disengagement before new creative ideas start surfacing, and his best strategic memos cluster around these weeks. He does them alongside a few other people, not alone — being near others in the same deep-focus headspace helps him avoid getting distracted.
- **Loom + written memo, paired.** For long documents (e.g., a 200-page memo), he pairs it with a 20-minute Loom walkthrough summarizing what matters, watchable at 2x, because most people won't read the whole thing but need the context.
- **"Add value to the internet."** The Levels blog's success (millions of visitors) comes from writing deeply researched, definitive content on underexplored topics (metabolic health) rather than chasing generic SEO copy — echoing Paul Graham's "make something people want" as the real marketing strategy: "Write a damn good piece."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Writing is thought. If you can't write it, you don't understand it as well as you think you do."
- "If you know you're not going to remember something from reading a book, try writing one." (James Clear, quoted by Corcos)
- "I thought it was 20% of my time. It was actually closer to 5%."
- "Writing freezes thought... [it's] the only way to get everyone on the same page."
- "95% of people would not be happy working in the culture that we've built. And the 5% who would be have been looking for this their entire lives."
- "If you want to build an average company, that's okay. But if you want to build an extraordinary company, [it takes] a lot more courage to push those things."
- "The medium is the message... if you're giving somebody tough feedback... you should do it on a video."
- "You've just solved it in your head. You have not cured cancer. You've just solved an isolated incident that's happened."
- "The best strategy for marketing will always be... write a damn good piece."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own work am I substituting talking-about-an-idea for actually writing it down — and would writing it expose that I don't understand it as well as I think?
- Do I have any equivalent of a "hypothesis" step before starting projects, with a falsifiable numeric prediction I could be proven wrong on later?
- Am I using "communication is the last step" as a lens on my own writing — do I have analysis or notes sitting unshared that have effectively never happened?
- Could I distinguish my own one-way-door vs. two-way-door decisions more explicitly, and calibrate how much writing/deliberation each deserves?
- What would a personal "keeper test" look like for commitments or projects I'm on the fence about continuing?
