## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Rosanne Cash about how she builds songs: from deconstructing Bob Dylan's lyrics as a young writer, to treating anger and grief as clarifying, generative fuel rather than things to avoid. Her core method is craft-before-freedom — master rhyme scheme and structure deliberately (like Picasso learning representational drawing before cubism) so intuition has something to work with. Most of her songs begin as a visual image rather than a theme, and she deliberately writes concrete "furniture" (a coffee cup, a slamming door) instead of abstract nouns like "loss" or "love." She also draws a throughline between her prose rhythm, her singing voice, and her songwriting, treating all three as expressions of the same underlying sensibility that has changed as she's aged.

## Key ideas

**Craft first, intuition second.** Cash's touchstone is a Linda Ronstadt line she's carried in her wallet: "Refine your skills so you can support your instincts." She compares this to Picasso mastering representational portraiture before earning the right to go abstract — "he knew how to do the basics and then he let it go... if you want to let it go, great. But you better know how to do it first."

**Deconstruction as deliberate practice.** In her 20s she literally wrote out Dylan's lyrics by hand and asked "how did he get from that to that? And why does that work?" She did the same with Guy Clark, Appalachian ballads, the Beatles, Elton John, and Joni Mitchell — studying not just the songs but the discipline behind the people who wrote them.

**Write the furniture, not the theme.** Her advice to young songwriters she teaches in master classes: "don't write about themes... get really specific about the details of it without saying the theme of it... talk about the furniture of it. The window, the door, the coffee cup, the table, the slamming." She illustrates this with "Sea of Heartbreak" — the opening line "The lights in the harbor don't shine for me" evokes grief without naming it.

**Anger and grief are generative, not just painful.** "Anger is one hell of a muse... it's one of the emotions where you're the most out of your head," and writer's block is "so much... just being in your head." Grief goes further: "You can't talk yourself out of grief," and its contours include not just sadness but "a kind of weird exhilaration... a storm that's moving through you." She wrote "Roses in the Fire" after literally throwing a bouquet into a fireplace mid-argument, and "The World Unseen" grew from a Psalm line ("I'm the sparrow on the roof") she read to her dying father.

**Songs usually start as a visual image, not an idea.** "Most songs start visually for me." "Motherless Children"-style songs aside, she describes writing "The Way We Move" by seeing headlights on a road while playing chords at the piano, before she knew what the song was about. The exception was "The Killing Fields," about lynchings in Arkansas, which she consciously designed as an Appalachian narrative ballad with a "bell curve" structure — present, deep past, back to present — before writing it.

**Ambiguity is a feature, not a failure of clarity.** Unlike prose, where she wants economy and clarity, in songs "sometimes you don't want people to understand clearly... I put the ambiguity back in." She cites her own line "a feather's not a bird, the rain is not the sea" as intentionally mysterious — "there's a mystery at the center of songwriting and I don't want to kill that in service of clarity."

**Voice changes with a life lived, not just skill.** She contrasts her younger and older singing voices the way she contrasts Joni Mitchell's two versions of "Both Sides Now" — citing Ray Charles: "you're a better singer at 50 than you are at 20 because your life shows up in your voice." Aging costs top notes but adds "refinement" and self-knowledge.

**Editing feedback has evolved.** She used to refuse feedback while a song was in process; now she co-writes with her husband John Leventhal, who gives unsolicited feedback, and she's learned to accept it. Internally, she watches for "red flags" — overused nature metaphors, words she leans on too often, anything "too literal or too specific or too expected."

**Truth over pretense, learned from watching fame up close.** Growing up as Johnny Cash's daughter made her wary of performed authenticity: "pretense makes me anxious... is this real? Is there real connection there?" She saw fame both connect her father to real people and, in other cases, "literally kill people."

**Music outlasts everything else.** She notes that Alzheimer's patients "remember songs and not their children's names," and closes with the story of her grandfather asking to hear a specific Mozart piece in the last five minutes of his life.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What's my equivalent of "deconstructing Dylan" — have I ever hand-copied a piece of writing I admire to study its structure, rather than just reading it?
- Am I writing about themes ("loss," "growth") instead of the furniture — the specific coffee cup, slamming door, headlights on a road — that would let the theme emerge on its own?
- Where in my own writing do I over-explain instead of trusting ambiguity to do the work Cash describes — "a clarity of intent behind the ambiguity"?
- Do I treat anger or grief as material to write from, or do I wait until I've calmed down and lost the "clarifying" charge she describes?
- Have I actually mastered the basic structure of the form I want to break — or am I skipping to abstraction without earning it, the way Picasso didn't?
