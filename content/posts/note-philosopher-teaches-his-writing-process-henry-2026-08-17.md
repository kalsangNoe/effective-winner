## TL;DR
David Perell interviews poet, novelist, and Zen teacher Henry Shukman about where writing actually comes from. Shukman's central claim: real writing is never deliberately chosen or planned — it comes through you from a part of you that isn't under conscious control, and the writer's job is to get out of the way and let it speak, then edit carefully afterward without "cutting live flesh." He ties this directly to his 35 years of meditation practice, arguing that meditation trains the same muscle writing needs — releasing control — and that both poetry and meditation are ultimately practices of returning to direct, unmediated experience of the present moment. Along the way he shares three of his own poems and draws lessons specific to fiction, memoir, poetry, and Zen koans.

## Key ideas
- **Writing is discovered, not decided.** Shukman's first poem at 13–14 came unbidden one evening after knowing an itinerant old man named Speedy who had "a different energy." He grabbed a notebook and "wrote it saying what it told me to say" — and felt trembling and alive in a new way afterward.
- **You cannot write a poem on command.** "If I sit down thinking, oh, it'd be good to write a poem about X... it won't work. It never works. What works is it comes up from some other source." This has held true for decades, even though there's always a real editing/tinkering process after the fact.
- **The "fear barrier."** Right before his best writing happens, Shukman notices a moment of fear — "am I ready for this?" — that he has to go through to let something deeper than his conscious mind speak. He frames this as the controlling, threat-scanning mind (evolutionarily wired to be far more alert to threats than rewards) resisting surrender.
- **Meditation and writing train the same skill.** Both are "again and again... a lesson in reducing control." He cites Jack Kerouac as proof this isn't about being "mellow and calm" — Kerouac was a serious Zen meditator (sesshin, fire-lookout retreats) whose wild, torrential prose came from the same capacity to "let it through."
- **Emotion recollected in tranquility.** Citing Wordsworth, Shukman says his most powerful poems (e.g., "Frozen Lake," written about a loved one's crisis) could only be written after distance from the raw event — not in the "first onslaught of grief and shock." Writing lets him "relive" and truly digest what he's lived through a second time.
- **Editing without amputating.** His editor Robin Robertson's rule: "you don't want to cut live flesh." Revision means getting back into the original spirit/mindset of the piece before touching it, not approaching it from a "different vantage" that risks blindly excising what was actually good.
- **Craft-by-medium, in his own words:**
  - *Fiction:* the protagonist needs both an immediate practical problem and a deeper spiritual/philosophical one (Pierre Bezukhov in *War and Peace* as example). Place is "the third most important character" — Homer's Aegean, Hardy's Wessex, Tolstoy's Russia are inseparable from the human story.
  - *Memoir:* know precisely what the narrow thread is — his memoir *One Blade of Grass* was "not about Henry's life," only about his journey into Zen; everything else got stripped away.
  - *Poetry:* the closest form to meditation — "a tracking of present-moment experience," including the experience of remembering.
  - *Koans:* enigmatic Zen phrases (e.g., "What is the sound of one hand?") designed to scramble ordinary logic and carry the practitioner into the same wonder/boundlessness the enlightened masters who spoke them were in. Etymology of "paradox" = beyond thought.
- **Discipline still matters.** Real writers write near-daily; when stuck, use Balzac's "automatic writing" (cover pages with gobbledygook just to get the engine turning) — echoed by Ed Sheeran's image of running the tap until dirty water clears. Coleridge wrote 6,000+ newspaper articles; volume across a lifetime (Ezra Pound on Hardy: "the clarity of a man who first written 3 million words") produces the clarity that later shows up in the concentrated work.
- **Writing is about intimacy with the world, not nature per se.** It doesn't require pastoral subject matter — a 14-year-old Shukman wrote about snowfall on a city street, the "shoe of light" cast by a lamp. "God is in the details... the wonder is in the details."
- **The body, not the mind, is where poetry lives.** "The real poet lives is the body, not the mind. The body uses the mind." Word choice should be embodied — he points to "crackles" and "sifts" in his own poem "First Snow" as words you feel in your fingertips.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I don't write the poem. If I sit down thinking, oh, it'd be good to write a poem about X... it won't work. It never works. What works is it comes up from some other source." — Henry Shukman
- "There's a little bit of a fear like a hump to get over or a little bit of a barrier to go through, and then the energy is there that's much deeper than the energy that I could come up with consciously." — Shukman, on the "fear barrier"
- "You don't want to cut live flesh." — Robin Robertson, quoted by Shukman on editing
- "The third most important character in every work of fiction is the place... All the greatest fiction knows that place is the bedrock." — Shukman
- "I think in a way the real poet lives is the body, not the mind. The body uses the mind." — Shukman
- "The clarity of a man who first written 3 million words" — Ezra Pound, on Thomas Hardy's late poetry, quoted by Shukman
- "Emotion recollected in tranquility" — Wordsworth, cited by Shukman as the "secret ingredient of poetry"
- "For them there is no consolation, nor is any needed." — closing line of Shukman's poem "Frozen Lake"

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing process do I recognize a "fear barrier" — a moment right before something honest comes out where I flinch and retreat to control?
- Am I trying to force pieces into existence by deciding topics in advance, rather than letting a "Balzac automatic-writing" warm-up surface what actually wants to be said?
- When I revise, am I reworking from the original spirit of the piece, or am I approaching it from a "different vantage" that risks cutting live flesh?
- What's the equivalent of "place as the third character" for the kind of nonfiction/essay writing I do — is there a setting, context, or structural bedrock I'm underusing?
- Do I give myself enough distance (Wordsworth's "tranquility") before writing about difficult experiences, or am I trying to write from inside the shock itself?
