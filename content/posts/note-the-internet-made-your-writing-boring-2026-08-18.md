## TL;DR
David Perell and The Cultural Tutor argue that writing (like design, architecture, and even accents) swings on a pendulum between maximalism and minimalism, and that we're currently in a minimalist era caused by convenience culture, word processors, spell-checkers, and the attention economy — one that has quietly stripped personality, texture, and "grandeur" out of how people write. Their claim isn't that minimalism is bad (it can be elegant and efficient), but that its *worst* form is standardizing and boring, because it never forces a writer to risk anything. The fix isn't picking a side but writing authentically — using maximalist techniques (rare words, etymology, longer sentences, breaking rules) as tools of contrast against a minimalist baseline, not as a costume.

## Key ideas
- **Style is a pendulum, not a ladder.** Victorian prose was maximalist; 16th-century prose (Chaucer, the King James Bible) was often as clear as anything written today — so "we're becoming more minimalist" isn't linear progress, it's a swing that has happened before and will swing back.
- **Definitions, cleaned of caricature.** The caricature of maximalism is Victorian purple prose ("portal" instead of "door"); the caricature of minimalism is Hemingway's short, choppy sentences. The real virtue of maximalism is precision through nuance — "emerald green" vs. "green," or a face that "looked green" — capturing texture that a generic word flattens.
- **The Microsoft Word test.** The Cultural Tutor pasted Edmund Spencer (framed in conversation as "Shakespeare") into Word and got squiggly red lines and "better" synonym suggestions — proof that a piece of software used by over a billion people, with ~95% of the word-processing market, is quietly training writers toward one homogeneous style via spell-check, grammar suggestions, limited fonts, and templates.
- **Convenience is the root cause.** Perell's framing: modern readers bail if writing doesn't deliver instantly ("if I can't get it straight away, why bother"), which pushes writers toward simplicity as a survival tactic rather than a craft choice — echoed by the observation that on Twitter/X, "the simpler, the catchier... if you can hook somebody in two seconds, it's not going to win" otherwise.
- **We may be less literate than 150 years ago — in a specific sense.** Not literacy rates (those are far higher now), but the *practiced skill* of deep reading and writing. People like John Ruskin spent entire days reading and writing with no internet, radio, or podcasts competing for attention; modern readers "spend more time typing... and less time doing the art of writing" and "more time reading... but less time doing the art of reading" (Perell's distinction between skimming Twitter and deconstructing *East of Eden*).
- **Letters vs. texts.** Historical biographies (T.E. Lawrence, Erasmus) draw on thousands of introspective letters; a modern biography would have to draw on emails "fired off on the subway." Letter writing forced sustained, solitary reflection that texting and email don't — a "genre" that has effectively disappeared.
- **Rewriting, not writing, makes you better** (citing Paul Graham/"Ven Cateshra" — an auto-caption mangling of a Perell-cited writer's name, likely a mis-transcription): first drafts don't improve a writer; the compounding is in going back and sharpening — otherwise everyone who's sent 50,000 emails would write like Shakespeare.
- **Minimalism's defense: engineered elegance.** Perell counters with *Atomic Habits* — the word "atomic" packs three meanings (smallest unit, explosive power, central to a system) into one word, showing minimalism at its best is maximal *compression* of meaning, not absence of it.
- **The yin-yang rule of emphasis.** Whichever style is your default, use its opposite sparingly for emphasis: mostly-maximalist prose can land a point with one short, blunt sentence; mostly-minimalist prose can spotlight importance with one ornate paragraph. The minority mode is the tool.
- **Practical advice for reclaiming maximalism:** read old and obscure books (not what everyone else on your feed is reading) to see what's structurally possible; turn off spell-check and Grammarly; learn more words — not thesaurus-flipping, but learning what words *actually* mean; learn etymology, since word origins often contain whole essays (e.g., "passion" comes from suffering — the Passion of the Christ — reframing "find your passion" as "what am I willing to suffer for").
- **Churchill case study (closing segment):** "We shall fight on the beaches..." is structurally repetitive filler building suspense toward one payoff line — "We shall never surrender." The content of the middle list is nearly interchangeable ("the order of the locations is immaterial"); the eight-word compressed version ("We shall fight everywhere and we shall never surrender") is efficient but forgettable. The 31-word maximalist version is what people still quote a century later.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "If there's one thing human beings cannot handle, it is being bored." — The Cultural Tutor
- "Minimalism is a lack of personality, a lack of edge... it feels like the sharp edges of humanity have been sanded down." — The Cultural Tutor
- "That this program that's been designed in Silicon Valley apparently knows how to write better than Shakespeare." — The Cultural Tutor, on Word's grammar suggestions
- "Simplification is not itself a virtue... just because it's simpler does not mean it's better." — The Cultural Tutor
- "The point of writing is you put ideas, words onto the page and now they're still. And now that they're still, you can go deeper and deeper and deeper." — David Perell
- "Language is like clay... we think we've been given a Lego box... instruction manual and you have to build it in that way." — The Cultural Tutor
- "The worst fault in all writing and speaking is circumlocution" — quoting Quintilian, cited by The Cultural Tutor to clarify he isn't defending needless padding
- "Eight words could have had the same meaning, but that wouldn't have been memorable. We wouldn't be talking about it almost a century later." — David Perell, on Churchill's "We shall fight..." speech

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I defaulting to the "safe, convenient" word instead of the precise one — and what would I lose by writing for someone willing to look a word up?
- Am I using tools (spell-check, AI writing assistants, style guides) in a way that's quietly homogenizing my voice the way Word homogenizes everyone who uses it?
- What's the "letter-writing" equivalent I could reclaim — some slower, more introspective writing practice that isn't optimized for a feed?
- In my own pieces, what's my default mode (minimalist or maximalist), and where's the one moment I could use its opposite for emphasis, yin-yang style?
- Do I read differently — with underlining, re-reading, deconstruction — or do I mostly skim, even when reading something I claim to be studying?
