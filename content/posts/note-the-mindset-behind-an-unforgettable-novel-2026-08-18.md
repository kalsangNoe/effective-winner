## TL;DR
Amor Towles (author of *A Gentleman in Moscow* and *The Lincoln Highway*) explains his process for building novels that feel physically inhabitable to the reader. His central claim: description isn't about accumulating detail, it's about seeing a scene through a specific character's consciousness so vividly that the "right" details surface on their own. He's a heavy outliner — he spends years filling notebooks and fully visualizing a book before writing chapter one — because knowing everything in advance frees the "poetic," subconscious side of his brain during actual drafting. He also talks about vocabulary as something gathered from many "languages" (French, finance, baseball, technical jargon), the difference between page-turners and literary urgency, why he distrusts the tortured-artist myth, and how ordinary scenes (a Carnegie Hall recording, an unusual bar patron) become the seed — the "opening of doors" — for entire stories.

## Key ideas

- **Description should come from character, not the writer.** Rather than asking "how would I describe coffee arriving," Towles asks what the *character* (Count Rostov, an aristocrat) would notice — the waiter must have run up the stairs because the coffee wasn't a degree cooler. He says roughly 90% of the passages readers find most moving are lines he'd never say in his own life — they came from imagining himself as someone else entirely.
- **Vocabulary is gathered from many "languages."** French entered *A Gentleman in Moscow* because 1900s Moscow aristocracy actually spoke French to each other, not Russian. He treats baseball, finance, city streets, ethnic communities, and technical/scientific fields as their own vocabularies a writer should tune their ear to.
- **Research by reading contemporaneous fiction, not textbooks.** Before writing *The Lincoln Highway* (set in 1954), he read four novels all written within about 18 months of each other and set around that time — Flannery O'Connor's *A Good Man Is Hard to Find*, James Baldwin's *Go Tell It on the Mountain*, *The Man in the Gray Flannel Suit*, and Raymond Chandler's *The Long Goodbye* — to recover the period's different sensibilities rather than relying on Wikipedia-style facts.
- **Avoid "landmark" details — cliché period signaling.** His example: a foreign writer asked to depict 1964 American suburbia might reach for "Bird's Eye frozen peas" and "the Beatles on the radio" — obvious, expected markers. Instead he asks what a specific 8-year-old would actually notice (the physical sensation of sliding a frozen brick of peas out of the box) and layers in an emotional subtext (the mother's hidden affair or being cheated on) that the child senses without understanding.
- **He is a heavy outliner, and outlining serves the *poetic* brain, not the analytical one.** Every novel starts from a one-sentence premise (for *A Gentleman in Moscow*: "guy lives in a hotel for a long period of time," conceived in about 90 minutes in a Geneva hotel, with the scaffolding for nearly all key events written within three days). He then spends a couple of years filling handwritten notebooks, scene by scene, out of order, visualizing everything — geography, characters' backgrounds, dialogue — before writing chapter one. The logic: if he hasn't solved a scene's mechanics in advance, the analytical left brain has to solve it while writing, which dampens the "poetic surprises" that come from the more subconscious right brain.
- **Editing is about hearing your own writing coldly.** The test for a slow passage: "do I find this section boring?" — if it bores him on a third read, it will bore the reader. He distinguishes literary "urgency" (compelling because of psychology or sentence-craft) from the plot-driven urgency of a page-turner (he cites Lee Child's Jack Reacher novels) — literary fiction can and should include deliberately slow parts.
- **First draft is written for himself; the edit is for the reader — his "covenant."** He writes drafts indulgently, following whims and digressions. Editing means turning the lens toward the reader and cutting redundancy, cliché, and anything that only satisfies his own vanity. He frames this as an obligation: readers invest money and time in his book, so "I owe it to them."
- **Craft is teachable through repetition, not formula.** Though he hasn't taught (unlike peers like Michael Cunningham or Jeffrey Eugenides), he believes young writers benefit most from writing across many identities — different genders, ages, classes, races — because it reveals that there is no single "how to describe a room"; the approach changes with the character's interior mindset.
- **He rejects the "tortured artist" trope.** Citing Matisse and Picasso as counterexamples to Van Gogh, he argues history selectively remembers extreme suffering artists. His own observation from decades of accomplished writer friends: most work regular hours, at a desk, every morning, and treat inspiration as something that shows up *because* you're at the desk — not something you wait for.
- **He predominantly reads dead authors** in a 20-year-old reading group of four friends, on the theory that history is bad at preserving genius but very good (over roughly 50 years) at filtering out mediocrity — so reading the survivors is a more reliable use of limited time.
- **Stories begin as "the opening of doors."** A vivid but unfinished real moment (an old man illegally recording a Carnegie Hall concert, a hand-painted mural bar) triggers a cascade of narrative possibilities — he compares the feeling to synapses firing, all the doors of a mansion opening at once, and treats that expansive feeling as the signal that an idea has enough scope to become a novel or story.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "One of the most important things is to make the reader feel like they're living the experience in the book... they can see things unfolding, their thought processes and feelings are turned on by what they're reading about."
- "I plan, design, and outline before I start writing... the reason that I do that is in order to free up the right side of my brain when I'm in the writing process — the more subconscious, more dream-oriented, more poetic side."
- "90% of the time when someone comes to me and says [a passage] was so moving... it is something which I would never have said in the course of my own life... I have imagined for a moment that I am this person who I am not."
- "Is this slow for me — do I find this section boring? That's never a good sign... if I find it boring on the third read, the reader is definitely going to run the risk of finding it boring."
- "They've invested in my art, and I owe it to them."
- "Inspiration is what comes to them when they're at their desk... it's not something you wait to become inspired and then go to your desk, because if you do that you will never make any progress."
- "History is not very good at capturing all that is great in art... but history is very good at discarding all that is mediocre... and the amount of time that takes is something like 50 years."
- "It's as if you walked into a mansion... and all the doors start opening up... that's the feeling of when a new idea — I can tell it's got promise."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I actually know my characters' (or subjects') backgrounds and sensibilities well enough that specific, non-generic details would surface naturally — or am I still reaching for "landmark" clichés to signal a setting?
- Where in my own writing process do I let the analytical brain solve problems that should have been decided in advance, at the cost of more surprising phrasing?
- What's my equivalent of Towles's reading group or trusted first readers — do I have people who give "thoughtful, careful feedback" before I show work to a wider audience?
- What was the last "door" — a small, real, unresolved moment — that I noticed but didn't follow up on? What would happen if I opened it?
- Am I editing for myself (indulgent, exploratory) or for the reader (cutting for economy) at the right stage, or am I conflating the two passes?
