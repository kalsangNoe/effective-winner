## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Packy McCormick (Not Boring) about the mechanics behind writing a newsletter that grew from 20 subscribers to 220,000+ and seeded a $40M venture fund. The conversation moves through the tactical layer — titles, intros, essay length, editing, using AI — before landing on the deeper claim: writing well is mostly about telling the truth with "uncommon honesty," and doing it consistently enough (200+ essays) that you can't keep faking a persona. Packy frames his whole career — writing, investing, the fund — as one integrated practice of gathering information, synthesizing it in public, and building trust with an audience.

## Key ideas
- **Titles and images are a "messy, messy process."** He writes a rough title, puts it in brackets above the draft, and revisits it after the piece is written; images matter less over time because thumbnails get compressed on social feeds anyway.
- **The introduction is a "hook," not throat-clearing.** He compares it to skiing a fresh run — pick your line before you commit, because once the piece's frame is set it's hard to change direction mid-essay.
- **Barbell essay lengths, borrowed from Sam Parr.** Either very short (a few hundred words, one idea, TikTok-length) or very long (2,000+ words, the "definitive take") — the flabby middle length doesn't work as well.
- **Write for "my mom to understand what you do."** The recurring compliment he gets on deep dives is that a reader's mom finally understood their job; his job is to arrive at a topic slightly ahead of the audience and translate expert language into plain language, then hyperlink out for readers who want to go deeper (a technique he credits to Byrne Hobart's The Diff).
- **Voice comes from repetition, not design.** Cultivated at Write of Passage by sitting at the intersection of pop culture, business strategy, and technology; over ~1 million published words the "caricature" of Packy solidified and now sometimes limits what the real person can write about.
- **The core writing lesson, credited to Kevin Kelly: "tell your story with uncommon honesty."** He connects this to Robert Trivers' research on self-deception — the actual work is figuring out what you truly think, since we're built to lie to ourselves; if he nails that, the piece "flies" with minimal further effort.
- **Process: get excited, don't research first.** From Write of Passage: write the first draft fast while still excited, without editing or researching, then go do the research and write again — a roughly weekly cycle of excitement → research → write → repeat until something clicks. He'll frequently scrap a draft entirely and restart if it hits a wall.
- **Deadlines force the best writing.** His hardest, best essays often get rewritten start-to-finish between 4:30am and send time; a real deadline surfaces the version he "should have written all along."
- **Flow is real and physical.** He gets early-onset-arthritis-like finger pain, so he now dictates by pacing around the room talking into his phone — holding it to his mouth like a phone call so people don't think he's talking to himself — then structures the transcript afterward.
- **How he uses AI (Claude/ChatGPT):** as an overly complimentary editor he has to discount ("It's an A... one of the best things ever written"), as a "cynical attacker" prompted to poke holes in an argument, and as a "bomb thesaurus" — feeding it a sentence with a word in quotes and asking for ten synonyms that fit the intended meaning.
- **Sponsored deep dives are core to the business, not a compromise.** He avoided a paywall because he didn't want to gatekeep half his audience; instead he grew sponsorship revenue by writing deep dives on companies (sometimes ones the fund has invested in), and founders often say those are their favorite pieces because of the access and care put into telling their story accurately.
- **Writing and investing are the same information-gathering skill.** Founders read the newsletter and open up to him; on the investing side, a month-and-a-half fundraising process forces founders to condense years of context into a memo, which he calls a uniquely efficient way to absorb how smart people actually think.
- **200 essays is enough to force self-honesty.** "You can lie to yourself for piece one... you can't lie to yourself for piece 200" — sustained public writing eventually strips away any performed intellectual or academic style because it's physically unsustainable to fake.

- Packy McCormick, on sustained writing: "You can lie to yourself for piece one, you can lie [for a while], you can't lie to yourself for piece 200."
- Packy McCormick, on his writing process: "Get excited. Then write. And then go [do research]... condense a week into four days."
- Packy McCormick, on sponsorship vs. paywalls: "If I start paywalling even half of my [audience], I don't want to do that."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I picking a clear "line" (hook/frame) before I start writing, the way Packy skis a fresh run — or am I discovering the frame mid-draft and forcing readers through my confusion?
- Where in my own writing am I still performing a persona instead of practicing "uncommon honesty" — and would 200 consecutive pieces expose that?
- Could I use AI more deliberately as a cynical-attacker prompt rather than just accepting the first complimentary pass?
- Do I have a barbell in my own writing lengths, or am I defaulting to a mediocre middle length that Packy explicitly avoids?
- Is there a version of "write for one person" I could apply to make my own pieces feel less generic and more like a specific letter to a specific reader?
