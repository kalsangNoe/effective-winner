## TL;DR
David Perell interviews journalist and author Johann Hari (Chasing the Scream, Lost Connections, Stolen Focus, Magic Pill) about how he researches and writes his books. Hari's method starts from a personal question, not a thesis; he does 200-300 interviews per book, mostly in person, guided by a fixed list of questions pinned to his door. His interviewing philosophy centers on radical openness, refusing to demonize or valorize subjects, and building emotional connection before presenting evidence. His writing process is built around clarity, killing anything that doesn't serve the book's core questions, and confessing doubt to the reader rather than hiding it.

## Key ideas
- **Start from a personal problem, not a systemic thesis.** Every book begins with something Hari needs to understand in his own life — addiction in his family (Chasing the Scream), his own depression (Lost Connections), his own worsening attention (Stolen Focus). The systemic insight comes later, from following the research, not at the outset.
- **The "bee and the beehive" method.** Borrowed from Annie Dillard's *The Writing Life*: you don't know where the hive is, so you catch a bee, let it go, follow it as far as you can, then catch another. Applied to research, Hari writes a list of questions, identifies who could carry the story, and lets each interview lead to the next ("who else should I talk to?"), doing 200-300 interviews per book even though ~80% never make the final cut.
- **Interviewing philosophy: look for a reason to love the person.** Start from "this person knows something I don't know," whether a homeless person or a Yale professor. Prepare thoroughly, but enter with radical openness rather than "interviewing for an angle." Ask open-ended questions ("How did you feel?" not "Did you feel bad?") to avoid leading the answer.
- **Don't demonize, and don't sanitize either.** With Sheriff Joe Arpaio, understanding his childhood (his mother died in childbirth after he was born) didn't excuse his actions but explained them — going in "the ecstasy of sanctimony" would have gotten nothing. Equally, with the homeless people in his forthcoming Vegas book, valorizing them as "angels" disrespects them as much as demonizing them; people "contain multitudes" and deserve the dignity of being shown as real and contradictory.
- **The "12-year-old test" for structuring a book.** Faced with a wardrobe of transcripts "longer than War and Peace," Hari sits down and tells the whole story out loud to a 12-year-old, recording it — spoken compression forces out what doesn't matter, in about 15 minutes.
- **A fixed list of questions anchors the whole project.** For Stolen Focus: "Is our attention really getting worse? If it is, why? Who is doing this to us? How do we fix it? Where in the world is fixing it?" — pinned to his door for three years. His edit on Fitzgerald's "kill your darlings": kill anything that doesn't serve the questions you're asking, regardless of how beautifully it's written.
- **Confess doubt instead of hiding it.** The instinct is to suppress uncertainty to sound persuasive; Hari argues the opposite works better. Example: he stalled on visiting Portugal for Chasing the Scream because he feared its drug decriminalization hadn't worked and would undercut his whole argument — naming that fear to the reader became part of the book. Writer's block, in his view, is usually a signal of some unacknowledged truth, not a malfunction to push through.
- **Editing formula: earn every insight with a story, and earn every emotional connection before arguing.** Drawing on "deep canvassing" work from the LA LGBT Center, he opens each book with a moment of personal vulnerability to build emotional connection before presenting a shared goal, because "once we've agreed on our goal, we will weigh evidence very well." Insights delivered without a story ("the war on drugs was created for racist reasons") don't land; insights earned by a complicated, morally ambiguous story do.
- **The editing pipeline.** Write start to finish (never out of sequence). Shelve it a month. Print it out, cut ruthlessly. Rewrite. Read it on his phone for a different perspective. Rewrite again. Send to three trusted friends, then his editors, then to the people he wrote about for their input — then rewrite once more before fact-checking and copyediting.
- **Topic selection.** His best books sit at the intersection of "a major problem affecting a lot of people" (addiction, depression, attention) and "a screaming public debate" (like Ozempic/Magic Pill) — versus passion projects like his in-progress Chomsky biography, which he doesn't expect to be commercial.
- **Currents vs. winds.** His analogy for why most journalism misses the point: winds (single events, data, charts) are visible but weak; currents (underlying, slow-moving forces) are less visible but decisive. Years-long research projects are an attempt to trace the currents, not chase the winds.
- **Career arc: from arguing to listening.** Early in his career (ages 21-31) he wrote both persuasive newspaper op-eds and on-the-ground reporting, and realized reporting was the more honest mode — a pre-existing narrative "blocks you from seeing what's in front of you." The biggest change in his writing life: "I talk much less and listen much more."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The world isn't made of atoms, it's made of stories." — quoting poet Muriel Rukeyser, used to open the episode
- "I always start from a position of this person knows something I don't know." — Johann Hari, on his baseline stance entering any interview
- "There are evil acts. There are no evil people. People are the way they are because of reasons, things that happened to them." — Hari, on interviewing Sheriff Joe Arpaio
- "Kill anything that isn't serving the questions you're asking and the story you're telling." — Hari, tightening F. Scott Fitzgerald's "kill your darlings"
- "Sometimes the reason not to write your essay should be the subject of your essay." — Hari, on confessing doubt to the reader
- "In a jury we have agreed on our goal... once we've agreed on our goal we will weigh evidence very well. But if we don't agree on our goal, we're not going to be good at weighing evidence." — Hari, citing researchers Sperber and Mercier
- "You need your nausea. It will tell us what's wrong with you." — a Vietnamese doctor to Hari, after he nearly died from a poisoned apple; became the framing metaphor for Lost Connections' argument that depression and anxiety are signals, not malfunctions
- "The time to make your mind up about someone is never." — line from *The Philadelphia Story*, quoted by Hari as his approach to writing about people

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I "interviewing for an angle" — gathering material to confirm something I already decided, instead of genuinely not knowing the answer?
- Do I have a fixed, written list of core questions for my current projects, the way Hari pins his to his door? Would that discipline cut the rabbit-holing I tend to do?
- Where am I currently blocked, and is the block actually a signal that I'm avoiding an uncomfortable truth rather than a lack of material?
- Am I earning my insights with stories, or asserting them and hoping the reader takes it on faith?
- What's the "current" beneath the "wind" in the topics I write about — the slow, underlying force I'm missing because I'm reacting to the latest event or data point?
