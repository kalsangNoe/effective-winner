Most meetings fail before they start: three-quarters of people have never been trained to run one, and most default to 30+ minutes out of habit rather than need. A meeting run well applies the same discipline as concise writing — a stated objective up front, a hard time cap, and an explicit close — to the live, synchronous format.

## Key Concepts
- **The meeting is decided before it starts.** Confirm a meeting is actually necessary (privacy or brutal honesty needs might mean a 1:1 instead), then send a one-sentence objective and a ≤3-bullet agenda the night before, giving people time to actually think.
- **Bezos's "study hall" memo model.** Amazon replaces PowerPoint with a silently-read, narratively structured 6-page memo at the start of each meeting — narrative prose is harder to hide fuzzy thinking in than a slide deck, so the format itself forces clearer thinking before the discussion starts.
- **Default to 20 minutes, not 30–60.** Most meeting lengths are inherited habit, not a calculated need. Slack's internal norm of 25- or 50-minute blocks (leaving a buffer before the next meeting) is cited as a smarter default; 5–10 minute "micro-meetings" are worth testing for narrow decisions.
- **Structured open and close.** Open with the one-sentence objective as a headline, state "why it matters to this specific group right now," and name the decisions that need to be made. Reserve the last 2 minutes explicitly to restate decisions and owners, then send a same-day bulleted recap email — which often surfaces points people thought of after the meeting, sometimes preventing a follow-up meeting entirely.
- **On-time culture is a leadership signal.** Karl Rove was locked out of an Oval Office meeting for being late in the first week of the Bush administration and was never late again — starting exactly on time (and thanking people for being there on time) establishes the meeting's seriousness for everyone who follows.
- **Facilitation is active, not passive.** Interrupt tangents with a light "Off-topic!" rather than letting drift eat the time box; explicitly invite quieter participants to weigh in, since they're statistically likely to have the sharpest, least-obvious point.

## Details
The protocol has three phases. **Pregame**: confirm necessity → write a 1-sentence objective + ≤3-bullet agenda → send it the night before. **During**: enforce a hard time cap (20 min default) → open with the headline objective → state why it matters to this group → name the decisions needed → actively guide focus and invite quiet voices → close the last 2 minutes with explicit takeaways and owners. **Postgame**: same-day bulleted recap email while it's still fresh.

The unifying idea with concise-writing practice is that a meeting's opening sentence is functionally a lede + Axiom: it has to answer "what is this" and "why does it matter to you, right now" in the first breath, or attention drifts immediately — 90% of people admit to daydreaming in meetings and 72% do other work during them.
