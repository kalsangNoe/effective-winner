## TL;DR
Lulu Cheng Meservey (comms advisor to Substack, Anduril, Activision Blizzard) argues the old PR playbook — publicists and journalists as gatekeepers — is dead, replaced by founders "going direct": speaking in the first person, without filtering, to a decentralized audience. She lays out a practical framework — message, medium, messenger — for shaping a comms strategy, argues that raw, imperfect, emotional writing beats polished corporate copy, and warns that attention is worthless unless converted into something concrete (recruits, sales, funding). The conversation closes with concrete techniques: how to ask for favors without burning relationship capital, how to take a stand without splitting your base, and how to write an apology (the CrowdStrike rewrite).

## Key ideas

- **Go direct doesn't mean doing everything yourself or boycotting media.** It means the founder — the only person who can speak about the vision in the first person — has to personally originate some of what represents the company, in blog posts, events, or government relations, rather than handing it all to PR intermediaries.
- **Opinions became decentralized**, killing the old curator model (six TV channels, a dozen news outlets). A meme from an anonymous account can now spread faster than a CNN story, so founders must speak directly rather than rely on centralized media to carry their message.
- **Perfect writing is now a liability.** Corporate polish "loses its soul." Better to be bad and honest than textbook-correct and dead — she invokes Orwell's rule that it's better to break every rule than write something barbaric (or, here, boring). People now engineer *moments of spontaneity* (Obama's scripted "ums," TikToks filmed "in the car," leaving the button-press in a video) because inauthentic-seeming authenticity performs better than polish.
- **Ship-to-yap ratio.** A founder can be extremely vocal (Elon tweets constantly) without it being a red flag, as long as the company is visibly shipping. The danger sign is founders who are all talk — "shipping blogs, shipping tweets, but where's the product?"
- **The message/medium/messenger framework** (borrowed, she notes, from Mao's propaganda strategy):
  - *Message* is highest-leverage — get the words wrong and no amount of podcast bookings or press hits matters. A good message usually isn't novel; it gives shape and a name to something people already feel but can't articulate (example: "founder mode" — it didn't invent a concept, it gave existing frustration a name and permission).
  - *Medium* means finding people's "cultural erogenous zones" — what they're already obsessed with — and delivering the message where they already look for information (Stripe going after developers specifically on Hacker News, not mass press). She frames the message as medicine that needs a "candy coating" to get people to want it.
  - *Messenger* must have earned authority for that specific claim. The founder should say the vision; the lowest-ranking employee has more credibility saying "the boss is great" than the boss does; investors, not the founder, should talk about market size. A paid spokesperson with no real stake "neutralizes" the message entirely.
- **Reading the cultural erogenous zone**: watch *who* is talking about a topic (which status pocket), *how* they feel (resentment/jealousy shows up loudly and publicly; cringe/embarrassment shows up as silence — "read what's not in the room"), and the *trajectory* (founder mode went from cool to cringe in under 36 hours).
- **Writing from experience creates a monopoly.** "Competition is for losers" applies to comms too — an opinion anyone could hold has no reason to be bought from you, but no one else has your exact experience, so speaking from it is inherently differentiated and credible. She also flags the "I spent 487 hours learning X" genre as having curdled into engagement-farming slop.
- **AI can't do certain things in writing**: it can simulate but not feel emotion, and it can't hold conviction under pressure ("even the models are telling you you're wrong and you're like screw it, this is what I believe"). Cleaning up manifestos with AI often removes the "wrongness" that gave them their punch.
- **Favors are not a renewable resource.** Directly asking people to share/amplify content usually produces resentment or grudging compliance. The fix: frame it as an FYI tied to something the person already cares about, so sharing becomes their own choice (category three: "they wanted to and you gave them the opportunity"), not an obligation.
- **Two comms tests**: (1) Could you say this without a call-to-action for money in the next sentence? (2) If your company didn't exist, what could you say that would make your audience feel understood? Both push toward leading with genuine relevance to the audience, not self-interest.
- **Taking a stand without splitting your base**: draw the line in the sand around "us vs. them who don't need us" (Anduril/Palantir's "the West has superior values, period") rather than an arbitrary partisan line (Coke vs. Pepsi) that fractures your own people for no gain. She contrasts this with Apple's current "Think Different" nostalgia vs. today's feeling of being pushed to use features (the Genmoji ad inside Settings) that serve Apple, not the user.
- **Pressure = force / area.** Concentrate the message on the few people who matter and say one thing, not ten (Steve Jobs's "Stay hungry, stay foolish" — nobody remembers the rest of that speech). Slogans work by repetition of a few words, not whole paragraphs, or they become "background noise" — or worse, robotic.
- **The CrowdStrike apology rewrite**: the original statement used passive voice ("customers impacted by a defect found") to avoid responsibility. Her rewrite used first person, named the CEO, and stated "this came from an error that is my responsibility." Her theory: an upset customer will drag an unbothered company down to their emotional level; a company that comes in already devastated lets the customer pull *them* back up — illustrated by the coat-shop story where a furious boss defuses an angry customer faster than an apologetic clerk.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "The crux of what it means is for the founder... to speak directly to the audience without middlemen, without screens, without filtering it through all this kind of PR corpo talk."
- "I would rather see writing that is suboptimal, that has personality, that has intent and has conviction than writing that is like textbook correct."
- "There has to be a ship-to-yap ratio."
- "The message is the highest leverage thing to get right... it's like finding product market fit with a message where the message is the product."
- "You want to create a lab-made virus with gain-of-function virality that is designed to maximally spread, as opposed to something that can't spread."
- "Favors are not a renewable resource... you've actually asked a lot. You've burned through more relationship capital than you might think."
- "You should draw a line in the sand, but that line should be gerrymandered to fit around your people."
- "To increase your pressure, reduce your surface area."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I still optimizing for "perfect" instead of honest and specific — and what would the deliberately-imperfect version look like?
- What's my equivalent of a "cultural erogenous zone" right now — a thing my audience is already obsessed with that I could give shape and a name to, instead of inventing a new topic?
- Am I asking people for favors (shares, feedback, intros) in a way that creates obligation rather than genuine excitement — category two instead of category three?
- If my project/newsletter didn't exist, what could I say that would make my actual readers feel understood, independent of anything I'm trying to sell them?
- Where have I drawn a line that splits my own audience for no real gain, versus one that unites "my people" against people I never needed anyway?
