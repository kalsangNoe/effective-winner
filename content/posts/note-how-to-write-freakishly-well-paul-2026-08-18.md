## TL;DR
Paul Harding, who won the Pulitzer for his debut novel *Tinkers*, argues that great fiction comes from patient, precise observation rather than outlines, plot, or received language. He treats character as something that emerges from description (not the other way around), rejects "message" fiction because a lesson closes a book off rather than opening it, and pushes his students to write ambitiously — for the smartest possible reader — instead of dumbing things down. His method is almost musical: he was a drummer before he was a novelist, and he composes sentences by tempo and rhythm as much as by meaning. He writes roughly ten times more manuscript than he keeps (a thousand pages down to 150) and treats revision as an "insurance policy," not a punishment.

## Key ideas
- **No outlines, writing as improvisation.** Harding compares his process to Buster Keaton stepping into open space without knowing where the girder is — he wants to see "what language gives me and what experience gives me," trusting that complex systems produce emergent properties you couldn't predict beforehand.
- **Observation is a trainable skill, not talent.** The core discipline he teaches is dropping "received, acculturated, or habituated language" — the pre-fab phrases you use to get through your day — and re-approaching a scene pre-linguistically, without presuming you already know what you'll find.
- **The Tinkers ceiling-crack example.** He shows how he builds an extended metaphor by relentlessly asking "then what would happen?" — cracks in the ceiling → roof caves in → sky falls → stars fall → black — arriving at an image of a funeral shroud purely by following literal, concrete steps, not by reaching for symbolism.
- **Slow down.** The two words he says he wants on his headstone, and the thing he tells students almost 100% of the time applies to. Patience — "really taking in what you're looking at" instead of ramming a plot through — is a skill separate from talent that has to be learned.
- **Description *becomes* character.** Rather than shuttling between interior and exterior, Harding uses Marilynne Robinson's term "co-extensivity": there's no gap between inner and outer, so how a character notices and describes the world (the Boston Common's layered light, say) is how character gets built on the page.
- **Write for smart readers, not everyone.** "Don't write your books for bad readers. Write them for brilliant, big-brained and bighearted people." He wants a book so rich that returning readers keep finding more in it (his example: people rereading *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance* six times), unlike propaganda, which "gives you everything at the beginning."
- **Plot is Newtonian; consciousness is quantum.** He deliberately keeps plot minimal (*Tinkers* is a literal countdown: "eight days before he died") so there's room and time to saturate the page with a character's associative, non-linear consciousness instead of mechanical cause-and-effect.
- **No lessons, no messages.** "If somebody reads my book and gets what the point is, they never have to think about it again." Fiction persuades by recognition, not argumentation — the reader thinks "I've felt that but never seen it put into words," rather than being handed a thesis.
- **Maximum density, maximum readability.** His ambition is to write 150-page books that read "800 pages deep" rather than wide — as substantive as *War and Peace* without being a slog, wanting the prose so lucid the reader forgets they're reading.
- **Reading dictionaries daily.** He reads Webster's 1913 dictionary every day, tracing word etymology (e.g., "temptation" originally meant "a true test of character," and "-ly" historically never attached to negative words — "happily" but not "sadly") to get sentences with more dimensionality without using words so obscure they stop the reader.
- **Ambitious reading, personal canon.** He set out as an undergrad with the ambition to be "a good reader," and believes your writing can only be as good as the best stuff you've read and the best readings you can give it — deliberately trying to write a book "as good as *The Sound and the Fury*."
- **The drummer's ear for prose.** He composes prose by tempo, rhythm, and dynamics before he knows literal meaning — pulling back or pushing on the beat the way a drummer keeps time — and once made ~250 line-level revisions to *Tinkers* for a reprint, mostly commas and rhythm.
- **A thousand pages down to 150.** He's unafraid of a cluttered manuscript, letting in everything he's currently interested in (paintings, music) and then distilling — "systole and diastole" — because pulling back from too much is easier than padding out too little.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I love to just light out for the territories without a compass, without a map... I just love seeing what language gives me and what experience gives me."
- "It's slow down. Slow down. Just slow down, slow down, slow down."
- "Description actually becomes character... I'm never doing something just to make like a nice rococo painterly like look at Paul, he can write like paintings."
- "Don't write your books for bad readers. Write them for brilliant, big-brained and bighearted people who will love you for feeding their minds with feasts of beauty."
- "If somebody reads my book and gets what the point is, they never have to think about it again."
- "I want it to be maximum density with maximum readability... every word should be apt. There's no showing off."
- "You have to get it wrong in a particular way... nobody ever has to read what stinks, right? But in order to get it right, you have to get it wrong."
- "My novels are no more, no less than just like literary MRIs of my imagination, of my brain."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I reaching for "off the rack" language instead of slowing down to actually observe the thing in front of me?
- Am I writing toward a message/takeaway that closes the piece off, or toward an experience that opens something up for the reader?
- What would it look like to write something "800 pages deep" instead of wide — dense with what I actually care about rather than padded to length?
- Do I have a personal canon I'm consciously writing in conversation with, the way Harding treats Emerson, Melville, and Shakespeare as "aunts and uncles"?
- Am I revising toward precision (cutting until it's exactly right) or just polishing surface-level issues without going back to the underlying observation?
