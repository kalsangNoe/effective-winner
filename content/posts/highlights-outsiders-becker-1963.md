# Outsiders—Studies in the Sociology of Deviance by Howard S. Becker

**Source:** The Free Press, New York, 1963

## TL;DR
This foundational sociology text challenges conventional theories that treat deviance as an inherent property of certain behaviors or individuals. Becker argues instead that deviance is socially constructed through processes of rule-making and labeling: social groups create deviance by making rules and applying labels to those who violate them. The concept of "outsider" is relative—defined by those with power to impose their definitions. This perspective transformed criminology, sociology, and social theory by shifting focus from individual pathology to power dynamics and social reactions.

## Key takeaways

1. **Social Construction of Deviance**: Deviance is not an inherent property of an act or person but is created through social processes. An act becomes deviant only when it is labeled as such by a social group with power to enforce that label. The same behavior may be deviant in one context but normative in another.

2. **Labeling Theory Foundation**: Becker's work foundationally articulates labeling theory: deviance results from the application of rules by others to an "offender," and the deviant is one to whom the label "deviant" has been successfully applied. The behavior itself is less important than the social reaction to it.

3. **Relative Nature of "Outsider"**: The term "outsider" is inherently relative and strategic. Those labeled outsiders by the society may, from their perspective, consider the society's rules illegitimate and the society itself the "outsider." Perspective and power determine who is deviant.

4. **Rule-Making and Moral Entrepreneurs**: Becker examines how rules and moral standards are created by "moral entrepreneurs"—crusading reformers with resources and status who campaign to make their moral views into law. Historical examples show deviance categories are not discovered but created by moral entrepreneurs with agendas (e.g., marijuana prohibition in the U.S.).

5. **Process of Labeling and Identity**: Once labeled deviant, individuals may undergo identity transformation. The label can become a master status—the primary way others interact with and perceive the person. Secondary deviance may result, where labeled individuals internalize the deviant identity and commit further deviance because of how they are treated.

6. **Institutional Deviance Processing**: Becker examines how institutions (courts, prisons, psychiatric hospitals, schools) systematize and amplify labeling. Institutional processing often confirms and deepens deviant identity rather than reforming behavior.

7. **Power and Perspective**: The theory emphasizes that those with more power in society are able to make their definitions of deviance stick. Powerful groups are rarely labeled deviant even when their behavior violates others' norms. Deviance is a matter of power and perspective, not objective facts.

8. **Case Studies**: The book includes detailed case studies (jazz musicians and marijuana use, repeated offenders and institutional labeling, etc.) illustrating the mechanisms of deviance creation and labeling in practice. These cases show how normative behavior becomes redefined as deviant through social processes.

9. **Structural Similarity to Expertise Analysis**: Becker's framework is structurally similar to Chambliss's argument about excellence: just as deviance is retrospectively applied to explain rule-breaking (the label comes after observation), excellence is retrospectively applied to explain superior performance. Both are social attributions rather than inherent traits discovered in people or acts.

10. **Implications for Reform**: If deviance is socially constructed, then the solution is not to "fix" individuals but to examine rule-making processes, power differentials, and labeling mechanisms. Reform should target institutions and norms rather than solely treating individuals as pathological.

11. **Influence on Sociology**: The book launched labeling theory as a dominant paradigm in criminology and sociology of deviance, shifting research from individual criminology to social reaction perspectives. It remains widely taught and cited in sociology, law, criminology, and related fields.
