Good design is invisible. Poor designs blame the user; good designs blame themselves. Don Norman's foundational framework argues that "user error" is a design failure—when a door is confusing, when controls are hidden, when feedback is unclear, the designer failed, not the user.

## Core Problem: The Psychopathology of Everyday Things

Most products feel harder to use than they should. The culprit isn't user stupidity—it's designers' lack of understanding of how humans think and act. Design that works with human psychology succeeds; design that ignores it fails, no matter how technically sophisticated.

**The glass door problem:** Which side do you pull? A well-designed door makes the answer obvious through affordances (visual clues suggesting action) and signifiers (explicit cues like handles). A poorly designed door requires a mental model of hidden mechanism instead of relying on perception.

## Key Design Principles

**Visibility:** Make system state obvious and possible actions perceivable. Users shouldn't have to guess what mode they're in or whether their action worked. Provide immediate feedback on every action.

**Constraints:** Use physical, semantic, cultural, and syntactic constraints to guide correct behavior and prevent errors. A USB plug's unique shape prevents wrong insertion. A red button suggests "danger—stop."

**Mapping:** Ensure controls relate to their effects logically. Stove burners in a 2×2 grid should match the actual burner layout, not be arranged in a line. The spatial relationship should mirror the functional relationship.

**Consistency:** Follow conventions and standards. When people learn one pattern (light switches), don't force them to relearn it for every new context.

## The Seven Stages of Action

Users form intentions and take actions through a predictable cycle, and poor design breaks it at multiple points:

1. **Intention:** Goal formation (what do I want to accomplish?)
2. **Specification:** Plan the action sequence (what steps do I take?)
3. **Execution:** Carry out the plan
4. **Perception:** Observe system state (what happened?)
5. **Interpretation:** Make sense of feedback (does this mean it worked?)
6. **Comparison:** Evaluate outcome vs. intention (did I get what I wanted?)
7. **New Intention:** Based on results, form next goal

**Gulf of Execution:** The gap between what users want to do and what the system allows. Design narrows this through clear affordances and signifiers.

**Gulf of Evaluation:** The difficulty assessing whether an action succeeded. Design narrows this through clear feedback at each step.

## Two Minds, Two Levels of Processing

Humans operate with dual systems:

**Visceral (Emotional, Immediate):** Aesthetics, first impression, whether something feels right. A beautiful product *feels* easier to use, even before you learn how.

**Reflective (Conscious, Rational):** Deliberate thought, memory, social standing, self-image. Reflective systems decide whether to trust a product, whether to recommend it, whether it aligns with your values.

Good design appeals to both. Visceral appeal gets adoption; reflective quality sustains it. Norman's insight: *Beautiful things work better*—not because aesthetics magically improves function, but because emotional reward and trust make users more forgiving of minor usability issues and more patient with learning curves.

## Handling Error

Assume humans will make mistakes. Design doesn't prevent errors—it makes them obvious and recoverable.

**Slips:** Actions that don't match intentions (you typed the URL wrong, meant to send a different message). Prevent through constraints (confirmation dialogs, undo buttons, constraints that make wrong actions impossible).

**Mistakes:** Intentions that are themselves flawed (you thought that button would delete, but it saved). Make obvious through clear feedback and easy recovery (clearly show what will happen before irreversible action).

Design for error tolerance: implement undo broadly, ask for confirmation on destructive actions, show system state clearly so mistakes become obvious immediately.

**Critical quote:** "When things go wrong, it is not usually that people have made mistakes. It is that the purveyor of the technology has made it easy for people to err and difficult to discover the error."

## Knowledge Distributed Between Head and World

Your brain is optimized for interpretation and reasoning, not for storage and retrieval. Smart design externalizes memory by making information visible and accessible rather than requiring users to remember rules.

**Examples:**
- Airplane cockpit checklists reduce pilot workload
- Restaurant kitchen pass systems organize complex orders
- Well-labeled drawers externalize knowledge of what's stored where
- A calendar externalizes memory of commitments

Design that works with your brain's limitations succeeds. Design that ignores them fails.

## Design Thinking as Process

Design is systematic:

1. **Understand:** Research real user behavior and needs (not assumptions). Watch people actually use products; you'll find problems and opportunities you didn't anticipate.

2. **Ideate:** Generate multiple solutions without initial judgment. Quantity matters here too—explore many approaches.

3. **Prototype:** Build rough versions to test ideas quickly. Paper prototypes catch problems before expensive development.

4. **Test:** Observe real users under realistic conditions (stressed, distracted, fatigued). Iterate based on what actually happens, not what you predicted.

**The Double Diamond:** First narrow from many possibilities to a focused problem definition. Then expand from problem to multiple solutions, then converge on best. Most failed designs skip the first divergence phase—defining the real problem.

## Emotional Design Matters

Aesthetics aren't luxuries—they're legitimate design goals. A product you find beautiful is:
- More forgiving when it breaks
- More likely to be adopted and recommended
- More effective at the emotional level (makes you happier to use it)
- Perceived as easier even if functionally equivalent to an ugly alternative

The "placebo" effect is real: a beautiful product actually *performs better* in your mind because emotional reward primes you to work through minor friction and attribute problems to your skill rather than the design.
