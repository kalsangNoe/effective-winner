## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Jayne Anne Phillips, who won the Pulitzer Prize for fiction for *Night Watch*, about how she actually writes: not by planning or thinking, but by getting "inside" the material and following sentences into whatever comes next. She reads passages from *Sweethearts*, *Machine Dreams*, and *Night Watch* and unpacks the sensory, embodied process behind each — dense metaphor, hand-written drafts, research that has to be felt rather than just studied. Recurring themes: writing as time travel, children as the ideal narrative viewpoint because they see without context, and writing as a way of saving what would otherwise be lost, including a rehearsal for one's own death.

## Key ideas
- **Writing can be taught, but only up to a point.** She calls "can writing be taught" a "silly question" — talent can't be taught, but committed writers who teach literature can "encourage, inspire, mentor, and instruct" the next generation. She teaches writing by teaching literature, and describes contemporary serious writers/readers as almost a "medieval guild," since so few people now "read literature for meaning."
- **She started as a poet, and it shows in the compression of her prose.** Her early "one-page fictions" had a "spiral construction that moves out of the center like an oil well bursting," where "the last line should be perfect" and titles are transformed in meaning by the time you finish the piece.
- **Process is not conceptual — it's line-to-line following.** "I can't answer that question really because when I'm writing nothing is going through my head. I'm just focused on following it, following into the next line." She writes drafts mostly whole, then rearranges "just a few little things."
- **The goal is to put the reader inside the material, not observing it.** Quoting a reader's reaction: "When I read your work, I don't feel as though I'm reading about something. I feel as though I'm inside it." She wants the same immersive, near-unconscious identification a reader feels with "I cross the street."
- **Intense/obsessive reading as craft training.** She tells students to find their "allies" (favorite writers) and reread their work repeatedly — first for pleasure, then to study mechanics, then sometimes to chart out each chapter almost from memory. Examples: *Fat City* by Leonard Gardner (cut from 800 pages "until it was just like a diamond") and Faulkner's *The Sound and the Fury*, which she carried everywhere while writing *Lark and Termite*. Faulkner, she says, was really writing about perception: "every book teaches the reader how to read it."
- **Fiction as time travel, not historical novel-writing.** She doesn't consider herself a historical novelist — each book's time period is simply "the time or the place for this story." Research for *Night Watch* (the Civil War) had to be "embodied," "more whole body," not just intellectual: she visited the asylum, went back to West Virginia, read four volumes of Civil War-era letters, and let period speech patterns "wash over" her rather than transcribing them directly.
- **On being stuck:** she doesn't feel calm about it — "there's always a lot of fear," like "walking a tight rope without a net." Her method: reread yesterday's page; if still stuck, go back 10 pages; if still stuck, reread from the beginning — "that's how you know what to write next because you have to be inside the material."
- **"Edit with your ears."** From a 2002 teaching philosophy statement — read work aloud because "language is a music," and a wrong note "goes off... like a flat note on a piano."
- **She wrote for years around a full-time teaching job**, calling herself "just doggedly persistent" — she's "never thrown away a book" and only one story. Retired from teaching in January 2020, just before the pandemic; the isolation of that period became a strange refuge that let her finish *Night Watch*, mirroring the asylum-as-refuge theme inside the novel itself.
- **Children as narrators: "the ultimate outlaws."** They "don't see anything in context — it's all new," a kind of beginner's/Zen mind that lets fiction do something no other viewpoint can. Her key early influence here is James Agee's *A Death in the Family*.
- **Fiction isn't really "about" real people.** "The minute you start to write about someone, it's not that someone" — fictionalizing invents a new reality, even when inspired by real people (she named her own parents as inspiration for her first novel, *Machine Dreams*). She also warns against writing for an imagined audience: thinking about the audience "cuts you off from what's really happening."
- **Writing as practice for death.** "Writing is practicing for death because you're... trying to write about transformation" — the shift from thinking/wanting to actually writing is itself a transformation, and she wants to be conscious the way we aren't at birth.
- **Every person is a "historical genius."** Without being written down, entire lives become an anonymous "huge field of moving tall grass." Writing is a "statement of faith" — not religious, but faith "that life is meaningful."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I feel as though when I'm inside the work, following the sentences themselves deeper into the material... there's not myself thinking about the material — I want to be inside the material."
- "Each book teaches the reader how to read it. You get inside the book and you begin to see, feel where you are in space. Sort of like you sit down in a chair, the chair starts revolving, but you keep your seat because you're so intensely in place."
- "It's like walking a tight rope without a net... another metaphor is the writing is like you put on this asbestos suit and you walk straight into the flames, and the asbestos suit is writing itself."
- "The minute you start to write about someone, it's not that someone... you're inventing a new reality."
- "Writing is practicing for death because you're trying to write about transformation — there is a transformation involved from the thinking and the wanting to actually writing."
- "Children are the ultimate outlaws. They don't see anything in context. It's all new."
- "Each person is a kind of historical genius in that each person is completely separate in their experience, their traumas, their healing if it happens... it means so much."
- "Unless you write it down, it will be lost."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I reread my own drafts obsessively enough to "edit with my ears," or do I mostly edit silently and miss the flat notes?
- What's my equivalent of Phillips's "go back 10 pages, then go back to the beginning" method for when I'm stuck — do I have a repeatable ritual at all, or do I just wait?
- Am I writing toward an imagined audience's expectations instead of staying "inside the material" — where would that be showing up in my own work right now?
- Which of my own early, formative reading experiences (like her discovering *Rabbit, Run* at nine or ten) actually shaped how I write, and have I ever traced that influence explicitly?
- Is there a "world that's gone" in my own life that I'm at risk of losing simply because I haven't written it down yet?
