## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Suleika Jaouad, author of *Between Two Kingdoms* and *The Book of Alchemy*, about the writing practice she built after being diagnosed with leukemia at 22. Her core argument: creative work requires trading thick skin for "porousness," and the first draft has to be written for no audience at all — by hand, in a journal — before the "judge" (the editor) is allowed anywhere near it. Around that thesis she offers a toolkit of concrete, repeatable techniques: rituals for protecting unstructured time, the "bed, bath, bus" framework for getting unstuck, feedback formats that ask "where's the heat" instead of "what's wrong," and daily journaling exercises like "Just 10 Images" that train attention. The throughline is that the subject you're avoiding, and the question you're already asking your friends privately, is usually the book.

## Key ideas

- **Porousness beats thick skin.** Jaouad grew up in a "pull-yourself-up-by-the-bootstraps" immigrant family that valued thick skin; her cancer diagnosis at 22 forced the opposite — "so exposed, so vulnerable, so porous to everything around me" — and she found that fragility, not armor, was what her creative process needed.
- **Write what you don't want to know about yourself.** Her stated thesis: writing what you don't want others to know makes a good book; writing what you don't want to know about yourself makes a great one. First drafts of her own book were "full of lies" — aspirational surface stories — until she excavated beneath the outline she'd written in her book proposal.
- **The journal as a private reporter's notebook.** Writing from the hospital, with no audience or expectation, she used her journal to write toward things she couldn't say out loud — a habit she kept: she still writes every first draft longhand, in a notebook, before touching a computer.
- **Handwriting keeps the "judge" out of the room.** Typing invites the internal editor to operate at the same time as the creator, which shuts down access to what's "in the sediment of your mind." She cites her friend, poet Marie Howe, who writes with her non-dominant hand in a big scroll when blocked, starting with "I don't want to write about."
- **Protect the space with rituals, not willpower.** While writing *Between Two Kingdoms* she house- and dog-sat for friends to get remote, self-styled writing residencies; on writing days she'd walk the dog, leave her phone in the mailbox, write for a couple of hours, then retrieve the phone. She now uses a physical device called "the brick" that she taps to start a screen time-out.
- **Memoir can retraumatize you while you write it.** She spent six months unlearning her book proposal's outline, then found herself collapsing on the floor most afternoons around 3–4pm — a trauma response to re-rendering the experience, not just difficulty writing it. The fix wasn't more discipline but distance: after a first reader told her 150 pages simply "weren't working," she drove to a beach, had a flash of insight, and rewrote part of the book from past into present tense on her laptop in the car — which unlocked it.
- **Bed, bath, or bus — and creative cross-training.** When stuck: sleep on it, relax completely, or change your physical environment. The harder discipline is being honest about whether you're truly out of fuel or just avoiding the work — a friend's test is whether you're "on record" as someone who finishes projects, which earns you the benefit of the doubt to step away.
- **Prompts as an escape from recursive loops.** Feeling stuck and "bored of the sound of my own voice," she started reading other writers' journals at random (Susan Sontag, Virginia Woolf, Audre Lorde) as a kaleidoscope to shift her own thinking. That practice grew into her newsletter *The Isolation Journals* — 40,000 subscribers in the first 24 hours during the pandemic, now nearly 300,000 — which pairs a guest essay with a daily prompt, including ekphrastic prompts (writing from a painting) and Brian Eno–style constraints (writing for the length of one ambient song).
- **Feedback should locate the heat, not just the flaws.** She now avoids asking "is it as bad as I think" and instead asks readers to mark where the energy is — echoing Melissa Febos's "heat mapping" and George Saunders's mental yes/no meter for each line. Perell's parallel technique is trunk/branch/leaf: ask for structural ("trunk") feedback early, and line-level ("leaf") feedback only once a piece is developed.
- **Journals are source material and memory itself.** She tries to journal daily, even for two minutes, because "committing something to ink" helps commit it to memory. A favorite exercise, "Just 10 Images" (from writer Ash Parsons Story, written while in the NICU with her son), forces you to log ten concrete images from the last 24 hours — pushing past the first few obvious ones into what you'd otherwise never notice or remember.
- **Paying attention is the actual craft.** She points to Annie Dillard's *Pilgrim at Tinker Creek* and David Foster Wallace's page-long description of a sink on a cruise ship as writing that hands the reader a new vocabulary for noticing ordinary things — quoting Mary Oliver's line that "to pay attention, this is our constant work."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I actually writing porous first drafts with no audience in mind, or is some imagined reader already sitting in the room the moment I open a document?
- What's the question I'm already asking my friends privately that I haven't recognized as "the subject" — the thing that's right on my forehead, per Jaouad's image?
- When I ask for feedback, am I asking "is this bad?" or "where's the heat?" — and how much has the former discouraged me from work that was actually working in places?
- What would my own "bed, bath, bus" list look like, and can I honestly tell the difference between legitimate depletion and procrastination dressed up as cross-training?
- Would a daily "Just 10 Images" habit change what I notice day to day, the way it sharpened Jaouad's attention — and am I willing to sit with "I've got nothing" until image eight or nine surfaces something real?
