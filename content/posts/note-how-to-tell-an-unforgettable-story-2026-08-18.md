## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Shaan Puri about how he reverse-engineered storytelling into a repeatable craft rather than an innate gift. Shaan's central claim is that a story is not a record of events ("this happened, then this happened") but a compressed account of *change*: someone was one way, then something happened, then they were another way — and the audience needs to feel the stakes of that change. He walks through frameworks borrowed from Aaron Sorkin (intention and obstacle), Matthew Dicks (stakes and the five-second moment of change), and his own practice (writing like you talk, working backwards from the emotion you want to produce, editing by "removing the suck"). The throughline is that storytelling, humor, and framing are learnable, practiced skills — most people just never practice them, which is exactly why they're a competitive advantage.

## Key ideas

- **Intention and obstacle (Aaron Sorkin).** Every character in a story needs to want something clearly (intention) and something needs to stand in the way (obstacle). Shaan's test: if you read any story or watch any movie, you should be able to point at the screen at any moment and say what the character wants and what's stopping them. If you go five minutes without that being clear, the story stops working.
- **The story must be real, not fabricated.** The audience has to believe you truly wanted the thing — Shaan's example is his own "Brussels sprouts" story about cooking for his mom after moving out, told at a corporate offsite, which worked because the desire (to seem like a capable adult) was genuine and low-stakes/relatable rather than dramatic.
- **Low status is a storytelling tool.** Learned from a comedian friend before going on stage: don't puff your chest out with false bravado — connection with an audience is built through vulnerability and low status, not impressiveness. A story where you crushed it and impressed everyone is boring; a story where you failed and everyone can relate is engaging.
- **Write like you talk.** One of Shaan's core writing rules — school teaches formal, padded prose (minimum page counts, five-paragraph essays) that doesn't work in the real world, where good writing just needs to be easy and entertaining to read. His practical trick for the "blank page of death": literally say the story out loud first, roughly hit the outline, then write it down.
- **Hooks vs. frames.** A hook grabs attention in the first line (essential for cold traffic, e.g. TikTok where you have ~0.9 seconds before someone swipes away). A frame is more powerful and underrated — it's how you position an idea so unrelated things feel related, and it works even when the underlying content is unchanged. His example: reframing a Clubhouse thread from "here's why I'm right" (logic/analysis) into a screenplay-style narrative ("you're the founder, everybody's throwing money at you...") — same facts, story format, and it went viral where a drier version by a smarter writer failed.
- **Storyworthy (Matthew Dicks) — two big ideas Shaan kept:**
  - *Stakes*: the story needs something at risk for the person — not necessarily extreme, but real to them (embarrassment, ego, social standing).
  - *The five-second moment of change*: every story/movie boils down to one moment where the character transforms — Batman's jump from the pit, Airbnb's Obama O's cereal pivot, Scrooge's turn. Find that specific moment rather than narrating the whole arc.
- **Signature stories / origin stories.** Companies and people need 4-5 "signature stories" that explain who they are better than a resume ever could — built the same way: act one (the low point / mismatch — "my job doesn't like me"), the moment of change (often small and specific, not a grand epiphany), act three (the payoff). He walks Perell through building his own Write of Passage origin story live in the interview.
- **Work backwards from the desired emotional reaction.** Learned from an ad-agency friend who found 8/100 of his videos went viral: instead of writing content and hoping, define the target reaction first (LOL, WTF, OMG) and edit toward it — "does this make somebody go 'oh my god'? No? Let's juice it up."
- **Write to one person, not "the internet."** Even though thousands will read a piece, the reader experiences it alone — at their desk, waiting in line, half-distracted. Shaan cites Gary Halbert's prison letters (written as if to his son) as the model for the intimate, casual voice he tries to replicate in his own writing.
- **Change your state before you create (Miss Excel / the "peak state" idea).** Get your physiology and energy into a "magnetic" state before writing or recording, because "content is just energy transferred to somebody else" — most creators write in a flat, exhausted, sober state and it shows. Concrete tactics: physical exertion (sprints, cold water) to change physiology; narrowing focus to just the task; and choosing a better internal story about why you're doing this.
- **Humor is sauce, not the meal.** Humor's job is to make ideas easier to swallow and more memorable — the mechanism is setup + subverted expectation. He breaks down Theo Von's storytelling style (funny specific words like "pooper scooper," absurd asides) and a pastor's "jungle tiger vs. zoo tiger" sermon as an example of wrapping a dry, memorable idea (comfort kills growth) in vivid, funny language.
- **Editing: "remove the suck."** Shaan writes a fast first draft with no editing (bad idea to edit immediately — kills the flow of thinking), walks away for hours/days, then comes back and asks specifically what isn't working yet and cuts/rewrites toward that, borrowing Pixar's phrase for their own draft-revision process.
- **Practice like reps, not talent.** His advice to aspiring storytellers, echoing a MrBeast-ism: tell 100 stories, expect the first ones to be cringe, and by the 100th you'll have figured it out — nobody actually does this, which is why it works as advice for those who do.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "Intention and obstacle is just, at any given moment, someone you're talking about has to have [something they want], and under that is like why do they want it... If you read any story or watch any movie, you should be able to point at the screen and just say, what do they want and what's stopping them from getting it." — Shaan Puri
- "The audience has to believe that you truly needed [the thing]... you don't have to fabricate the story, it should be real, but you have to make them believe that you really wanted it." — Shaan Puri
- "A story is not a record of what happened. It is not, 'this happened, this happened, this happened.' ... I was one way and then it was another way. All a story is, is just... it explains how that change came to be." — Shaan Puri
- "Content is just energy transferred to you. My emotion, how I feel about this... the way to do that is for me to be there first." — Shaan Puri
- "What's the suck I can remove from this? ... I think Pixar uses that analogy too. They say [their] job is to just remove the suck with every draft." — Shaan Puri
- "So humor is surprise, meaning I have to set it up... and the contrast of what you [expected versus what] said is what makes you laugh. You just kind of build up an expectation and then you subvert the expectation." — Shaan Puri
- "Tell 100 stories starting with maximum cringe... expect that it's going to take 100 stabs at this... nobody does it. And of the few people who [do] the work, by the 100th one, they figured it out. That's why it's the perfect advice." — Shaan Puri

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- In my own writing, can I name the "intention and obstacle" at every point, or do sections drift into exposition with no one wanting anything?
- What's my equivalent of the "Brussels sprouts story" — a small, real, low-stakes moment I've been skipping past because it doesn't feel dramatic enough to be "a story"?
- Do I write toward a specific target emotional reaction (LOL/WTF/OMG-equivalent), or do I just write what's true and hope it lands?
- What would it look like to "reframe" instead of just argue harder — is there a piece I've been trying to win on logic that would work better as a story?
- Am I editing my first drafts immediately (which Shaan says kills the thinking), or am I actually walking away and coming back to "remove the suck"?
