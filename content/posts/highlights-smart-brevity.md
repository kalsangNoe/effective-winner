## TL;DR
Modern readers scan, not read — 26 seconds average per piece, most attention lost after a few dozen words — so the Axios founders built a repeatable four-part structure (strong tease, one-sentence lede, "Why it matters" context, optional "Go deeper") that forces you to figure out the ONE thing you're trying to say and say it first. The book is equal parts origin story (Politico → Axios, the data that humbled them) and a practical cheat sheet for applying the structure to emails, meetings, speeches, decks, social posts, visuals and company-wide communication.

## Key takeaways
1. **Audience first, always.** Picture one specific person and ask what *they* need, not what you want to say — this single mental shift eliminates most bloat before you write a word.
2. **The Core 4 structure is the whole system**: a ≤6-word tease → one sharp lede sentence → a bolded "Why it matters" (the Axiom) → an optional "Go deeper" link. It fits on one phone screen.
3. **Short is not shallow.** Brevity is a discipline of selection and clarity, not dumbing-down — cutting words is how you find and protect the signal, not how you lose nuance.
4. **Word choice is mechanical and learnable**: shorter/one-syllable words beat longer ones, active verbs beat passive, concrete/"strong" words (things you can picture) beat abstract/"weak" ones, and foggy hedges ("could," "may," "might") should be replaced with what's actually happening.
5. **Bullets, bold, and Axioms ("Why it matters," "The big picture," "By the numbers") aren't decoration — they're skimming infrastructure** that lets a reader who gives you 3 seconds still walk away with the point.
6. **The "think, then type" trick**: say your point out loud to a real person (or record yourself) before writing — the spoken version is almost always sharper than what you'd draft at a keyboard.
7. **Brevity scales into culture.** Applied consistently — weekly newsletters, Smart Brevity emails, capped meetings — it becomes an organizational operating system (Axios runs itself on it internally via "5 Big Things").

## Who this is for
Anyone who writes for other people under time pressure — managers, founders, teachers, salespeople, students — not just journalists; the book explicitly says it wasn't written for media people.

## Verdict
Quick, practical read (~106 minutes per its own count) that mostly *is* what it teaches — skimmable, example-driven, with a genuinely reusable structure (Core 4) and a literal fill-in-the-blank cheat sheet (Ch. 23) worth returning to before writing anything important. Best used as a reference you dip back into, not a cover-to-cover deep study.

## Chapter notes

### Introduction: The Fog of Words

**TL;DR**: We're drowning in words at unprecedented velocity (26 seconds average reading time, phone checks 344+/day), and the fix isn't better willpower but a new default: adapt to how people actually consume information — in short scannable bursts — rather than clinging to how they "should" read.

**Key concepts**:
- **Continuous partial attention** — readers are always thinking about the next alert, not fully present with your text, even when they care about the topic.
- **17-millisecond click decision** — the brain decides almost instantly whether it likes what it just opened.
- **Two root causes of the fog** — (1) tech gave everyone free, instant, unlimited publishing power; (2) writing habits didn't adapt — people still write "like it's 1980."
- **Length-equals-depth myth** — school and workplace culture trained us that more words signal more intelligence/effort, which is backwards for a distracted reader.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Never in the history of humanity have we vomited more words in more places with more velocity." (Introduction)
> "It takes most people more than 20 minutes to snap back into focus after a distraction." (Introduction)
> Stewart Butterfield (Slack CEO): in a hypothetical 10,000-employee, $1B-payroll company, "50 to 60 percent of the average employee's time is spent on communication of some sort." (Introduction)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Stop assuming more words = more thoroughness; assume the opposite by default.
- Before writing anything, ask what's "new" and "why it matters" — that's usually all the reader wants.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- None yet — this chapter is diagnosis, not method.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- The 344-phone-checks and 26-second stats are presented without full methodology; useful as color, not rigorous data to cite elsewhere.

### Ch2: Smart Brevity, Explained

**TL;DR**: This chapter delivers the core mechanism of the whole book — the "Core 4" structure (tease, lede, "Why it matters," "Go deeper") — illustrated with a real CIA intelligence-briefing rewrite and paired before/after examples (a kid's birthday party email, a board update) showing the same information compressed without losing meaning.

**Key concepts**:
- **The Core 4** — (1) a muscular tease of 6 words or fewer, (2) one strong first sentence/lede, (3) context via a bolded "Why it matters" Axiom, (4) an optional "Go deeper" for those who want more — all ideally fitting on one phone screen.
- **Smart Jazz** — the Core 4 is a guardrail, not a rigid rule; you can add a sentence of intrigue before the "Why it matters" punchline if it serves the audience.
- **The CIA case study** — real-world proof that Smart Brevity applies even to classified intelligence writing (rewritten Afghanistan/Taliban warning), attributed to former PDB author Philip Dufresne.
- **"Ask the author, don't ask them to write a report"** — people can always tell you verbally what's most interesting in their own data; only in writing do they bury or omit it.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "His tip, which works every time: Ask the author of the data what's the most interesting thing about it. They know what it is—and they'll tell you. But if you ask them to write a report about it, they'll either bury it or, more likely, leave it out." (Ch. 2, re: Mike's CIA talk)
> "The gold standard is always what's best for the audience—the structure that is clearest and most efficient for a reader whose attention is being pulled in a million ways." (Ch. 2, Smart Jazz)
> "Smart Brevity is music theory—it gives you logic and elegance. But that magnificent architecture leaves room for... jazz." (Ch. 2)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Draft any update using the Core 4 skeleton explicitly: Tease → Lede → Why it matters → Go deeper.
- When stuck on what matters most in something you wrote, say it out loud to someone else first — the verbal version reveals the real point.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Core 4**: Tease (≤6 words) → Lede (1 sentence, most memorable fact) → Why it matters (context, usually 1–2 sentences) → Go deeper (optional link/detail) — the load-bearing framework of the entire book.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged — this chapter is presented as settled doctrine, not argued.

### Ch3: The Road to Smart Brevity

**TL;DR**: The authors' own journalism careers (Politico, then Axios) converted them to brevity only after data forced their hand — 80% of readers of their "toast of Washington" 1,600-word Obama columns never made it past page one, and even $100k/year Politico Pro subscribers said, when surveyed, that they wanted *fewer* words, not more.

**Key concepts**:
- **Nicholas Johnston / Bloomberg First Word** — the wire-service brevity model ("Reid Won't Seek Reelection" as the ideal opening four words) that pre-dated and influenced Axios's style.
- **The Politico page-break data** — the moment analytics replaced ego: readers consumed at most ~490 words of columns "that were the toast of the town."
- **Politico Pro survey result** — only ~5% of paying, expert, high-need subscribers said they valued longer stories most — the "holy-shit" moment that led to founding Axios.
- **Founding insight** — build media (and by extension any communication) around what the audience actually wants, not what journalists/writers/ad-sellers want.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "I don't care what the fifth word of the story is, as long as the first four are 'Reid Won't Seek Reelection.'" (Nicholas Johnston, Bloomberg First Word, Ch. 3)
> "Blobs of text make the eye sad." (Nick Johnston, wall slogan, Ch. 3)
> "This was our holy-shit, life-will-never-be-the-same moment. Even the most discerning, high-need readers in the world were screaming for fewer words." (Ch. 3)

**Action items / Implications**:
- If you have any usage/read data on your own communications (email opens, page scrolls), look at it — it will humble you the same way it humbled the authors.
- Assume readers, even expert/high-stakes ones, want less, not more, unless you have specific evidence otherwise.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- None new — this chapter is origin story/evidence for Ch. 2's framework.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- Survey stat ("5 percent... valued longer stories") is self-reported and from the authors' own company — useful anecdotally, not a rigorously sourced external stat.

### Ch4: Audience First

**TL;DR**: "Audience first" — the first two words of the Axios manifesto — means picking one specific real person to write for (not a vague demographic) and figuring out the one thing you want *them* to remember; the chapter includes concrete before/after rewrites (apology, feedback, workplace update) showing how self-focused writing balloons and audience-focused writing collapses to a sentence.

**Key concepts**:
- **Single-person targeting** — even for group communications, picture one specific name/face/job, not "everyone," because "if you try to speak to everyone, usually you reach no one."
- **"Cowards hide in clauses"** — hedging language in apologies/feedback is a way of avoiding directness, not being polite.
- **The friend-test** — read your draft to a friend, then ask them to state the one big idea back to you; if they can't, you haven't said it plainly enough.
- **"Then stop"** — the recurring instruction that once you've said the point, adding more just dilutes it.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "All you can do is the next right thing." (Pastor David Glade, quoted Ch. 4, on distilling complex ideas to one line)
> "Vigorous writing is concise. A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason a drawing should have no unnecessary lines and a machine no unnecessary parts." (William Strunk, Elements of Style, quoted by Glade, Ch. 4)
> "We hide the good stuff in piles of words. We make people deduce what we're trying to say instead of just blurting it out. Don't be fancy—be effective." (Ch. 4)
> "People want direct, clear, honest communication. If you try to spin or bullshit me, I'm out." (Lisa Ross, CEO of Edelman, Ch. 4)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Before writing, name the one specific person you're writing to (job, name if possible).
- Rewrite your next apology or piece of feedback by deleting every clause that isn't the direct point (see "Better: 'I'm sincerely sorry I said that.'").
- Run the friend-test on your next important email before sending.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Audience-first check**: (1) Focus on ONE person you're targeting. (2) Plot out ONE thing you want them to remember. (3) Write like a human, for humans. (4) Write that one thing down first. (5) Then stop. (Tips & Tricks, Ch. 4)

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch5: Be Worthy

**TL;DR**: Grounded in Prof. Ronald Yaros's eye-tracking research (26 seconds average "time on text," attention lost to "kick-outs" like too much text/jargon/choices/long video), this chapter argues you earn attention by ruthlessly pre-selecting only the essential points — demonstrated with a real estate agent's "no fluff" client-communication style and a messy-vs-clean soccer-logistics email.

**Key concepts**:
- **Time on text** — Yaros's term for the ~26-second average a reader spends on a piece before disengaging; anything written after that point is usually wasted.
- **Digital engagement model / kick-outs** — Yaros's four top attention-killers: too much text, too much jargon, too many choices, long video. Common thread: less is more, across formats (writing, video, games).
- **Continuous partial attention** (Linda Stone, referenced again) — readers are never fully "in" your content even when interested.
- **No-fluff communication in practice** — Megan Green (realtor) skips pleasantries, uses bullet points and color-coding, because in high-emotion transactional contexts (buyers/sellers), busywork literally costs money.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "This is NOT multitasking but a user constantly thinking about the next alert, text or email." (Yaros study, quoted Ch. 5)
> "Stick to the facts. Be polite... I don't waste time by saying: 'Hi, hope you're having a good day.' It's bullet points... No fluff." (Megan Green, Ch. 5)
> "Write your business email or letter. Then, when you've written the whole thing, go back and make the first two to three sentences say all of what you wrote below. It's often the only part that gets read." (Chris Sacca, Ch. 5)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Assume you have ~26 seconds; write as if the reader will stop there.
- Apply Sacca's trick: after drafting, rewrite the opening 2–3 sentences to contain the whole message, in case that's all that gets read.
- Audit your own writing for the four kick-outs: excess text, jargon, too many choices/options presented, long attachments/video.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **List → whittle → gut-check → delete** (Tips & Tricks, Ch. 5): (1) List every point you must make, ranked by importance. (2) Whittle to one or two if possible; else bullet them. (3) Gut-check: is this point essential, and is there a simpler way to say it? (4) Delete, delete, delete before sending.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged; Yaros's research is cited as an early preview/informal share rather than a published, checkable study.

### Ch6: Grab Me!

**TL;DR**: Headlines, subject lines, and opening tweets are the single highest-leverage words you write — the Axios team found ~6 words is the optimal email subject line length — and this chapter gives concrete do/don't headline rewrites plus the self-test: "Would you read it if you hadn't written it?"

**Key concepts**:
- **The 6-word optimal subject line** — short enough to display fully on a mobile screen; found by the Axios audience team.
- **Fight-or-flight framing** — the brain makes a fast binary decision (click/scroll, read/ignore) on a tease, so every word in it is "a battle for additional time and attention."
- **Hot-name effect** — putting a recognizable name/brand in a headline (e.g., a motivational sales coach's name in a recruiting newsletter) measurably lifts open rates (Eddie Berenbaum / Century 21 case).
- **Syllable rule** — one-syllable words are stronger than two-syllable, which are stronger than three-syllable, as a rule of thumb for headline word choice.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Just tell me something I don't f@$&ing know." (John Bresnahan, Punchbowl News, on what a first sentence should do — Ch. 6/7 boundary)
> "Write—then go back and kill at least half the words. It winds up sharper every time." (Berenbaum's 11th-grade English teacher, Ch. 6)
> "You would never cook a gourmet meal and serve it in a dog bowl." (Ch. 6, on pairing good ideas with weak headlines)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Cap your own subject lines/headlines at 6 words as a hard constraint, then test if it still works.
- Test every headline against: "Would I click this if I hadn't written it?"
- Try naming a recognizable person/brand in your next internal newsletter subject line if genuinely relevant.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Start by stopping** (Tips & Tricks, Ch. 6): Stop using too many words (cap 6). Stop being funny/ironic/cryptic. Stop using SAT/business-speak words. Then: state the reason for writing in ≤10 words, as provocatively-yet-accurately as possible, favor short/strong/active words, and read it aloud to confirm it entices.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- The "6 words" figure is internal Axios audience-team data, not independently replicated — treat as a strong heuristic, not a universal law.

### Ch7: ONE Big Thing

**TL;DR**: The most important sentence you write is your first one (the "lede") — journalists themselves are often bad at this — and the reliable trick is to say out loud what happened to a real person (editor, spouse, roommate) before you ever try to write it down, because your spoken instinct for what's newsworthy beats your typed instinct almost every time.

**Key concepts**:
- **The lede test** — imagine you're in an elevator with the reader and they're about to walk out the door: what's the one thing you'd shout after them? That's your opening sentence.
- **Verbal-first drafting** — Cliff Sims's book (Team of Vipers) only worked once he told the stories out loud (recorded on iPhone, transcribed) instead of trying to write them cold.
- **"No one gives a shit if no one sees or reads it"** — Jim's blunt framing for why the lede matters more than any other sentence: without it, nothing else you wrote gets consumed.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "No one gives a shit if no one sees or reads it." (Jim, quoted by Mike, Ch. 7)
> "Just tell me something I don't f@$&ing know." (John Bresnahan, Ch. 7)
> "I know my value and want to discuss a raise." (example rewrite of a rambling raise-ask, Ch. 7)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Before writing any important note, call/tell someone what happened out loud first — that's your draft first sentence.
- Apply the elevator test to your next update: what's the one thing you'd shout as the reader walks away?

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Lede checklist** (Tips & Tricks, Ch. 7): (1) Boil down the most important point, audience in mind. (2) Skip anecdotes/jokes/showing off. (3) One-sentence limit. (4) Don't repeat the tease verbatim. (5) Cut adverbs/weak/extraneous words. (6) Ask: if this is the ONLY thing they see, is it exactly what should stick? Then move on.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch8: Why It Matters

**TL;DR**: The bolded "Why it matters" (and its siblings — "The big picture," "By the numbers," "The bottom line," etc.) — collectively called "Axioms" — are signposts that answer the reader's unstated second question after the lede ("OK, but why should I care?"), and Axios essentially named the whole company after this concept (Axios = Greek for "worthy").

**Key concepts**:
- **Axiom** — the bolded framing phrase (borrowed and sharpened from journalism's "nut graf") that cues the reader's brain to process context, not just facts.
- **Menu of Axioms** — "The big picture," "What's next," "What we're watching," "What we're hearing," "Between the lines," "The backstory," "Catch up quick," "Zoom in," "Zoom out" — interchangeable signposts depending on the kind of context needed.
- **Weak-framing → strong-Axiom mapping** — e.g., "Here's what's important to know" → "Why it matters"; "In conclusion" → "The bottom line."
- **The nut graf origin** — traditional newspapers buried this context "in the fourth paragraph or lower"; Smart Brevity moves it to the very top, right after the lede.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Axios is Greek for 'worthy,' as in worthy of your time, trust and attention." (Ch. 8)
> "If this is all a person hears and learns, does it convey what matters most to you in the most blunt and understandable way possible? If the answer is yes, you have done more in 200 words than most people do with 20,000." (Ch. 8)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Add a bolded "Why it matters:" line immediately after your lede in your next work update or email — even if nothing else changes, this one habit is described as the single most effective Axiom.
- Read your headline + lede + Axiom together, out loud, as a 3-part gut check before sending anything important.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Axiom rule**: after "Why it matters," explain in 1–2 sentences max what it will change, what it signals, or what larger context it fits — must add perspective, never repeat the lede.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch9: Go Deeper

**TL;DR**: The optional fourth Core 4 element — a closing "Go deeper" link/pointer — lets you preserve depth and nuance without forcing every reader through it; most people won't click, but its mere presence signals thoroughness and respect for the reader's choice.

**Key concepts**:
- **"Go deeper" as an exit ramp** — a way to end an item that offers more (source article, academic paper, podcast, map, excerpts) without burying the core point in it.
- **Bullets as the Golden Rule** — if you're explaining 3+ related data points or ideas, always split them into bullets rather than a paragraph clump.
- **Bold as an eye-trap** — bolding is preferred over italics because it's more visually distinct for skimmers.
- **Mix it up** — cap paragraphs at 2–3 sentences; alternate text with bullets/bolding/Axioms/charts to avoid "big blobs of bloviation."

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "It says: I've done the work so you don't have to." (Ch. 9, on the value of "Go deeper" links)
> "The Golden Rule of Bullets: Nobody wants to stare at a clump of words and figures." (Ch. 9)
> "The most useful communication is often silence, grasshopper." (Ch. 9, closing line)

**Action items / Implications**:
- End your next long-form update with an explicit "Go deeper" line/link rather than trying to cram everything into the main text.
- Convert any paragraph listing 3+ related facts into a bulleted list.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- None new beyond completing the Core 4 (this is the "4" in Core 4).

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch10: The Right Words

**TL;DR**: A tactical toolkit for word-level editing: prefer short/concrete/"strong" words over long/abstract/"weak" ones, purge fancy "10-dollar words" and academic "journalese," replace foggy hedges ("could/may/might") with what's actually happening, and default to active verbs — illustrated by a middle-school teacher's success bolding key words for skimming parents.

**Key concepts**:
- **Strong vs. weak words** — strong words are concrete/picturable (fire, boat, cage, chop, crush); weak words are abstract and unseeable (process, civics).
- **The bar test** — if you wouldn't say a word at a bar or the beach, cut it (targets "fancy"/"spelling-bee" words and academic "journalese" like *discourse, posit, dearth, ubiquitous, disseminate*).
- **Foggy words** — "could," "may," "might" convey nothing; replace with the actual status (planned, considered, feared, expected).
- **Active > passive** — "The Taliban captured Afghanistan" vs. "The situation in Afghanistan continues to deteriorate" — active names the actor and the action.
- **Syllable-count rule (reiterated)** — check every word in an opening sentence for a shorter, punchier synonym (e.g., "revenge" over "retribution").

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "The difference between the almost right word and the right word... 'tis the difference between the lightning bug and the lightning." (Mark Twain, quoted Ch. 10)
> "They just want to write as little as possible." (Mark Smith, middle-school teacher, on why his students already embrace Smart Brevity, Ch. 10)
> "Jesus wept." — cited as the shortest, most powerful line in the Bible: two words, nine letters. (Ch. 10)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Build a personal "kill list" of your own overused fancy/journalese words and their plain replacements.
- Scan drafts specifically for "could/may/might" and replace each with a concrete claim.
- After writing an opening sentence, go word by word and ask if a shorter synonym exists — always take it.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Strong-word checklist** (Tips & Tricks, Ch. 10): (1) Shorter is always better (1-syllable > 2 > 3). (2) Deploy strong (concrete/picturable) words. (3) Purge weak/fancy/journalese words. (4) Avoid foggy hedge words. (5) Use active verbs — "who, doing, what." (6) Embrace strong short phrases ("Sales cratered," "I quit"). (7) Check every word for a shorter option.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch11: Emojis

**TL;DR**: Used sparingly and consistently, emojis function as visual shorthand that instantly signals topic/tone (e.g., 📊 = data, 🎙️ = podcast) and measurably lifts email open rates when placed in subject lines — but overuse reads as try-hard, so restraint is the actual skill.

**Key concepts**:
- **Emoji as topic-branding** — using the same emoji consistently for a recurring feature (e.g., a "Data of the day" item) trains readers to recognize the content type at a glance.
- **Subject-line emoji effect** — an emoji in a subject line makes it visually pop against a wall of plain-text inbox items (cited: *Morning Brew*'s consistent morning emoji branding).
- **Restraint as the rule** — the risk isn't using emojis, it's using them without discipline ("your old man in designer skinny jeans").

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Brevity nirvana is saying something with NO words." (Ch. 11, opening line on emojis)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Pick one or two emojis to consistently "brand" a recurring section of your own newsletter/update, rather than sprinkling emojis randomly.
- Test adding a single relevant emoji to a subject line and compare open behavior.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- None formal — this is a tactical aside, not a structural framework.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- Authors admit: "We know of no science here" regarding emoji impact on open rates — explicitly anecdotal, not data-backed.

### Ch12: Mike's Playbook

**TL;DR**: Mike Allen's 15-year, 2,500+ consecutive daily newsletter habit is the human proof-of-concept for Smart Brevity — his unstructured Politico Playbook email (which made him "The Man the White House Wakes Up To") got restructured at Axios into a disciplined 10-numbered-item format, which cut his word count in half without cutting his influence.

**Key concepts**:
- **"Literary handcuffs"** — Jim's framing for imposing a hard numbered-item structure (originally 10 items) on Mike's previously freeform, marathon newsletter.
- **You are the chef** — the writer's real value is in *selection* (deciding what makes the cut), not exhaustive inclusion; readers can't do their own curation for you.
- **The Big 6 experiment** — one slow-news Easter weekend, Mike cut his usual "Top 10" to "Big 6"; readers then asked to permanently subscribe to the shorter version — direct evidence that less can outperform more even with a loyal, engaged audience.
- **Gamified editing** — Mike's editing trick: silently cut hundreds of words from someone's draft, then challenge them to identify what's missing (usually they can't).

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "You don't have to do anything else, just read Mike Allen." (Bob Woodward, on Morning Joe, quoted Ch. 12)
> "It's Easter—no one's going to complain if their newsletter isn't long enough." (Mike, on trying Big 6, Ch. 12)
> "Where can I sign up for the Big 6 version?" (reader response after Mike reverted to Top 10, Ch. 12)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Try deliberately halving your usual update length for one cycle and see if anyone notices what's missing.
- If you write a recurring update, consider capping it at a fixed number of numbered items rather than letting it grow organically.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- None new beyond reinforcing "selection is the job" (ties back to Ch. 5's "Be Worthy" and Ch. 9's bullet discipline).

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- None flagged.

### Ch13: The Art of the Newsletter

**TL;DR**: A practical build-guide for launching your own Smart Brevity newsletter — name it, state the time cost up front, lead with "1 big thing," cap it at 5–10 items and under 1,000–1,200 words total, and end on something human — illustrated by Austin, TX mayor's office adopting the format for public/media communication.

**Key concepts**:
- **Smart Brevity count** — stating explicit word count and read time (Axios uses 265 wpm average) at the top of a piece, so the reader knows the time cost before committing.
- **"1 big thing"** — explicitly labeling your lead item as the single most important thing, both as a headline convention and a mental discipline for the writer.
- **Hard length caps** — 5–10 items ideal (more "is a book, not a newsletter"); under 1,000 words total ideal, 1,200 as the outer limit; 200 words max per item.
- **End on something human** — close with "1 fun thing" / "1 smile to go" — personal, funny, non-work content that builds reader affinity.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "A world that exclusively uses bullet points" — Elizabeth Lewis (Austin mayor's comms director) describing her communications dream. (Ch. 13)
> "Reporters want the shortest possible pitch. That's how they consume information. That's how I consume information." (Elizabeth Lewis, Ch. 13)

**Action items / Implications**:
- If starting a newsletter: pick a punchy 1–2 word name, state word count/read time up top, and hard-cap total length under 1,000 words.
- Always open with a "1 big thing" item and close with something personal/fun.

**Frameworks introduced**:

### Ch17: Smart Brevity Your Speeches

**TL;DR**: Great speeches are short and built around one memorable, memorizable "Big Thought" stated verbatim at both the open and close — illustrated by Nancy Duarte's "secret structure" analysis of iconic speeches, TED's hard 18-minute cap, and a contrast between a flat 28-minute Biden commencement address and a razor-sharp Pamela Meyer TED opening.

**Key concepts**:
- **Duarte's secret structure** — describe the status quo → contrast with your idea → move back and forth between what-is and what-could-be → call to action → end with a vivid picture of the idea realized (used to analyze MLK's "I Have a Dream" and Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch).
- **The neighbor-encounter rule** — your speech's opening anecdote should last exactly as long as a chance hallway/street encounter with a neighbor would naturally run — social instinct already tells you the right length.
- **Verbatim Big Thought bookending** — write your one-sentence takeaway word-for-word, state it near the top ("The one thing I want you to remember today is...") and restate it verbatim at the close.
- **TED's 18-minute rule** — "short enough to hold people's attention... long enough to say something that matters" (Chris Anderson).

**Quotes worth keeping**:

**Action items / Implications**:

### Ch18: Smart Brevity Your Presentations

**TL;DR**: PowerPoint decks fail because they try to do the brain's work with dense bulleted text — the actual, research-backed fix is one message per slide, favor images over text (recall jumps from ~10% to ~65% with an arresting image, per molecular biologist John Medina), cut ruthlessly (McKinsey's onboarding rule: turn 20 slides into 2), and always end by explicitly asking for what you want.

**Key concepts**:
- **The "3-second billboard" test** — treat each slide like a highway billboard: could someone glancing at 65 mph get the point?
- **Two-stimuli limit** — the brain can process roughly two simultaneous inputs well (e.g., spoken word + image); adding bulleted text on top of both overloads it.
- **Image-driven recall** (John Medina) — an arresting image paired with words lifts recall to ~65%, versus ~10% for words heard alone.
- **McKinsey's 20-to-2 rule** — new hires are told: for every 20 slides you want, use 2.
- **"Always be closing"** — every deck should end with an explicit, specific ask, distilled to as few words as possible.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Imagine a widely used and expensive prescription drug that claimed to make you beautiful but didn't. Instead, the drug had frequent, serious side effects: making us stupid, degrading the quality and credibility of our communication, turning us into bores, wasting our colleagues' time." (Edward Tufte, on PowerPoint, quoted Ch. 18)
> "Simplify to exaggerate." (Ch. 18, guiding principle for presentations)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Before building your next deck, write down the precise outcome you want and the 3–5 points that support it — that's your outline, before you open PowerPoint.
- Apply the McKinsey 20-to-2 cut: draft everything you want to include, then cut it to a tenth.
- End every deck with an explicit ask, not a generic "Questions?" slide.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **5-step deck build** (Ch. 18): (1) Write the precise outcome + 3–5 supporting points, ordered like a legal case. (2) Simplify every slide to one message, ≤3-second read, one font/style. (3) Pair points with pictures, not bulleted text. (4) Keep total structure to one big idea + 3–5 points (~a dozen slides total). (5) Always close with a specific, explicit ask.

### Ch19: Smart Brevity Your Social Media

### Ch20: Smart Brevity Your Visuals

**Key concepts**:

### Ch22: Communicate Inclusively

**TL;DR**: Smart Brevity's plainness and directness naturally strip out much of the cultural bias and complexity that creeps into writing, but inclusive communication also requires deliberate practice — being specific about identity only when relevant, avoiding stereotype-reinforcing descriptors, and checking your blind spots — grounded in co-founder Roy Schwartz's personal account of being mislabeled as a "problem child" due to undiagnosed dyslexia.

**Key concepts**:
- **The relevance test** — per the Asian American Journalists Association: "Is race/ethnicity/religion/national origin relevant?" — if not, omit it; using an identity descriptor without explaining its relevance perpetuates stereotypes.
- **The swap test** — mentally swap one identity/race/nationality for another in a sentence and check whether the language and intent still read as nonjudgmental.
- **Accessibility math** — 1 in 5 children report learning disabilities (National Center for Learning Disabilities); extrapolated to adults, that's ~65 million Americans, i.e., a real fraction of any audience.
- **Plain language as accessibility** — clear, jargon-free, short-sentence writing directly helps people with learning disabilities (e.g., dyslexia) and non-native English speakers, even though that wasn't the original design goal.

**Quotes worth keeping**:
> "Is race/ethnicity/religion/national origin relevant?... Using the descriptors when they're not relevant or without explaining their relevance perpetuates harmful stereotypes." (Asian American Journalists Association, quoted Ch. 22)
> "Short and not very sweet. Do you not own a dictionary?" (Roy's 7th-grade English teacher's comment on his dyslexia-affected paper, Ch. 22)
> "Dyslexia caused great pain in my life, but it gave me a gift. I had to problem-solve, work harder, create systems to thrive." (Roy Schwartz, Ch. 22)

**Action items / Implications**:
- Before including someone's race/ethnicity/nationality/disability in a piece of writing, apply the relevance test — cut it if it's not doing real work.
- Have sensitive or complex writing reviewed by someone with a different lived experience before publishing.
- Default to plain, jargon-free language as a baseline accessibility practice, not just a style preference.

**Frameworks introduced**:
- **Roy's 3-point inclusive-writing checklist** (Ch. 22): (1) Write in plain, clear language. (2) Use bullet points to force separation of distinct ideas. (3) Keep it simple and short — complexity confuses, abstraction alienates, length loses.

**Open questions / disagreements**:
- The chapter is candid that Smart Brevity wasn't *designed* for accessibility/inclusion — it's a beneficial side effect the authors noticed in practice, which is worth naming explicitly rather than overclaiming intent.
