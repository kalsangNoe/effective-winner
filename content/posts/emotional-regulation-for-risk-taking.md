Fear, anxiety, and anger aren't obstacles to suppress before taking original action — they're states that can be deliberately redirected. The research favors reframing over calming down, and redirecting anger toward victims rather than perpetrators.

## Key Concepts
- **Reframe anxiety as excitement, don't try to calm down.** Saying "I am excited" instead of "I am calm" before a stressful task (public speaking, a math test, singing in public) measurably improved performance across multiple experiments — because both states are physiologically high-arousal, so converting rather than suppressing works with the body's actual state instead of against it (the "go system" vs. "stop system").
- **Defensive pessimism helps once you're already committed.** Envisioning worst-case scenarios in detail, once a course of action is settled, drives thorough preparation and outperforms both blind optimism and no preparation at all — but only after commitment; before commitment, imagining failure just triggers avoidance.
- **Venting escalates anger; it doesn't relieve it.** Controlled experiments (hitting a punching bag while thinking about the person who wronged you) show venting groups became *more* aggressive afterward, not less — contradicting the catharsis theory.
- **Redirect anger toward the victim, not the perpetrator.** Focusing on who was harmed by an injustice (rather than dwelling on who caused it) produces "empathetic anger" — a constructive, justice-seeking motivation — rather than retaliatory aggression. This was a deliberate technique in MLK's civil-rights training and in Otpor!'s nonviolent campaign against Milošević.
- **A single ally is enough to break conformity pressure.** In Asch's classic conformity experiments, adding just one dissenting voice to a unanimous wrong-answer group cut conformity errors from ~37% to ~5.5%. You don't need a majority to make dissent feel safe — you need one visible ally.

## Details
The pattern across all these findings is the same: strong emotion is energy, and the useful move is redirecting its target or its label, not eliminating it. "Calm down" fights the body's actual arousal state; "get excited" works with it. "Don't be angry" is often impossible mid-injustice; "be angry *for* the victim, not *at* the perpetrator" channels the same energy toward something constructive.
