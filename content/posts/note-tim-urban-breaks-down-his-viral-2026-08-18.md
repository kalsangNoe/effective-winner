## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Tim Urban (Wait But Why) about how his writing voice, drawings, and research habits developed. Tim's core argument: his distinctive style came from six years of writing an obscure blog with zero audience pressure, which let a real personality form before anyone was watching. He treats writing as an emotional-experience-design problem (curating "dopamine hits" for the reader), uses procrastination as a mulling process rather than pure avoidance, and edits by literally reading his own drafts as if he were a stranger encountering them for the first time. He also lays out a specific technique — "calling chapter 11 bankruptcy" — for rescuing an overloaded piece of writing by cutting it and starting a new, more honest one.

## Key ideas

- **Voice comes from writing without being a writer.** Tim wrote for six years on a pre-Wait But Why blog with maybe six readers, with "no pressure" and no self-conception as "a writer." That anonymity let an unintentional, undistilled personal voice form — he says he wouldn't have his current voice without that period.
- **Confidence, not skill, gates personality on the page.** He describes writing confidently ("I'm just going to say whatever, I can break all the rules") versus writing "less interestingly, more matter of fact" when he's not feeling it. Putting more of himself in is "about mood," and a trick to reset is asking: "What if I were just blogging? If this were 2006, how would I explain this to a friend?"
- **Writing is curating an emotional experience, densely.** He compares it to a stand-up comedian's hour special: the audience is watching a distilled, funniest-moments-only version of years of material. His research process is mostly "bored... dry" digging for the rare "euphoric little jolt" of a genuinely interesting fact, then he filters ruthlessly down to just those jolts for the reader.
- **"Chapter 11 bankruptcy" as an editing move.** When a long piece (his *What's Our Problem?* book chapter, in this case) gets so overloaded he can't make it work, he "declares chapter 11 bankruptcy" — cuts it and starts a cleaner version rather than continuing to patch a structurally broken draft.
- **Public feedback is a stress test, not just a threat.** Publishing draft chapters and getting hundreds of comments (some hostile — "this is so disappointing coming from you") was, in his telling, "incredibly valuable." He weighs comments by substance, not tone, and specifically sought out one especially thorough, critical anonymous commenter to review his full book draft.
- **Procrastination is often mulling, not avoidance.** He distinguishes "self-defeating" procrastination (avoiding hard work out of fear) from a productive kind where reading more sources or thinking longer becomes "fun" and produces a better final piece — the same impulse that makes him want to get an A instead of a B-.
- **History is drama and gossip, not dates.** His pitch for what makes history interesting: "it's the best fiction story, and we are... inside of it." He credits primary-source details (e.g., John Adams's flirtatious letters to his wife) with turning distant, mythic figures into real people — a "time machine" effect that plot summaries alone don't give.
- **"Horizontal history."** Instead of tracing one lineage backward in time, he likes tracing what else was happening simultaneously — e.g., the Protestant Reformation, Copernicus, and Shakespeare all landing in roughly the same window — because it makes people from the past feel like they were living in *their* present, not "old-timey" background extras.
- **On writing the TED Talk.** He describes drafting it by talking it out loud to himself as if explaining it to a person, then capturing that spoken version in writing, because live speech forces a natural, non-academic structure. He eventually memorized it close to word-for-word because a talk, unlike a blog post, is a single unrepeatable take that "is going to be online forever."
- **Tools were mostly about avoiding Word's failure modes.** He recounts losing work to Word crashes and OneDrive/autosave issues, and now relies on Google Docs plus his assistant's research notes in Workflowy — less about features, more about not losing drafts.
- **His stated goal for young/aspiring writers:** don't aim for "good writing" first — aim for a reader reaction: "teach me something or make me laugh." He frames academic writing (thesis statements, formal structure) as valuable as a discipline but responsible for making people think writing has to be serious, when it doesn't.
- **The final edit is reading as a stranger.** His last-pass method is to try to forget he wrote the piece and read it purely as a reader encountering it for the first time, flagging anything "not interesting, or not clear" — treating himself as the last gatekeeper before an audience sees it.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "It's easy when you're writing for the most snooty critic... that person does not matter. For me, [it's] my silly readers who I am friends with in my head."
- "I'm calling chapter 11 bankruptcy" — on cutting an overloaded chapter and rewriting it from scratch.
- "History is just drama and gossip... these betrayals, and then these surprise attacks... this big picture."
- "It isn't a college paper. If you're not having fun, [don't] do it." — on his reminder-to-self while drafting blog posts.
- "The monkey is going to suddenly do everything" — his metaphor for procrastination/avoidance taking over when confidence dips.
- "I call it horizontal history" — describing tracing simultaneous events across a time period instead of one lineage backward.
- "Teach me something or make me laugh" — his summary of what he wants a first-time writer's reader to feel.

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Do I have (or need) an equivalent of Tim's "obscure six-year blog" — a low-stakes space where I can write without an audience shaping the voice prematurely?
- Where in my own drafts would "declaring chapter 11 bankruptcy" — cutting and rewriting rather than patching — actually be the faster path, and what's stopping me from doing it sooner?
- Am I weighting critical feedback by substance the way Tim describes, or am I discounting it by tone?
- Could "horizontal history" (simultaneity) be a useful structuring device outside of history writing — e.g., in explaining any timeline-heavy topic?
- What's my version of the final "read it as a stranger" pass — do I actually do this, or do I edit while still inside the writer's head?
