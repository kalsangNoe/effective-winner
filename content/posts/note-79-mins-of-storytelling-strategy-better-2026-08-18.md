## TL;DR
Nick Bilton (author of *American Kingpin* and *Hatching Twitter*, journalist for the New York Times and Vanity Fair) argues that narrative nonfiction should be built like a screenplay crossed with a murder mystery: everything is a story, every story needs tension from the first line, and craft comes from stealing techniques across mediums. His core method is reporting first, writing second — build a database of chat logs, interviews, and social media, find where the timestamps line up, then "paint the room" using real sensory detail instead of guesswork. He's obsessive about theme (won't write a word until he knows it), character nuance (no one is purely good or evil), and pacing (cliffhangers, short chapters, showing not telling).

## Key ideas

- **Everything is a story.** A friend, a Hollywood screenwriter, told Bilton after reading an early draft of *American Kingpin*: "At the end of the day, everything is a story... You go to the supermarket, it's a story that's being pitched to you to buy this cereal." What makes a story great is emotion, tension, and characters.

- **Two craft sources: screenwriting and murder mysteries.** Bilton says he learned narrative nonfiction not from journalism school but from (1) learning to write screenplays ~10 years before this interview, and (2) a book on how to write murder mysteries, sought out after a friend annotated a draft of the Twitter book with "I hate them all" — meaning he didn't care about any of the characters.

- **Techniques stolen from murder mystery writers to make bad people sympathetic:**
  - Every murderer has a mother who loves them — give villains someone who loves them.
  - Give them mundane human moments (losing car keys, forgetting the mail) so readers can identify with something even if not with the crime.
  - Show the good intentions behind bad actions — "the road to hell is paved with good intentions." Readers need to feel what the character was actually going for.

- **Screenwriting discipline for pacing:** a screenplay page = 1 minute of screen time, so every word counts, unlike a book where you can meander. The rule: "get into the scene as late as possible and out as early as possible." His example — instead of a long scene of an old couple sitting down to dinner and arguing, cut straight to: "Honey, pass me the salt." / "Did you not hear what Dr. Brown said the other day?" Recommended book: *Save the Cat*.

- **Theme comes before a single word is written.** "I won't start a book until I know what the theme is" — like being able to pitch the book in an elevator ride, even though it's 100,000 words. Examples: his Tim Cook/Apple Vision Pro Vanity Fair piece — theme was "the future is coming whether we like it or not." Theranos piece — "fake it till you make it" taken too far. Elon Musk piece — "he needs a problem to solve, and if there isn't one, he'll create one."

- **No one is fully good or evil.** Citing Solzhenitsyn's line that the line between good and evil runs through every human heart, Bilton insists on nuance: Ross Ulbricht (Silk Road) was willing to have people killed to protect his empire, but also helped old ladies cross the street and bought flowers for a flower-stall worker "because he figured no one ever buys flowers for the woman who works at the flower stall." Screenwriter Charles Randolph (*The Big Short*) told him: "You can't look down on your characters, you have to look out with them."

- **"Painting the room" — reporting technique for vivid scenes.** Learned from watching NYT colleague Andrew Ross Sorkin interview a source who couldn't legally discuss deal details, so Sorkin instead asked about the boardroom: mahogany desk, croissants, carpet or wood floor, the view of the Statue of Liberty. The resulting piece felt authoritative even though Sorkin knew nothing about the actual deal — because painting physical detail makes the reader feel present.

- **Reporting as Tetris.** For *American Kingpin*, Bilton got two and a half years of Dread Pirate Roberts' chat logs, put them in a database with timestamps, cross-referenced interviews (roommate, girlfriend, friends) and social media (tweets, Venmo, geotagged photos), then physically visited every location — sitting in the same chair, ordering the same sushi. He once reconstructed an untagged campground location by calculating drive times from geotagged photos of the Golden Gate Bridge and searching Google Maps for wooded areas within that radius.

- **Sensory detail, used sparingly.** "We describe what we see and what we hear, but we never describe what we smell" — a trick from murder mystery writing he deliberately uses. But he warns against over-describing ("purple prose"): two or three specific details (pine needles, smoke, crickets) do more work than a paragraph of description, because "the brain is very good at taking a few details and inferring" the rest — like a cartoon character.

- **Cliffhangers: give away enough, not too much.** "Giving away enough that you want to keep reading but not too much that you know what's going to happen." Cliffhangers can't be spaced too far apart, or the payoff feels disconnected — you "seed" a question early and answer it later, but not 200 pages later.

- **Character introduction follows screenplay rules.** In film, the first person you see is the main character — you don't meet side characters first. Bilton builds books the same way, and re-anchors characters with a repeated physical trait (Jared the HSI agent always has a Rubik's Cube nearby) so readers remember who's who without re-explaining.

- **Truth in nonfiction is a judgment call.** When two sources disagree — e.g., Jack Dorsey remembers rain and soup at Whole Foods, Biz Stone remembers sun and tea at the Japanese Tea Garden — Bilton checks external records like weather almanacs. Trivial details (shirt color) he just picks one; consequential details (shot in the head vs. leg) he has to nail down or can't write it.

- **Dialogue should show, not tell — "not be expositional."** Rather than writing "I went and sat in the room with Dave and we talked about X," describe the room and let the conversation happen in it. The goal is making the reader do some work, but not too much ("you're talking to me like I'm a baby").

- **Character conflict is structural.** "In any story, one character must be trying to do something and another character must be standing in their way." Walking into the kitchen for water isn't a story; someone blocking you with a knife is.

- **The magazine feature has changed because of the internet.** Thirty years ago a New Yorker or Vanity Fair lead might spend 800 words describing a scene because readers had no other way to picture it. Today "we can Google it in 12 seconds," so that style of scene-painting reads as dated — the exception being his Tim Cook piece, where he *did* paint the room because no one had ever seen Apple's secret hardware labs.

- **Writing is like editing a screenplay: read it aloud.** Bilton now uses AI tools (Speechify, Eleven Labs) to read his drafts back to him so he can hear the pacing.

- **On finishing and not being precious.** Quoting advice he calls "keep typing until it turns into writing": he's written millions of words and doesn't remember 98% of them, and it doesn't matter. After a piece about the Amazon Kindle triggered backlash early in his career, his editor told him: "You're just going to write another story tomorrow. Don't worry about it."

## Open questions / reflection prompts

- Do I know the theme of what I'm writing before I start, or am I discovering it as I go — and would naming it upfront ("elevator pitch") sharpen the draft?
- Where in my own writing am I "telling" instead of "painting the room" — describing conclusions instead of the physical details that let a reader arrive at them?
- Am I over-describing scenes the way pre-internet magazine writers did, when two or three sharp sensory details would do more work?
- Which of my "characters" (people, ideas, competitors) are currently flat villains or heroes in my writing, and what's the mundane human detail that would give them nuance?
- Where could I borrow the "seed early, pay off later" cliffhanger structure instead of resolving tension immediately?
