Guy Kawasaki's framework (drawn from Apple, VC, and Google experience) for what separates real innovation from incremental improvement: jumping to a fundamentally different curve rather than optimizing the current one — which requires defining your product by the benefit it provides, not the thing it currently does.

## Key Concepts
- **Jump curves, don't ride them.** The classic example: better ice-harvesting and better ice factories are improvements on the ice curve; the refrigerator is a jump to a new curve entirely. Companies fail to jump because they define themselves by *what they do* ("we make ice") rather than *the benefit they provide* ("we keep things cold").
- **Make meaning, not money.** Durable innovation starts from wanting to change the world (Apple: democratize computing; Google: democratize information; eBay: democratize commerce). Money follows meaning, not the reverse.
- **Mantra over mission statement.** A 2–3 word phrase capturing *why* you exist (Nike: "Authentic athletic performance"; FedEx: "Peace of mind") beats a 50-word corporate mission statement — it's usable in daily decisions.
- **Roll the DICEE.** Great products are Deep, Intelligent, Complete, Empowering, Elegant.
- **Don't worry, be crappy.** Ship the revolutionary-but-imperfect version on the new curve rather than waiting for perfection — with an explicit carve-out for domains like biotech where "crappy" is unacceptable.
- **Let 100 flowers blossom.** Customers, not the maker, ultimately decide what a product is for — the Macintosh survived its early years not via the spreadsheet/database use case it was designed around, but because desktop publishing (PageMaker) redefined it.
- **Polarize people.** Products that matter (TiVo, Harley-Davidson, Macintosh) are loved and hated — fearing that reaction is a sign of playing it too safe.
- **Niche thyself.** Plot yourself on a uniqueness × value 2×2; aim for unique + valuable, avoid the "neither" quadrant (Kawasaki's example of failure: pets.com).
- **Perfect your pitch.** The 10/20/30 rule — 10 slides, 20 minutes, 30-point font — forces clarity; tailor the pitch to the specific audience rather than reusing one deck.
- **Beware "Bozos."** Obvious losers are easy to dismiss; the dangerous ones are well-dressed, rich, and famous — their success reads as "smart" but is often just luck, and following their advice uncritically is a trap.

## Details
The throughline across the list is that innovation is fundamentally a *definitional* act before it's a technical one — you have to first re-describe what business you're in (benefit, not artifact) before a curve-jump becomes visible as an option. Historical quotes Kawasaki cites as cautionary tales of curve-blindness: IBM's Thomas Watson ("a world market for maybe five computers"), an internal Western Union memo calling the telephone "of no value" (1876), and Ken Olsen doubting anyone would want a computer at home.
