## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Ted Gioia (jazz historian, "The Honest Broker" on Substack) on how he built a writing career around honesty, deep reading, and resistance to formula. Gioia's core thesis: most writing today is dishonest because writers optimize for coolness, ideology, editors, or algorithms instead of the reader, and the fix is to become an "honest broker" the reader can trust. He traces his method back to decades of disciplined reading (mostly pre-1900 classics until his 40s), a refusal to chase dopamine-culture formulas, and a belief that inspiration is a semi-mystical, uncontrollable force best protected by taking risks rather than repeating what worked. He closes by predicting a cultural swing back from anger and distraction toward kindness, substance, and slowness — and argues writers should build for that reversal rather than chase current trends.

## Key ideas

- **The Honest Broker origin story.** Gioia picked up the concept doing business consulting in China, where a drunk Australian in a hotel bar explained that since there's no rule of law, every community has an "honest broker" — someone everyone trusts who makes introductions without charging upfront, cashing in the favor only years later. Gioia realized music writing had the same trust problem the media broadly has ("nobody trusts the media... nobody trusts the expert") and decided to become that trusted broker for his readers.
- **Three questions before publishing:** *Is this fair? Is this accurate? Will this be persuasive to all fair-minded observers?* He estimates roughly 50% of writing that circulates in newsletters/articles today has a hidden agenda, and readers can smell it.
- **Abandon the "cool pose."** The biggest curse for music writers especially is performing coolness (he cites Lester Bangs, Hunter Thompson) rather than speaking honestly; he thinks the gap between public image and private self is what destroys writers like Thompson. His countermove: deliberately admit his own limitations and embarrassing moments in his writing (e.g., a story about cops arresting him at Stan Getz's house) — self-exposure builds reader trust, modeled on Karl Ove Knausgaard's unflattering self-portrayal.
- **Style follows persona, then follows the medium.** Early in his career he changed prose style per book, borrowing from critic Hugh Kenner, who wrote about Yeats "like Yeats" and Pound "like Pound." His own West Coast Jazz book was flashy/Hollywood; his Delta Blues book turned slow, soil-and-crops, Faulknerian. On Substack, his prose became conversational without planning it — a Substack colleague, Dan Stone, told him readers feel like personal friends because they get frequent emails, so he started writing the way he'd talk to a friend over dinner, occasionally dropping obscure references (e.g., Kant's "dogmatic slumber," which came from Hume) as a treat for the 1-in-1000 reader who'll catch it.
- **Journaling exercise for finding your honest voice:** keep a private journal nobody will see; write brutally honest entries about what irritated you each day; after ~30 days, reread it — you'll find risk-taking material you didn't know you had. His reasoning: evolution rewards *fitting in*, not honesty (citing Girard on mimetic imitation), so writers must actively give themselves — or "propel themselves into" — permission to break from the herd.
- **Read old before new, then flip it.** He deliberately read old books/music when young and only started consuming contemporary fiction and 80% new music around age 45 — the opposite of most people. Rationale: he didn't feel qualified to judge a contemporary novel until he'd read 300–500 classics first, so he could place new work against tradition (e.g., reading *Breaking Bad* as literal Greek tragedy).
- **Domain expertise as a writing edge.** His MBA/BCG consulting background taught him to analyze S-1 filings, read financial statements, and predict trends (he became "a futurist" for Fortune 500 clients) — skills he later applied to music criticism, e.g. using epidemic-spread models from business forecasting to argue jazz was an *innovation* born of densely populated, diverse New Orleans, while Delta blues was a *retention* of old tradition preserved by rural isolation. His advice to writing students: find one or two areas of expertise outside writing itself — a lawyer-turned-sportswriter who understands athletes' legal troubles will beat a pure writer every time.
- **Input over output.** He spends more time per day on "input" (reading, listening) than writing; writing doesn't start until the afternoon. He faults employers for measuring only output and never asking "what are you reading?" — arguing input quality determines output quality in *any* field, from cooking to driving Uber.
- **Formula is the enemy.** He "hates formulas" — cites the flattening of Hollywood (Disney's weak year, declining six-month box-office trend) and country radio staying on old hits rather than embracing new ones. He credits the Beatles' relentless self-reinvention (from the unsophisticated "Love Me Do" to constant change) with forcing labels to grant artists more creative freedom in the 60s — the opposite of today's risk-averse system, which he says punishes genuine originality ("the system will shut you down").
- **Inspiration is real and should be protected, not repeated.** Draws on jazz saxophonist John Handy's story: after an electrifying, spontaneous solo, bandleader Charles Mingus told him "don't ever do that again" — meaning don't try to recreate it, stay open to the moment instead. Gioia links this to songwriting-in-dreams (Paul McCartney's "Yesterday" and "Let It Be," Keith Richards' riff for "Satisfaction," Sting's "Every Breath You Take," Billy Joel's "Just the Way You Are") and to poets historically crediting the Muse. He starts each morning trying to recall his dreams as a deliberate practice.
- **Prediction: culture reverses in 5–10 years.** Citing George Soros's theory of reflexivity, Gioia bets that anger/outrage culture will swing back to kindness and compassion, and that "dopamine culture" (distraction as a third category beyond art and entertainment, engineered by social platforms the way potato-chip companies engineer craveability) will give way to demand for substance — analogous to the backlash against processed food toward organic/gourmet cooking. His advice to writers tempted to chase quick hooks: don't chase the algorithm, because a viral 10-second clip (he cites a 2-million-view Instagram clip of himself) builds no lasting relationship, while consistent long-form writing does.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "So much of the writing out there is dishonest, there's a crisis of trust, nobody trusts the media... this is why I write directly for the reader."
- "It's very difficult to come out with something creative and fresh because the system will shut you down."
- "I believe we are within five or ten years at most from the culture reversing" — from anger and outrage to kindness and compassion.
- "Don't ever do that again" — Charles Mingus to saxophonist John Handy, meaning: don't try to recreate a moment of pure inspiration; stay open to the next one instead.
- "What I said was, this is not correct, because the most important things in our life cannot be sensed with our sense organs" — on why he became less rationalistic and more open to metaphysics over time.
- "I believe a lot of the crises we have in the culture now are driven by too heavy a reliance on formulas."
- "The reading is like fuel in the tank... I don't start writing till I have read a big chunk every day."
- "It's death to try to chase the culture... those editors are out of a job now."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I performing a "cool pose" instead of writing honestly — and what would it cost to drop it?
- Do I read enough "input" relative to how much I write — and am I reading things that stretch me, or just things that confirm what I already think?
- What is the equivalent of Gioia's 30-day brutally-honest private journal for my own writing practice — could I run that experiment myself?
- Am I optimizing any part of my writing for short-term distribution (hooks, virality) at the expense of the long-term trust relationship with readers?
- What non-writing domain expertise do I have that could give my writing genuine differentiation, the way Gioia's business/finance background did for his music criticism?
