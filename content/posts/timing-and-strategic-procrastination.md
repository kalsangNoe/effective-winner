Acting first or fastest is usually assumed to be an advantage. In practice, first movers face a well-documented disadvantage, and deliberate delay — strategic procrastination — measurably improves creative output by giving divergent thinking room to work.

## Key Concepts
- **Procrastination can boost creativity, but only for tasks you're intrinsically motivated on.** In controlled experiments, people randomly assigned to procrastinate (while still holding the task in mind) produced ideas rated 28% more creative than people who started immediately. The effect disappeared if the "procrastination" was just an unrelated break, or if people weren't invested in the problem.
- **Mechanism: delay prevents "seize and freeze."** Starting immediately tends to produce the first, most conventional idea that comes to mind. Deliberately not finishing yet keeps divergent thinking open longer.
- **Famous procrastinators used the delay productively.** Leonardo da Vinci worked on the *Mona Lisa* and *The Last Supper* on and off for over a decade each; MLK finished writing the "I Have a Dream" closing the night before delivering it; Lincoln wrote the Gettysburg Address's final paragraph the morning of the speech.
- **First-mover disadvantage is real and underrated.** Being first to market or first to act carries higher failure risk than commonly assumed — early movers absorb the cost of educating the market and iterating through unknowns that later movers can skip.
- **This connects to risk management**, not just creativity: waiting is itself a way of not putting "all your eggs in one basket" — see [[Risk-Taking & Risk Portfolios]].

## Details
The Egyptian language distinguished two verbs for what English collapses into one word "procrastination" — one for laziness, one for waiting for the right time. Grant's research (with Jihae Shin) suggests the useful kind of procrastination is *active*: the task stays live in the back of the mind (e.g., playing a game while still thinking about the pending proposal), rather than being fully set aside. That's the difference between productive incubation and simple avoidance.
