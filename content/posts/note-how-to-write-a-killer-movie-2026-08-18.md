## TL;DR
Eric Roth (Forrest Gump, Benjamin Button, Dune, Killers of the Flower Moon) walks David Perell through his screenwriting process: he starts every writing day back on page one to keep "eroding" and reshoring the material, builds characters from accumulated research detail rather than plot function, and anchors every scene to a single underlying theme. His stories work, he argues, because they stay emotionally honest and specific — visual, human, subtextual — without tipping into cartoonishness. He writes longhand-adjacent on an old DOS program, plans only his opening and closing scenes in advance, and treats the middle as something he discovers day by day.

## Key ideas
- **Starting from page one every day.** Roth rewrites from the beginning of the draft daily, calling it a fight against "erosion" — things falling down that need to be shored back up. It keeps him "living" in the material and lets him catch what feels tired before it compounds.
- **Character comes from accumulated detail, not backstory summary.** He cites director Michael Cimino giving actor Mickey Rourke a wallet stuffed with a fake photo, a phone number, and personal ephemera the character would carry — Rourke never had to look at it, but it shaped the performance. Roth applies the same logic on the page: "if you're trying to make something out of nothing," small concrete objects accumulate into a life.
- **Theme comes first, scenes get tested against it.** "I think you start with at least I start with what's the theme of the movie... that's always going to be underneath it." When a scene feels inert, he asks how it relates back to the theme rather than patching the scene itself. Example: Killers of the Flower Moon's theme was justice — specifically the absence of it.
- **Subtext over exposition.** The best writing says one thing while talking about another — he cites a High Noon scene where a judge illustrates the theme by describing an unrelated incident in ancient Athens rather than commenting directly. His rule of thumb for young writers: "talk about a dream you had rather than tell us that you're upset with your mother."
- **Voice comes from listening, not invention.** Forrest Gump's "shrimp" speech (Bubba's monologue) was written by yelling "give me shrimp dishes!" at his family on vacation and typing what came back — an exercise in generating texture, not plotting.
- **Fanciful, not cartoonish.** Roth calls his characters "the other side of the moon" — not real people, but built with enough psychological consistency that they feel real for two hours. The line he draws: characters should invite empathy ("this is somebody worth caring about"), not just cartoon reaction.
- **Villains don't need to be likable, just legible.** "I don't think they should be likable. I think you should potentially understand why they are what they make them tick" — even when the motive resists full explanation, as with real-world mass shooters he references without pretending to understand.
- **"Change the weather" as an unblocking technique.** When stuck on a scene, Roth introduces rain, wind, or a specific time of day (dusk, dawn, "the children's hour") to shift the emotional register and unlock new choices — not as decoration but as a way to make characters act "slightly differently."
- **Plan the beginning and end; discover the middle.** He always knows a movie's opening and closing scene before writing; the only exception in his career was Munich, where the ending location changed after the World Trade Center was destroyed. The middle he outlines only in single-word beats ("wedding, shootout") and ends each writing day on a scene he's satisfied with so he isn't dreading the next morning.
- **Directors are the key collaborators, and it's a negotiation.** "It's a marriage. You have to be willing to get married." He looks for a "third rail" where writer and director can agree, citing different working styles with Spielberg (visual, entertainment-driven, "Rube Goldberg" plotting), Fincher (brought unexpected personal vulnerability to Benjamin Button, partly echoing his own father's death), and the writers' room he ran for Ben Affleck, where every collaborator converged on wanting authenticity above all else.
- **Personal grief bled directly into the work.** Roth lost both parents while writing The Curious Case of Benjamin Button, and says that loss shaped how he approached the film's meditation on mortality and time. A friend and film critic (Elvis Mitchell) once told him his movies are "all about loneliness" — Roth agrees it's likely a driving force.
- **Craft habits:** he still writes on an old DOS program that loses pages past ~40 without printing; he keeps notes minimal (just a word like "tension" to remind him what a scene needs); and misattributed lines (a Benjamin Button monologue people now frame as F. Scott Fitzgerald, when Roth wrote it himself) don't bother him.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "I always start from page one... I call it sort of a sense of erosion — if there's things that need to be fixed and backfilled, like dirt piled back up."
- "I think you start with — at least I start with — what's the theme of the movie? What's the movie literally about? ... It's always going to be underneath it, so that when you have a scene that feels inert, you have to find a way to somehow utilize it with the theme of the movie."
- "The great writers know how to write subtextually. They don't write what's literal... talk about a dream you had rather than tell us that you're upset with your mother."
- "I call them the other side of the moon... these are really good movies — they last — but it's almost like they're gone, you know? It's it's like Fredo and Michael in the Godfather... it still can feel like that."
- "I don't think they should be likable. I think you should potentially understand why they are — what makes them tick — even if you may not always understand it."
- "It's very simple. If you're stuck, I just think, well, I think I'll make it rain and all of a sudden you look at it differently."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I identifying a single theme before I write, or discovering it after the fact through revision — and would naming it earlier make my inert scenes easier to diagnose?
- Where in my own writing am I explaining a feeling directly instead of routing it through a concrete, tangential image (Roth's "talk about the dream" rule)?
- Do my characters carry the equivalent of Mickey Rourke's wallet — accumulated, specific detail that never gets stated outright but shapes how they come across?
- When I'm stuck, do I have my own version of "change the weather" — a small mechanical move that shifts tone rather than trying to force the plot forward?
- Am I drawing the line between "fanciful" (emotionally real, exaggerated) and "cartoonish" (hollow, unearned) carefully enough in my own work, or defaulting to one or the other?
