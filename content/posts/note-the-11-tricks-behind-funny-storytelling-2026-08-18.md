## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Scott Dikkers, former editor-in-chief of The Onion, about the mechanics of writing funny. Dikkers argues comedy isn't innate talent but a codifiable craft: find your point of view (POV), write down opinions, run them through one of 11 "funny filters" (irony, character, shock, hyperbole, wordplay, reference, madcap, parody, analogy, misplaced focus, meta humor), and escalate a single narrow idea to absurdity rather than sprinkling jokes everywhere. The conversation covers The Onion's editorial process, why quantity beats quality for beginners, the role of a "straight person" to set up jokes, character archetypes, and how to land an ending. Throughout, Dikkers stresses restraint and simplicity over piling on cleverness.

## Key ideas
- **Comedy is POV, not material.** "It's all about your perspective... certain people go through life as normies... other people think 'there's something not quite right about this.'" Cultivating a slightly off way of seeing the world, not finding inherently funny subjects, is the real skill.
- **Quantity over quality is the #1 beginner mistake.** People spend months polishing one piece instead of writing many. The Onion staff wrote roughly a dozen articles a week for 40 years, generating hundreds of headline pitches to land a dozen.
- **Start from opinions, not jokes.** Dikkers' onboarding exercise: write down 10 opinions about anything. Subtext (the underlying opinion/observation) "almost never" needs to be funny on its own — it just needs to be a genuine observation, then run through a funny filter.
- **The "take" is everything in short comedy.** Once you have a premise, you're on "a very narrow track" — one joke off in either direction and the piece falls apart. You escalate one obsessive idea to absurdity rather than diversifying.
- **The uncanny valley applies to comedy characters.** Full realism and full cartoon both work; the danger zone is in between. Comedy characters are "cardboard cutouts" — representations, not relatable humans (citing a study where a caricature of Prince Charles was recognized as "more him" than an actual photo).
- **The 11 funny filters:** irony, character, shock, hyperbole, wordplay, reference, madcap, parody, analogy, misplaced focus, meta humor. Stacking 2-3 cleanly (e.g., Peter Griffin = character + irony when he's suddenly smart, or character + madcap when he does something gross-physical) raises the ceiling, but doing three at once in one beat kills it — "one concept, one thing at a time."
- **Irony = swap big/small or invert an opinion.** Simplest filter: take any opinion and flip it, or take something small and inflate it (or vice versa).
- **Analogy works by mapping, not stating.** You hide the real comparison and map its tropes onto the surface subject without naming it — e.g., the Seinfeld "new barber" storyline is secretly an affair story; the audience gets the unstated half and that recognition is the laugh.
- **A straight person is essential.** "Comedy needs a straight person and a joke person." Serious framing (a funeral, a strict classroom, The Onion's AP-style newspaper voice) is what makes the joke land — lose the frame (e.g., an Onion headline shared bare on Facebook) and you lose the joke.
- **The best Onion articles had literally no explicit jokes** — just strict, straight-faced AP-style reporting on an absurd premise, with the joke living entirely in the premise and payoff word. Overloading with acronym gags or silly asides is described as "a killer."
- **Wordplay is a garnish, not a load-bearing filter.** Good wordplay (Dikkers cites Jimmy Kimmel's monologues) rides on top of deeper character/irony humor rather than carrying a joke alone; bad puns just make people groan.
- **Satire flops when it gets preachy.** Contrasts Swift's "A Modest Proposal" (great satire) with the sermonizing final section of *Gulliver's Travels* (bad). "As soon as you're tasting the medicine, you've lost."
- **Clown brain vs. editor brain.** Draft uninhibited (clown brain, no judgment, just generate), then switch to editor brain days later with fresh eyes to cut and refine. Beginners should keep the two phases strictly separate; Dikkers now oscillates fast between them.
- **Endings get two worlds to play in.** Perell's framing, which Dikkers endorses: the body of a piece lives in one world (the fictional premise), but the ending can step back into the real world — often via a reference joke — since by then you're allowed a slight tonal swerve without penalty.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "It's all the second thing... It's all about your perspective. Point of view is everything." — Scott Dikkers
- "The main one I would say is quantity over quality... you have to write a lot of screenplays to get good at writing screenplays." — Scott Dikkers
- "Whatever subtextual idea you had... zero in on that. Focus in on that... you get as absurd as you can with it." — Scott Dikkers
- "The thing that you have to do is you need to go so ridiculous and even more ridiculous than you think... you got to go way farther than you think." — Scott Dikkers, quoting his friend Jack Raines
- "The perfect Onion article had no jokes... not a single joke... It's all totally straight. And to us that was the pinnacle." — Scott Dikkers
- "As soon as you're tasting the medicine, you've lost. You've done a poor job of being a caretaking mother for the reader." — Scott Dikkers, on satire
- "I know from reading The Onion heat maps that 85% of readers never read the full article." — Scott Dikkers
- "At the end you have two worlds to play in, which is the world that we all live in and then the world that you've created in the article." — David Perell

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What are my own 10 opinions right now, and which one has an obvious "funny filter" I could run it through?
- Where in my writing am I piling on cleverness instead of picking one narrow track and escalating it?
- Do my pieces have a genuine "straight person" framing device, or am I relying on the reader already knowing to expect a joke?
- Am I separating "clown brain" drafting from "editor brain" revision, or am I judging ideas the moment they appear?
- For my endings: am I staying inside the piece's created world, or could a reference back to the real world land harder?
