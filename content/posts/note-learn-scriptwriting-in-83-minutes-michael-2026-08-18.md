## TL;DR
Michael Jamin, a 28-year Hollywood sitcom writer (King of the Hill, Beavis and Butt-Head, Just Shoot Me, Tacoma FD), argues that comedy is a byproduct of character and story, not the goal. Jokes come from characters with strong, consistent attitudes who are never trying to be funny; the story underneath has to be about an emotional truth ("what's this really about?"), not just "shit happening." He walks through how he applies these same principles — visual writing, weaknesses over strengths, judgment/blind spots as material — to his personal memoir, and closes with a sharp distinction between using AI to write *for* you (which he rejects) versus *with* you.

## Key ideas

**On comedy and character**
- **Strong attitudes are funny, neutral reactions aren't.** "How's the soup?" / "It's okay" isn't funny; "best [soup] I've ever had" or "worst" is. Dumb, stubborn, pigheaded characters are funny because those are strong attitudes.
- **A character's goal should never be to make the audience laugh.** If a character is trying to be funny, it's cringey. Archie Bunker wasn't trying to be funny — he was just a bigot, and that's why it worked. Same with Larry David on Curb: he's just stating his (extreme, judgmental) opinions.
- **Writers split into "joke people" and "story people."** Jamin was the joke person; his partner Sever(t) Glaram was the story person. The joke person gets the credit, but the story person is more important — no amount of good jokes saves a story that doesn't hold together.
- **Jokes must be congruent with character.** If a line could be swapped from one character's mouth to another's, it's not a great line — the best jokes could only come from that specific character.

**On story vs. plot**
- **"Shit happening" is not a story.** Plot is what happens; story is the emotional underpinning — what it's *really* about. New writers often have plenty of plot and no story.
- **Ferris Bueller's Day Off example:** the plot is skipping school, the Cubs game, stealing a car — but the movie tells you in the second scene what it's *about*: Ferris trying to save his friend Cameron's life before they go separate ways. Stakes = saving a friend's life, not getting a day off.
- **Family/close-relationship stakes are usually the highest stakes**, even in disaster or sci-fi premises (e.g., end-of-the-world movies are really about a father reconciling with his daughter before the meteor hits).
- **Breaking a story means finding the theme simultaneously.** When plotting act breaks on the whiteboard, you're also answering "what's this really about?" — and if you're doing it right, you'll catch yourself repeating the same words (forgive, apology, etc.) and have to find synonyms so the theme doesn't feel obvious.

**On villains and character introductions**
- **A good villain is seductive, not just powerful.** Jamin finds Darth Vader uninteresting ("come to the dark side... why?") but finds Lestat (Interview with the Vampire) compelling because the dark side is genuinely enticing.
- **Antiheroes work by front-loading sympathy.** Tony Soprano is introduced nervous and vulnerable in a psychiatrist's office, then shown building a ramp so ducks can get in and out of his pool — sweet, likable moments — *before* we see him beat someone up. By then the audience is already on his side.
- **Every character needs a strong, deliberate entrance.** On multi-camera sitcoms, the rule is "get them in on a joke, get them off on a joke." How a character is introduced (e.g., a farmer's first action revealing how he treats a sick cow) tells the audience who they are in 2-3 seconds — and writers can spend a full day designing that beat.

**On craft and comedy mechanics**
- **Statements are funny, questions usually aren't**, because a question makes the audience wait for the answer instead of releasing into a laugh (rhetorical questions like "Are you an idiot?" are the exception).
- **Put the funniest word at the end of the sentence** — hard-K words (Kalamazoo, kangaroo) are inherently funnier-sounding; "Cleveland is funnier than Cincinnati."
- **"Stop polishing that turd."** New writers over-polish their first script for months or years. The fix is to finish it, move on, and write five scripts — script five will be light-years better, and nothing about the earlier ones was wasted.
- **Write toward actors' strengths, away from their weaknesses** — e.g., not giving a talented actress lines that make her sound strident if that's not her register; not writing slapstick unless the cast can actually do it.

**On personal/memoir writing**
- **Writing specifics is how you make it universal.** Jamin's memoir mines two running lists on his phone — one of his own weaknesses (judgmental, anxious), one of raw memories — and pairs them up to find the real story underneath a memory.
- **"No one likes an adorable main character."** Writing to look cute or interesting repels readers; writing to look human wins them over. Good stories come from weakness and vulnerability, not strength.
- **Comedy lowers the guard so drama can land.** The first half of a story is funny to relax the reader; then, without another joke to soften it, the second half hits them "in the heart." Jamin stopped a friend from tacking a joke onto an emotional ending: "Allow us to sit in that awful moment."

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Strong attitudes are funny... this is the best [soup] I've ever had in my life... strong attitudes are funny."
- "If a character's goal should never be to make the audience laugh, the character's goal should be to say something truthful... from their own perspective."
- "Shit happening is not a story... Plot is [shit] happening. What's a story? A story is the emotional underpinning underneath it."
- "The higher the stakes, the more interesting the story."
- "Writing specifics is how you make it universal."
- "I judge the quality of a story not so much by how much you're enjoying while you're reading it, but how long it stays with you when you're done reading it."
- "AI is not a tool. It's in lieu of writing... Do you want to be a writer or not? ... Writing is hard. It's supposed to be hard. That's why it feels good."
- "Every story is about a boy trying to become a man, or a girl trying to become a woman, or a toy or a doll trying to become a human being."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I describing "shit happening" (plot) instead of naming what it's actually about (the emotional underpinning)?
- What are my own consistent "blind-spot contradictions" — the Goodfellas-meme-poster kind of inconsistency — that would make a character (or memoir version of myself) feel real rather than either too neat or unraveled?
- Am I trying to look interesting/cute in my writing, or am I willing to write from weakness the way Jamin insists is the only thing that actually connects?
- In pieces I've stalled on, am I "polishing a turd" instead of finishing and moving to the next one?
- If I used AI while drafting, was it writing *for* me (outsourcing the thinking) or *with* me (like his metaphor-finding session) — and does that distinction actually hold up under scrutiny?
