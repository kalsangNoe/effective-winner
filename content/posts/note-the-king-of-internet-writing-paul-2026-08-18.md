## TL;DR
David Perell distills Paul Graham's approach to writing into 11 lessons, arguing that PG's success (essays that birthed Y Combinator and attracted Reddit, Twitch, Stripe, and Sam Altman) comes less from natural talent than from ruthless editing and a willingness to publish only the small fraction of what he writes that's actually good. The through-line is that simplicity is a discipline, not a gift: PG treats writing like line drawing or high-end sushi, where everything unnecessary is stripped away so only substance remains. Writing itself is framed as a thinking tool — most of an essay's ideas only surface after you start writing it — and the goal is always essays that are "general and surprising" rather than merely correct or well-crafted.

## Key ideas
1. **Write simply** — PG rejects jargon and SAT-word prose; readers have a finite "energy budget," and simple language leaves more of it for the ideas themselves. Being forced to write simply forces you to actually understand what you're saying.
2. **Great writing comes from great editing** — PG is "a great deleter," not a great first-draft writer. His rule: "you want to be quick to cut... don't keep something that isn't right just because it contains a few good bits or cost you a lot of effort." He compares his essays to Leonardo da Vinci line drawings — a few strokes that look effortless but demand near-perfection in placement. This is "the game of slugging percentage, not batting average": the goal isn't every draft being great, it's every *published* piece being great.
3. **Writing generates ideas** — "You should expect 80% of the ideas... to come after you start writing the piece," and half of those will be wrong. PG: "If writing down your ideas always makes them more precise and more complete, then no one who hasn't written about a topic has fully formed ideas about it."
4. **Find ideas that are general and surprising** — Surprise without generality is gossip; generality without surprise is a platitude ("work hard, be kind"). PG looks for questions that are "naughty" — counterintuitive, over-ambitious, or heterodox, ideally all three — and follows curiosity about minor questions (like Darwin and the finches) rather than writing what seems impressive.
5. **Try painting** — PG studied at art school (Florence, RISD) and argues painting trains seeing: "in everyday life we aren't consciously aware of how much we're seeing... but when you have to paint something, you have to look more closely." Good writing comes from good seeing (Perell parallels this with Robert Caro repeatedly asking interview subjects "what would I see?").
6. **Do hard things** — PG's essays weren't produced in isolation; they grew out of building Viaweb, founding YC, and constant conversations with founders. Perell: "to write a great essay you must first become the essay by doing hard things."
7. **Cultivate your taste for great writing** — Taste isn't subjective in PG's view; getting better at anything proves your old way was worse, not just different. PG built this by copying masters like Rembrandt and da Vinci in his paintings.
8. **Copy what you like, not what impresses you** — PG stopped imitating "impressive" philosophy papers and short stories once he realized he didn't actually enjoy them. His test: distinguish books you enjoy *having read* from books you enjoy *reading* — copy the latter, especially your lowest-status guilty pleasures.
9. **Write briefly** — but brief ≠ short. PG: "say what you mean and say it briefly." Perell links this to PG's software analogy that shorter code is often more correct because it removes conflicting assumptions.
10. **Don't try to develop a personal style** — "The only style worth having is the one you can't help." PG compares it to Michelangelo, who wasn't trying to paint like Michelangelo — style is a byproduct of trying to write well, not a goal in itself.
11. **When an ending appears, grab it.**
12. **Bonus: Sam Altman on PG** — Altman says he never got formal lessons, just absorbed PG's style by reading his essays: "clarity, precision, density... no posturing... he doesn't waste your time. Nothing feels fake."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Am I judging my own drafts by "batting average" (every sentence must be good) instead of "slugging percentage" (only what I publish needs to be)?
- What's my equivalent of PG's Robert Morris — the person I talk to who reliably generates new ideas, not just smart takes?
- Do I have a "shower thoughts" habit, or am I only writing about boardroom-safe topics I think I'm supposed to care about?
- Where in my writing am I keeping something "because it cost a lot of effort" rather than because it's actually right?
- Which of my "impressive to have read" influences am I unconsciously imitating instead of the guilty pleasures I actually enjoy rereading?
