## TL;DR
David Perell interviews Harry Dry (Marketing Examples) about how he actually writes copy, using his three-question test — can I visualize it, can I falsify it, can nobody else say it — as the spine. Harry walks through real ads (New Balance, Volvo, Snickers vs. Fuse, Apple's iPod, the Economist, De Beers) to show why concrete, provable, unownable claims stick, then live-narrates rebuilding his own "22,000 hours" and "throw money and pray" ads through dozens of rewrites to show that good copy is arrived at, not written in one pass. The second half covers his process pieces (know who you're talking to, have something to say, say it well), his newsletter habits (dense, conflict-driven, no paragraph over two lines since March 2021), and his argument that AI can't write good copy because it has no taste, conviction, or lived experience to draw on.

## Key ideas

**Harry's three rules for every sentence**
- **Can I visualize it?** Abstract kills recall — in the six-word memory game, listeners only remembered "charging pitbull" and "leg of lamb," not "seamless transition" or "better way." Fix: write the abstract word at the top of a page, keep rewriting until you hit a concrete object (e.g., "regain fitness" → "getting off the couch" → "running" → "5K" → *Couch to 5K*, the most-downloaded fitness program ever, made by a Boston TV producer, not Nike).
- **Can I falsify it?** A sentence that's provably true or false puts the writer "on the chopping block" and makes readers sit up — Galileo got house arrest for a falsifiable claim (earth orbits the sun), not a vague one. The test: "imagine you can't talk, you can only point" — describing a friend as "great values" is unfalsifiable; "he reads on the tube" is a fact you can point at.
- **Can nobody else say it?** Forces you to find what's actually differentiated. Volkswagen's "your car has five numbers on the speedometer, Volvo has six" — no competitor can borrow that line, and it's visually verifiable in the car itself.

**Ad breakdowns he walks through**
- New Balance ("worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio") hits all three rules at once — visualizable, true, and unownable by a competitor.
- Snickers vs. Fuse: same product category, same taste, but Snickers ("you're not you when you're hungry") won and Fuse got discontinued in 2006 — copy is the wrapper that makes otherwise-identical products win or lose. Same logic applies to Apple vs. Samsung: "we don't choose the whiskey, we choose the image" (Ogilvy).
- Apple's "1,000 songs in your pocket" works via metonymy — substituting a concrete, visual word (pocket) for the literal one (media player), while PC ads of the era stayed stuck on specs.
- The Economist's "I've never read The Economist. Management trainee aged 42." — built on Neil French's rule that a great ad can work with just one of the four standard elements (header, subheader, picture, logo); using only text is what makes it not look like an ad, so people actually read it.
- De Beers' "How can you make two months' salary last forever?" (Mary Gerety, 1948) created demand for diamond engagement rings from a market that previously used sapphires/rubies/emeralds — now ~85% of engagement rings are diamonds.

**Live rebuild of his "throw money and pray" ad** — starts from a single seed (a 2020 tweet: going from 1% to 2% landing-page conversion is a 100% increase), pulls in a Volkswagen "how to prepare your car for winter" ad purely for its layout, uses Luke Sullivan's tip of drawing a line down a page and writing any two conflicting ideas, iterates ~20–25 times (2 days), sends drafts to friends for feedback, and only adds design/bolding at the very end. Point: the finished ad ("want to grow twice as fast? you have two choices... or learn copywriting") could not have been written directly — it had to be built up piece by piece.

**Three-piece writing process**
1. *Who you're talking to* — Snapchat spent £7M on a Super Bowl ad ("more Snapchat, less social media") aimed at a 39-year-old audience that would find using Snapchat at that age suspicious; contrast with the St Ives café that just wrote "coffee and pastry = £5" for distracted tourists instead of a 17-item menu, and won.
2. *Having something to say* — you need to believe something beyond the sale. Harry started Marketing Examples because he was fed up with theory-heavy marketing writing and wanted examples-only content.
3. *Saying it well* — Hinge's "the dating app designed to be deleted" as a near-perfect example: alliteration, juxtaposition (an app that wants to be deleted), and an implied happy ending, plus it's copy no competitor can reuse.

**Newsletter craft** — writes directly in ConvertKit, not a Google Doc, to see the copy exactly as the reader will. Three deliberate habits: (1) dense — five or six examples per issue; (2) conflict in nearly every example (before/after, here's-how-they-did-it vs here's-how-Loom-did-it — Loom repositioned from "screen recorder" to "async video messaging" and is now worth more than the whole screen-recorder category); (3) no paragraph longer than two lines since March 2021, because short paragraphs are "monkey bars" easy to swing between, force tighter explanation, and give writing room to breathe.

**Simplicity tools** — Kaplan's Law of Words ("any words that aren't working for you are working against you," extended by Harry to ideas: "the strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope" — e.g., a jeans-only brand beats a jeans-and-shirts-and-socks brand). A good paragraph is a burrito that shouldn't fall apart if you remove one sentence, and if you can remove a sentence and the paragraph still works, it didn't belong. He writes multiple parallel versions of the same line/paragraph so his editor (his brother) can compare and mix rather than just say "I like it."

**Facts over adjectives** — "inequality" and "a bit unequal" are opinions; a graph or fact ("Kieran Trippier played at left-back when he's a right-back") is precise and gives writing credibility. Applies to sports commentary, ad copy, and general communication alike.

**Why AI can't write good copy** — it has no taste, conviction, or lived experience. Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce line ("at 60 mph the loudest noise is the electric clock") only worked because someone actually sat in the car; Bukowski wrote *Post Office* after 20 years as a postman, Kerouac wrote *On the Road* in 3 weeks after 7 years living it. AI "gums together long strips of words already set in order by somebody else" — it's predictive, not experiential, and can't say what it believes.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "Write copy that can't be copied." — David Perell, framing the episode
- "If I can't see it, it's not there yet." — Harry, quoting Lisa Cron
- "Can I visualize it? Can I falsify it? Can nobody else say it?" — Harry's three-question test
- "The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope." — Harry, "Harry's Law of Words," extending Kaplan's Law
- "Your standards are your work... Nine out of 10 plus, it goes out. If not, you won't see it." — Harry
- "A good writer arranges words and ideas in a way that haven't been laid out before. Any kind of robot lays them out in a fashion which is quite literally from all the stuff which has been laid out before." — Harry, on AI
- "The difference between 1% and 2% is not just 1%, it's 100%." — the tweet that seeded Harry's "throw money and pray" ad
- "You go you take a walk around the garden. You come back and you're like, I can't take anything away from that." — Harry, describing what finished, good copy feels like

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Which of my own recent sentences fail the "can I visualize it" test — where am I writing "a better way" instead of "a muscular Irishman"?
- Do I have a falsifiable claim I could point to instead of an adjective, in whatever I'm currently trying to persuade someone of?
- Am I writing on the actual medium the reader will see (like Harry writing newsletters directly in ConvertKit), or drafting somewhere disconnected from the real reading experience?
- What do I actually believe that's driving a given piece of writing — or am I writing just to hit a word count / deadline with nothing underneath it?
- Could I build a "conflict" (before/after, X vs Y) into something I'm currently writing flat, the way Harry restructures every newsletter example around a but?
