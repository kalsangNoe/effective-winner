## TL;DR
David Perell interviews "The Cultural Tutor" (Sheen), who went from working at McDonald's in April 2022 to 1.5 million Twitter followers within about 15 months. The conversation argues that writing discipline isn't about routines, productivity hacks, or hour-by-hour structure — it's about surrendering to one's own nature and treating the daily output (a thread, a sentence, a page) as non-negotiable. Sheen credits his growth to writing every day with real financial stakes, reading almost nothing published in the last 50 years to avoid thinking like everyone else, applying "hooky" copywriting techniques borrowed from self-optimization grifters to serious historical/artistic content, and radically narrowing his focus to one platform and one deliverable instead of chasing every opportunity that came his way after going viral.

## Key ideas
- **Disciplined output, undisciplined schedule.** Sheen wakes around 4–5 PM, is "basically nocturnal," smokes heavily, and has no productivity routine — yet has written a thread almost every day for over a year. He argues discipline should be measured day-by-day (did the work get done) not hour-by-hour.
- **Productivity hacks are excuses.** "All this productivity stuff is just getting in between you and the paper." He believes that if you truly need to write, nothing — bad schedule, no gym, no morning ritual — stops you; if you don't write, it's because you don't actually want to, not because you lack time.
- **Write when the idea first strikes you.** He keeps a list of potential thread ideas but rarely uses it — by the time he revisits an idea weeks later he's forgotten why it mattered. The highest-stakes, "now or never" ideas produce his best and fastest writing (his most popular thread, on "the danger of minimalist design," took ~5 minutes to write and hit half a million likes).
- **Heart vs. mind in writing.** The biggest cause of "writer's block" isn't a real block — it's writing what you think you should write instead of what actually grips you. He describes wanting his writing to "roar," pulled from something beastly/instinctive rather than planned.
- **Being a "conduit."** He describes his best writing (and a personal epiphany about faith) as arriving without conscious effort after long periods of reading, reflection, and doing nothing — walking, staring at the floor. He frames creativity as "a pattern of listening rather than doing."
- **Handwriting vs. typing.** Perell's theory: the delete key homogenizes writing because unlike speech or handwriting, typed text can always be erased and softened — this is why people often have more voice speaking than writing.
- **The McDonald's-to-Twitter story.** Rejected by Pizza Hut and Tesco, hired at McDonald's (Apr 2022) because he needed rent money and refused to commit to a law career. Quit McDonald's, got rejected from film school, accepted into the British Army, then went to Tenerife with friend Harry Dry. There he tried "cultural tutoring" (teaching art/history/architecture that schools don't cover), got zero traction, and Harry suggested a Twitter account to drive traffic — named "the cultural tutor" after the tutoring business. Within the two-week trip he had 1,000 followers and abandoned tutoring to focus on Twitter.
- **Brutal early distribution.** Wrote 2–3 threads a day early on; manually followed strangers hoping for follow-backs, messaged everyone who ever liked or retweeted a thread, and cold-messaged bigger accounts for advice. Hit 100,000 followers after 6 weeks.
- **The Perell deal.** At 100,000 followers Sheen planned a paid newsletter to finally monetize; Perell worried this would cap his growth and instead offered to pay him (~£25,000/year, what Sheen said he needed) just to keep writing daily threads and not launch the paywall — funded via Harry Dry's introduction.
- **Narrowing focus.** After going viral, many offers came in (YouTube collabs, etc.); Sheen and Perell treated almost all of them as distractions and stuck to "Twitter thread every day, focus on email, don't do other things" — Sheen credits this discipline with his growth, contrasting it with Perell's own regret about losing his "essence as a creative" by chasing too many projects.
- **Why he avoids anything written in the last 50 years.** (1) Everyone else already reads recent bestsellers, so reading them makes your thinking converge with everyone else's; (2) with too many books to read in a lifetime, "father time" (centuries of survival) is a better quality filter than the New York Times bestseller list — e.g., Boethius's *Consolation of Philosophy* was a bestseller for ~1,200 years.
- **Space bias → time bias.** Historically, new ideas came from geographic travel (a different bookstore, a different continent). Now the internet flattens geography — everything online was made "right now" — so the only remaining frontier for new ideas is time: going backward into old books instead of sideways across the globe.
- **Curator, not originator.** A friend told Sheen he isn't very "original" — he's good at curating, synthesizing, and reshaping existing material. He connects this to Christian writers who aim not at originality but at revealing what has always been true, and argues writers should write for themselves first, not for readers.
- **On hooks and copywriting.** He noticed serious, dry writers on history/art got ignored while self-optimization grifters got huge engagement purely through hooks and headlines. He borrowed their copywriting technique (e.g., Harry Dry's rule "make it falsifiable") and applied it to substantive content — e.g., opening a thread with "[the Great Wave of Kanagawa] is not actually a painting" instead of "here's a deep dive."
- **Editing as a social, verbal process.** Sheen struggles to hand writing over to editors and instead refines ideas by talking them through in conversation — most of his thread ideas originate from something said out loud. He connects this to historical creative circles (Renaissance Florence artists, Byron/Keats/Shelley, the Bloomsbury Group, Tolkien and C.S. Lewis) where proximity to other serious people raised the bar and provided encouragement.

## Quotes worth keeping
- "It's like I know if I don't write this today, I'm never going to write it. So, it's now or never." — Sheen
- "Every action expresses a priority... to say I don't have the time to write means you don't want to write." — Sheen
- "My aim is to write one good sentence every day. If I write one good sentence, it's been a good day." — Sheen
- "I see creativity more and more as a pattern of listening rather than doing... a sense of awareness and connection." — Sheen
- "The delete key is too available on the computer... when I speak there is no delete key. The words have left my mouth." — David Perell
- "If a book has been held in such high regard for over a thousand years, it's not unreasonable to assume that it has something of real value in it." — Sheen
- "Your judgment must always exceed the work" — Sheen, quoting Leonardo da Vinci's advice to young painters
- "Only think about the writing and don't think about anything else." — Sheen, his one piece of advice for writers

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own routine am I substituting "productivity infrastructure" for the actual sitting-down-and-writing?
- Do I have a running list of ideas I never revisit — and would I be better served writing the idea the day it strikes, even imperfectly?
- What would it look like to deliberately read something written more than 50 years ago before my next piece, specifically to avoid converging with what everyone else is reading right now?
- Am I writing for an imagined audience's approval, or for the version of the idea that actually grips me?
- What's my equivalent of Sheen's "circle" — people I talk ideas through with before they're ready to write?
