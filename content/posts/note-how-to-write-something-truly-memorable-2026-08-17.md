## TL;DR
David Grann (Killers of the Flower Moon, The Wager) argues that narrative nonfiction lives or dies on research depth, not prose flair: a 3,000-word chapter can rest on a 200-page outline of absorbed detail. His method is to chase serendipity in archives, visit the physical places he writes about even when none of that trip appears on the page, and then ruthlessly cut 90% of what he learns down to the handful of details that carry meaning. He tells stories chronologically and without hindsight so the reader feels the same suspense the subjects felt, and he insists that a story's "deeper theme" can't be imposed in advance — it has to emerge from the material, sometimes overturning years of prior work.

## Key ideas

**On finding a story — his three-step test**
- **Curiosity first:** does the subject get under your skin? For *The Wager*, it started with a stray interest in mutinies that led him to a digitized 18th-century journal by a 16-year-old midshipman, John Byron, whose phrase "the perfect hurricane" hooked him.
- **Can you actually tell it?** Is there underlying research — logbooks, journals, letters — dense enough to reconstruct scenes? He'll abandon "wonderful stories" he can't document: "those are stories that unfortunately can never be told."
- **Is it about something more?** The deeper theme — for *The Wager*, survivors facing a court-martial where losing meant hanging becomes a parable about "battles over truth" in a post-truth era.

**Research is 90% of the job.** "If you're writing non-fiction... a great book can't exist without" the underlying research. A single 3,000-word chapter can be backed by a 200-page outline covering every physical detail, quote, and diction quirk of a person, plus every account of a scene (down to whether there's a photo of the restaurant).

**Serendipity requires tedium.** Breakthroughs come from "staring at documents weeks and weeks and weeks" — his Killers of the Flower Moon evidence came from unmarked, improperly archived secret grand jury testimony someone had dumped in a public folder decades earlier. "The really only difference between being a writer and a non-writer is the writer is just willing to basically sit for hours and hours... looking through endless boxes."

**Visit the place, even if you cut it from the book.** He sailed to remote Wager Island in Patagonia in wintertime to match the castaways' conditions — and wrote almost nothing about the trip itself. But it corrected his research: he'd logged the temperature as ~32°F and assumed that was manageable, until standing there in constant 25-30mph wind and rain made him realize the crew must have had hypothermia, a detail that reshaped how he wrote their decision-making.

**Selective description beats exhaustive description.** He compares his approach to J.K. Rowling's one-paragraph introduction of Hagrid — a few vivid, well-chosen details ("eyes glinting like black beetles") do more than a full physical inventory. Diction reveals character too: the wager's unlettered gunner wrote "like Hemingway... direct, action, no adjectives, no adverbs," unlike the baroque prose of his upper-class peers.

**Kill the darling, ruthlessly.** He wrote 10,000–20,000 words on how 18th-century ships were built before his wife/first reader Kira reacted with "oh god no, god no" — he cut nearly all of it, keeping only the most astonishing facts (it took 4,000 trees to build one ship). "The reader does not care at all about the backstory of you getting that fact. All they care about is what is on the page."

**Suspense comes from chronology plus removing hindsight.** He tells most stories in order and deliberately writes as the subjects experienced events — not knowing the outcome — because "that is not the way history is lived." He also engineers chapter endings as cliffhangers (example: "The wager was alone at sea left to its own destiny.")

**Prologues are the hardest part.** A prologue has to earn trust and hook fast (roughly 800–1,000 words) while planting the story's stakes and themes without giving away the ending — competing against a phone buzzing with Spotify and other distractions for the reader's attention.

**He inserts himself only when the story needs it.** In *The Lost City of Z* he had to be talked into putting himself into the narrative by a friend who read the draft. In *The Wager*, he initially considered ending the book with his own trip to the island, then cut it entirely: "I don't belong here in this story."

**Truth over invention — the small human detail.** His favorite illustration: on Wager Island, right before the captain abandons the gunner to likely die, the two bitter rivals shake hands. He calls this the kind of detail "the truth could be more surprising" than anything he could invent, and argues the most interesting stories — even huge ones like 9/11 — are best told through ordinary people caught inside them, not the figures at the top.

**Themes emerge from the material, not the other way around.** He never starts a project knowing its theme. Two years into *Killers of the Flower Moon*, he had to scrap his working understanding entirely when he realized the story wasn't about one "evil mastermind" (the FBI's version) but a whole culture of killing — "the book I thought I was writing... just got demolished and I have to write another book."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- Where in my own writing am I keeping a "10,000-word ship-building chapter" — detail I love because I earned it, not because the reader needs it?
- Am I imposing a theme on a piece before the material has actually told me what it's about, the way Grann warns against?
- Do I have a version of "visiting Wager Island" available to me — some low-cost way to physically or experientially get closer to a subject before writing about it, even if the experience never appears on the page?
- When I write about people or events, am I defaulting to hindsight-flattened narration instead of preserving the uncertainty the subjects actually lived through?
- What's my equivalent of the "handshake before abandonment" — a small, true, surprising detail I'm overlooking in favor of the obviously dramatic one?
