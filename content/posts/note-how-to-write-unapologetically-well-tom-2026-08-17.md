## TL;DR
Tom Junod, the Esquire writer behind "The Falling Man" (9/11) and "Can You Say... Hero?" (Fred Rogers), explains that great narrative writing comes from chasing sentences that "make the hair stand up on your arms," refusing to look away from what everyone else avoids, and hunting for contradiction — two truths that don't reconcile — as the engine of a story. He traces his own writing voice back to his father's musical, ellipsis-laced speech and a childhood built on family secrets, arguing that the drive to "say the unsayable" is really the drive to tell the truth he wasn't allowed to tell as a kid. He shares concrete craft tricks (write "this is a story about..." over and over to find the real subject; write a whole draft in second person to cut fear and bloat) and closes with a defense of writing as evidence of a soul — his answer to AI-generated prose.

## Key ideas

- **The guiding test for a sentence: does the hair stand up on your arms?** Junod says this became his organizing principle after writing the opening line of "The Falling Man" ("He departs from this earth like an arrow"). He doesn't know where a sentence is going but trusts he'll recognize it when he finds it.

- **Writing as walking a tightrope between privileged knowledge and discovery.** He has deep knowledge of a subject, but the sentence itself must be written as if he doesn't know what's coming — otherwise it reads as inert reporting rather than live thought.

- **"Say the unsayable."** "The Falling Man" was written because the photo of a 9/11 jumper had become taboo — it ran once and never again. Junod's instinct: "if somebody tells me I can't write about it, I want to write about it... if somebody says I can't say it, I really want to say it." He traces this instinct to growing up in "a family of secrets," where his father was having an affair he wasn't allowed to acknowledge as a child (the hot-fudge-sundae story: waiting outside a locked door, "not supposed to talk about it 100%").

- **Look for contradiction — "two tectonic plates rubbing against each other."** The falling man's apparent calm ("he might very well be flying") against the physics of falling 150 mph to his death; Obama being "a good man" who orders drone strikes. Junod says a story needs "that dimension of ambivalence or contradiction... something's off here that I can't quite name" for him to want to write it.

- **The ellipses in his memoir aren't a Tom Wolfe tic — they're his father's speaking voice.** His book is written "in his language": pause, emphasis, pause, emphasis. His father's aphorisms ("always wear white to the face," "there is nothing like a fresh burn") shaped his ear for rhythm as much as any writer he read.

- **Craft trick #1 — "This is a story about..."** When a piece won't cohere, write a paragraph where every sentence starts "This is a story about..." and repeat until the real subject surfaces. He says it "feels childish... but it works," and it saved multiple award-winning pieces, including "The Rapist Says He's Sorry."

- **Craft trick #2 — write the whole draft in second person, or in all caps, or cursing freely, then edit it back.** He wrote all of Book Three of his memoir in second person before converting it back to first. The point isn't the final form — it's that unusual constraints cut fear and "extraneous stuff" and get you past your point of resistance.

- **Bad writing is boring because it's timid.** His antidote: "be brutally honest and try to say the thing you're not supposed to say, then build the story out of that." He no longer believes you must offend someone to be doing it right, but he still treats the sentence as a challenge to himself before it's a challenge to the reader.

- **On endings:** at Esquire, ending a story on a quote was considered a failure. His rule now: he writes a first sentence that "enables me to go on," and a last sentence that "enables me to stop" — not designed to hook a reader but to satisfy a bar he sets for himself.

- **Fred Rogers taught him goodness is as much a mystery as evil.** Junod had spent his career fascinated by human evil; meeting Rogers (who could read what every person he met needed, "an emotional awareness" bordering on telepathic) let "the spiritual sort of enter" his writing, and he says he couldn't have written "The Falling Man" with the compassion it has without Rogers in his life.

- **Revision philosophy: he's become "a maniacal rewriter, almost to a fault."** His first memoir draft ran 230,000 words and still hadn't introduced most of the main characters — he abandoned it. His editor David Granger's best advice, given on his last Esquire story: "everybody who hears about this story becomes obsessed with it — why is that?" / "I guess because it's a mystery." / "Write that."

- **Writing as thinking, not just recording it.** Junod says he doesn't know how to think without writing: "It's like hammering the pillar into the ground... I'm always looking for the tonic chord" — the single resolving note where a story's discordant threads come together.

- **Closing argument against AI:** the claim that AI can write rests on the premise that there's no soul. Junod's counter is that the "soul" is the part of humanity that bears witness — to suffering, to history, to the truth — and that this bearing-witness (which he traces through modernism from WWI, the Beat writers from WWII, DeLillo from Vietnam) may be "the only thing humanity has ever really done right." Writing seriously from the heart is doing that work; a machine can't.

## Quotes worth keeping

- "If you can keep on writing sentences that make the hair stand up on your arms, then you got this."
- "If somebody tells me I can't write about it I want to write about it, and if somebody says I can't say it, I really want to say it."
- "The urge to turn away is taken for granted. It's never mentioned... looking, looking, looking, and more looking, and not turning away."
- "Okay, I admit it. I wanted to skewer him." — on the opening line he still calls "confrontational of myself and confrontational of the guy."
- "I try to do the things that enable me to tell the truth. That's really what I'm looking to do."
- "It's like when I think of writing that sucks, the two words that come to mind are boring and timid. And it's boring because it's timid."
- "The idea that AI can write is sort of founded on the notion that there is no soul... the soul is the part of us that is willing to, and in fact needs to, bear witness to the truth."

## Open questions / reflection prompts
- What is the thing I've been told (by others or by myself) that I'm "not supposed to say" in my own writing — and what would it cost to say it anyway?
- Where in my own drafts is the "good stuff" getting left out, the way Junod's Walmart story and his 230,000-word memoir draft both buried their real subject?
- Would the "this is a story about..." repetition exercise surface a sharper thesis in something I'm currently stuck on?
- Do my sentences read like I already know the ending, or like I'm discovering it — and does that show up as flatness on the page?
- What's my equivalent of Junod's father's ellipses — an inherited voice or rhythm I could lean into instead of sanding away?
